# Chapter 6: Wierzbów

The Mission on the rue Saint-Honoré had a chapel that had been built in 1730 for the use of a convent of nuns who had been turned out of it in 1905, and it had been repainted twice since then, and the paint was the colour of weak tea, and there was a smell in it of cold stone and wet wool and candle wax that had been burned and re-burned for two centuries; and on the twenty-seventh of December 1943 Krystyna Stankiewiczówna sat in the fourth row on the right with her hat on and her hands in her lap, and said the list.

She said it the way she had said it since the winter of 1939, in the order in which the names had come to her, which was not an order that meant anything: Zofia; Jerzy; Wiktor; Aunt Jadwiga; Maria Brandt; Albrecht Brandt; the woman in the flat above who had had a canary; the girl from the Institute who had been called Wanda and who had had the first bicycle in the school; and, since the second of December, a girl of sixteen in a grey skirt with a cardigan, whose name she did not know and never would, who had come down the steps of a building in the rue de la Pompe with her hair down and her mother's fur coming off one shoulder.

No verbs. That was the method, and she had arrived at it herself, in a cold room in Warsaw in the first winter, and she had kept it, and it had never once failed her: if you say the names without verbs, they stay names. The moment you put a verb in, you have decided something about them, and the deciding is a lie, because you do not know. So: Zofia. Jerzy. Wiktor. She said them into the smell of wet wool and candle wax, without a verb, three times, and then she said the last one, the one she had added on Christmas Day: *Phillip*.

The priest had said to say the name out loud once. She had chosen to say it in a list, in a church, among the dead, which was the Catholic way of doing it and also, she recognised, the coward's way, since a list is a place where a name can hide.

Afterwards she stayed on the stone bench at the back and watched two old women and a boy light candles and the boy's mother hold his wrist to steady it, and she thought about the fact that she had, on the twenty-fourth, twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth of December, gone nowhere and done nothing, and had eaten Christmas dinner at the villa on the avenue Henri-Martin at a table with the Colonel, two of his officers, and a French prefect who had talked for an hour and a half about the quality of the soil in the Beauce, and had been, for those three days, the best-liked woman in Paris: had sung, had danced, had made the prefect laugh, had held the elbow of a Luftwaffe major while he told her about his mother, and had told the priest a lie on Thursday at the drop and given him a memorandum with one line missing from it.

One line. That was the whole of it, and it was small, and she had done it with a steady hand: the line about a captain of the intelligence section, seconded from the east, who had been at the Majestic on the night of the twenty-third, and who was the son of the engineer who had built the tram rails in Warsaw, and who had said her name. It was the kind of line that a woman can justify six ways, and she had justified it six ways, and she had not slept.

***

The estate at Wierzbów was thirty-one kilometres from Warsaw, on the flat land, in a country of willows and sugar beet and bad roads, and it had belonged to the Stankiewicz family since 1796, when a great-grandfather had bought it with money made in the salt trade at Wieliczka and had put up a house with a portico and four columns and twelve windows across the front; and by 1938 it had a roof that leaked over the north wing, a mortgage of four hundred and ten thousand zlotys held by a bank in Poznań, and a man called Antoni who kept the boilers and the keys and the three remaining horses, and who was sixty-eight years old and had known Krystyna's grandmother, and who kept the accounts in a hand that had not changed since 1904.

The reason the German engineer's family was at Wierzbów in September 1938 was the mortgage.

That is the whole explanation and it has no romance in it at all. Albrecht Brandt was the technical director of Lilpop, Rau i Loewenstein in Warsaw — the great ironworks on the Burakowska, where they made railway axles and diesel engines and had begun, that year, to make the fire-control equipment for the coastal batteries under a licence from a German firm — and he was also, since 1935, a member of the board of a Poznań bank, and the Poznań bank was the holder of the Stankiewicz mortgage; and the mortgage had been renewed in 1937 with a personal surety from Jerzy Stankiewicz, who was Krystyna's father and who had been, all his life, a man of the salon and the hunting field and the table and not of the ledger.

Her father had invited the engineer to the shoot because the engineer could save him, and he had invited him in the week in September when the partridges were good and the leaves had begun to go, and he had invited him with his wife and his son, and he had put them in the north wing, under the leaking roof, because the north wing had been done up in 1936 with money that had been about to become the bank's.

Maria Brandt was a Polish woman from a family of Warsaw doctors, and had married a German in 1911 against the wishes of her mother, and had lived in Warsaw since; and she spoke Polish to her husband when she was tired and German when she was angry, and she had a small room in the house on Hoża Street with photographs and a piano and a fern, and she had been, in the year 1938, a woman of fifty-three with a bad heart who had made her peace with everything except two things: that her son had been educated in Dresden and that he had, in the spring of that year, joined a firm of attorneys in Berlin for a year's training because his father had arranged it.

And Phillip Brandt, who was twenty-one in September 1938, was a tall, thin, untidy young man with his mother's eyes and his father's hands, who had read everything and retained about a third of it, who could quote a Polish poem and then spoil it by explaining the metre, who had been considered by the examining board at Dresden to have a good mind and no aptitude, and who had told his father at Christmas 1937 that he intended to be an engineer "but not yet," which was the sentence the two of them had been arguing about ever since.

He had come to Wierzbów with a suitcase containing four books, one of which was a Polish translation of an English poet, and a folder of the translator's correspondence, because he had written to the translator from Berlin in July with eleven objections and had received a reply in August; and on the second evening of the shoot, after dinner, in the library, with the deal table covered in the estate's papers and the whole house's worth of cigarette smoke, he had been discovered by Krystyna Stankiewiczówna standing at the shelf with the folder open, saying out loud, to nobody: "It is not that he has no ear. It is that he has an ear for the wrong language."

She was eighteen, and would be nineteen in November. She was in a borrowed coat because her own had been sent to Warsaw to be re-lined and had not come back, and she had spent the day in the field with the beaters' fires and had shot nothing and had been perfectly happy, and she had come into the library to find a book, and there was a young man she had been introduced to at luncheon talking to a shelf, and she stood in the doorway and laughed.

He turned round, without embarrassment, in the manner of a person who had already, at the age of twenty-one, decided that being overheard was a thing that happened.
"Not the poet," he said. "The translator. He has put an English rain into Polish with Polish words for rain, and they are not the same rain. He would have done better to write it as a Polish rain and be accused of translation. That is my objection number four and I have sent it to a man in Kraków who will not answer me."

"He will answer you," said Krystyna.

"Why?"

"Because he is Polish and you are rude, and the two of them together mean he will spend an afternoon on it."

And Phillip Brandt had looked at her with an expression she had never afterwards been able to describe except as the face of a person who has been given a present by a stranger, and had said, "Yes. That is exactly what will happen," and had cleared the papers off the second chair with one sweep of his arm in a way that told her the whole of his education and half of his father.

She sat down. They talked until one in the morning, and she found him funny, and she never forgave herself for it, and she never told anybody about it either, because the fact that it had been the talking that did it — not the face, not the hands, not the way he stood in a doorway — was a fact about the girl she had been that she did not want to have in the file.

***

Five scenes, and none of them a declaration.

**One.** The horse she could not ride. There was a bay gelding in the stable called Kajtek, and he had a habit of crossing his jaw against the bit and taking the bit and going sideways with his head down, and on the third afternoon she was thrown from him into a beet field, in front of the whole shoot, which is the exact sort of thing that happens to a person at eighteen. She came up out of the beet with earth in her collar and her hat gone and her shoulder stiff, and they caught the horse four hundred metres away, and it was Phillip who caught him, and who then did not hand her the reins; he took the girth strap in his hand, and ran his thumb along the buckle, and looked at it, and moved it two holes, and said, "The saddle was on the wrong rib. Somebody has been putting it on the wrong rib for years, and the horse has been telling you so."

She rode Kajtek that evening for an hour and a half with the saddle in the right place, and the horse went the way she had always known he could go, and something in her, which had been told all her life that horses were a matter of nerve, adjusted by two holes and a thumb and a boy who looked at the tack.

**Two.** The well. It was on the south side of the house beyond the orchard, under a lime tree, with a wooden cover and a windlass and a rope renewed the year before, and the shaft went down eleven metres, and at eleven metres there was a narrowing where the brick had been relaid in 1918, and the bucket stuck on the narrowing, always, unless you let it down twice with a slack turn on the second, which was a thing her brother had shown her in 1933 and which nobody else in the house knew.

On the fourth day, in the late afternoon, she took Phillip to fetch water because the kitchen had run out and the girl was busy. She let the bucket down, and it stuck, and she let it down again with the slack turn, and it filled, and she brought it up with two hands on the windlass and the whole of her back, and set it on the stone, and drank out of the dipper and gave it to him; and he drank, and wiped his mouth with his wrist, and said, "You knew it would stick."

"There is a narrowing at eleven metres."

"How do you know there is a narrowing at eleven metres?"

"Because I put a stone in it in 1934 and counted until I heard the bottom, and then I climbed down to twelve on the rope, with Antoni swearing at me from up here, and looked at it." She said it as a fact about a well. She did not say the other reason, which was that she had never told anyone in her life about the narrowing, including her brother, and that she had told him in the fourth day of knowing him, and that she had done it on purpose, in the same minute in which she had decided it, and that this was the way in which she, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, went about the business of falling in love: by handing over a fact that had no value to anybody except as a small piece of her.

**Three.** The lanterns. Her mother had a gramophone and fourteen records, and on the last Saturday of September, because there were guests, there was supper on the terrace under lanterns that had been in the family since the days of the salt money, and after supper somebody wound the gramophone and they played the records one after another, and there was a tango, and the whole party — the engineer, the mother, two neighbours from Radziejowice, an uncle, the priest from the parish and his sister — watched a girl of eighteen and a boy of twenty-one dance it on a terrace in Mazovia, in the September of 1938, under lanterns, to a record with a scratch in it; and the boy could not dance the tango and made no attempt to conceal it, and the girl taught him the four steps in the middle of the music, correcting him with her hand on his upper arm, which is a gesture that everybody in the party understood and only one of them objected to.

The engineer did not object. That was the thing that Krystyna remembered about it, at twenty-four, on the bench at the back of a chapel in Paris: Albrecht Brandt sat in a wicker chair with his coffee and his cigar, a heavy, quiet man of fifty-six with a gold watch chain, and watched his son be taught a tango by a Polish landowner's daughter, and said nothing at all, and looked — twice, in the course of the evening, and she had seen both times, because at eighteen a person sees everything and understands nothing — looked along the terrace at the mortgage papers in his portmanteau on the table in the library.

**Four.** The birch wood, and the wrist. That has been told once already, in a bedroom in Paris, in four sentences, by a woman doing arithmetic at four o'clock in the morning; and it is not going to be told again here, except for the part that matters, which is that when she let go of his wrist and he got up off his knees in the leaves with his face entirely white and his breath whistling, he laughed — laughed first, before he was able to speak, before he got his wind back — and said, "Where did you learn that?"

"My father's riding master."

"Then your father's riding master has a great deal to answer for."

And she had stood there with her back against the birch tree, and had understood that she had just shown this boy the one thing about herself that she had never shown anybody, which was not the wrist and the thumb but the fact that she had learned it, that she could do it, and that she would; and instead of being frightened, he had laughed, and gone on laughing for the rest of the walk down to the horses, and made her do it to him again in the field behind the stable in the dark the following night, five times, so that he could work out how it was done, and had then spent three weeks designing, in a notebook, a modification of the grip in which the wrist is not exposed, which he showed to nobody but her and which was, though he did not know it, the first intelligent thing the German army ever did about the Polish question.

**Five.** The last afternoon. It was the second of October 1938, and the partridges were finished and the leaves were down, and the engineer's family was to leave the next morning for Warsaw; and the two of them walked the boundary path along the ditch at the end of the birch wood, and there was a long, honest, entirely unbearable conversation which neither of them was willing to end, and in the course of it the following four things were said: that she would be at the flat on Mokotowska in the winter and he would be at his father's house on Hoża Street; that his mother was Polish and his father was not, which he said in exactly that order; that she intended to marry a man who would be in Warsaw and not in Berlin; and that he had no intention of being an engineer "yet."

And then neither of them said anything else for four hundred metres, and at the end of the four hundred metres he stopped and took hold of her hand, without any of the nonsense of the wood, and held it for the length of a long breath, and said: "I will write to you."

"You will not. You will write four letters, and I will answer three, and then it will be the summer, and in the summer you will come to the shoot and it will be bad, and then it will be the end of it, and neither of us will have said anything." She heard her own voice say it, in the ditch, under the willows, at eighteen, and heard him take it; and she saw that he had gone still, in the way a boy goes still when he has been handed the whole of his future in one sentence by a girl who is four years younger and who has grown up in a house where the accounts are not told to the children.

And he said, "Then I will not write to you."

And he did not. It was she who wrote first, which was the last thing her pride had left, and she did it in August 1939, four days after the announcement of the Soviet pact, from a hotel in Krynica where she was taking a cure with her mother, and the letter was nine lines long and contained no verbs in the important places, and she heard nothing back for eleven days, and the answer that came, on the thirtieth of August, was one line: *The shoot can wait a year. — P.*

It reached the flat on Mokotowska Street on the last morning of the peace, while her father was at the telephone to the bank and her mother was at the window with the blackout paper in her hands, and she put it in the pocket of the coat she was going to take to the cellar, and she had it still.

***

The war came into the flat on Mokotowska Street on the first of September 1939 at twenty minutes to six in the morning, by radio, while Antoni was still at the kitchen table with a glass of tea.

Antoni Solecki had come up from Wierzbów on the thirty-first of August with the last of the things that were worth fetching: two pairs of candlesticks, the box of papers, the portrait of Krystyna's grandmother in its frame, and a jar of honey wrapped in a shirt, and he had come on the cart as far as Grodzisk and then on the electric railway with the jar on his knees, and he was sixty-nine and had a bad hip and had said, when the family told him he should have stayed at the estate, that somebody would have to look after the papers when the bank took the house, and that he had known the papers since 1904.

The estate went to the bank in the third week of August 1939. It is important to state that plainly, because the last autumn at Wierzbów had been the autumn of 1938 and there was no other one: the mortgage, the beet that had failed in 1937 and again in 1938, the syndicate from Poznań that had bought the beet land in 1932 and put their dogs on it, the four hundred and ten thousand zlotys, the sale agreed in Warsaw on the eleventh of August with a lawyer who had come to the flat in a hat, the notice to quit for the middle of October; and then the general mobilisation, and nobody signing anything at all, and the whole transaction sitting in a folder on a table in a country that was about to stop existing at twenty minutes to six in the morning on a Friday.

Tadeusz had gone on the twenty-eighth of August with a bag and a railway warrant and his mother's scapular in his pocket, in the direction of the east, to a regiment that had been moved three times in five days.

On the first of September the bombs came down on the Ochota and on the aerodrome at Pole Mokotowskie and on the working-class streets on the other side of the tracks, and the flat on Mokotowska shook and the pianino sounded a chord of its own in the corner, and Hanna, who was thirteen, went and stood in the corridor with her back against the wall in the place where the load-bearing wall was, because the father had said on the first morning that this was the safest place in the building and had said it with a calm that he had then not been able to keep for more than two days.

On the third of September there was an announcement on the radio at midday that England was at war and France would be by the evening, and the whole courtyard came out into the yard to hear it through the open windows of the ground floor, and the caretaker's wife cried, and a man on the third floor said that the cavalry would be in Berlin by the end of the month, and Hanna counted, out loud, the number of aeroplanes she had heard since breakfast and got to nine.

And at twenty past nine that night, in the rain, a car stopped at the corner of Mokotowska and Wilcza, and Phillip Brandt came across the road to the gate of the building with the collar of his coat up and a white armband on his sleeve.

***

There were four of them in the hall: her father in his study jacket with the lamp in his hand, her mother on the stairs with a candle, Hanna behind the door of the kitchen, and Krystyna standing at the end of the corridor by the brass umbrella stand, which was the only piece of the palace's furniture that had come with them, and which is the only reason she had something to put her hand on.

Antoni had let him in, because Antoni had been at Wierzbów in September 1938 and knew exactly whose son it was; and Antoni then went back into the kitchen and stood there with the door open, which was his way of not being absent.

Phillip stood in the hall without taking off his hat.

"Herr Stankiewicz." He said it in Polish, and then he went on in Polish, and the Polish was correct and his accent in it was Warsaw, and every word of it was being said by a man in a coat with a white armband on the sleeve. "I have a car and a pass and I am leaving the city at four o'clock this morning with my mother. My father is in Berlin since the twenty-sixth. There is room in the car for five people and their cases and the driver is my father's driver, and he will be paid in marks, and there is a route to the south that is open as far as Grójec tonight and will not be open tomorrow afternoon. I came to ask you to come with us. All of you. Tonight."

Nobody moved.

"I can do this because of the papers," Phillip said. "The papers are German papers, and they say the car belongs to a firm that the German army—"

And there the sentence went into German. Not one word of it, not a phrase: the whole rest of the sentence, the important part of it, the part about what the papers were and what the car was for and who the car obeyed, came out of him in German, fluently, the German of a boy who had been educated in Dresden, and it hung in the hall of the flat on Mokotowska Street at half past nine in the evening of the third of September 1939 like a smell that could not be got rid of.

He stopped. He heard himself stop. She saw him hear it.

Her father lowered the lamp a little and looked at the young man in the hall for a long moment.

"Niemiec," Jerzy Stankiewicz said.

He said it quietly, without any passion at all, the way a man reads out the headword on a page; and then he said two more sentences, and the family in the flat heard the whole of it, and Hanna never forgot a word of it and neither did her sister.

"You have come to my house in a German coat, at half past nine at night, in the third day of the bombing of my city, to offer to take my wife and my daughters out through the German lines in a German car. And you have explained it to me in German. I am a teacher of Latin and I am old, and I do not know what will happen to my country in the next three weeks, and I do not know whether I will ever see my son again; but I know what happens to a man who gets into that car. He spends the rest of his life explaining that he was right." He raised the lamp slightly. "You were in the library at Wierzbów and you corrected a translation. That was a Polish boy. Go home and be that, and do not come here again with your mother's face and your father's papers."

The word her father used was *Niemiec*, and he used it once, and it was true on the third of September 1939, and it was the truest thing anybody said in that hall that night, and everybody standing in it, including the young man in the German coat, knew it.

Phillip looked at Krystyna, once, across the whole length of the corridor, past her father's shoulder and the lamp; and she looked at him; and then he bowed — actually bowed, from the waist, to Jerzy Stankiewicz, in a Polish hall in 1939, which was either the last of his Polishness or the first of his Germanness, and she has never been able to decide which — and went out, and Antoni closed the door and shot the bolt.

Her mother said, from the stairs: "Jerzy."

"There is no argument in this house that begins with the word 'but,'" her father said. "Not tonight."

Krystyna went into the room at the front, which was her parents' room, and stood at the window over the courtyard, and looked down through the rain on the glass with both hands flat on the sill, and Phillip Brandt was standing on the pavement under the lamp with his hat in his hand and his face up, in the wet, looking at the window; and there is no clock in that street and no record of how long it was, except that a woman standing at a window one storey above a man on a pavement in the rain has four or five minutes in which to go down the stairs, and that the stairwell of that building was sixty-one steps, and that she counted them without moving, and that at the end of it he put his hat on and got into the car and the car went away down Mokotowska Street towards the tram lines.

Her father came in behind her at some point and stood in the doorway.

"Not that window," he said. "You will not stand at that window again."

He never explained why, and she never asked, and it is the only rule he ever made that she obeyed for the rest of her life.

***

She left Warsaw on the morning of the fourth of September 1939, which was the fourth day of the bombing, with a small suitcase and the coat her mother took off her back at the platform and put round her shoulders and buttoned at the throat with her own hands, in the crowd of the Eastern station, where a woman was selling hot water and there was no sugar, and where the family's decision had been made the night before at the kitchen table in the way these decisions were made: the mother was ill and could not be moved an hour further than necessary, the son was with the regiment, the sister was thirteen and would stay with the parents, and the daughter was nineteen, and would be twenty in November, and was therefore the one who could travel, and there was a relation of the family's late aunt at a place south of Radom with a house and a room, and the road was still open.

The train was stopped twice by the aeroplanes and once by a rumour, and it went a hundred kilometres in nineteen hours, and the last view she had of Warsaw was from the carriage window with her forehead against the glass: not heroic, not a burning city in the illustrated sense, but a city with smoke standing up on it in three separate columns in a grey morning, and a balloon barrage hanging over the river, and a man on the embankment at the water's edge with his coat off, washing.

The city held out for another twenty-five days. It surrendered on the twenty-eighth of September, and she heard it in a kitchen in the south of Poland on a wireless set with a broken aerial, and the first thing she thought about was the window over the courtyard.

***

She knows three things about the letter, and she has known them since the winter of 1939, from a letter of her mother's that came to the south in November by way of a nun, and that had the price of coal in it, and her aunt's rheumatism, and the news that the maid Józefa had been dismissed in October because there was no money for a maid, and one sentence in the middle, dropped in without emphasis, the way her mother dropped the things that mattered:

*Your father says the German boy came to the gate in the last week, and that he has written to you, and that the letter went off in Józefa's prayer book when she left, and it is better so, Krysia, and do not write about it.*

Three facts, then: that he wrote it; that it went away in a maid's prayer book to a street nobody in the family knows; and that it never came, and never will come, and that her father knew about it and chose not to tell her for what he believed were good reasons, and was right.

She has never mentioned the letter to a living person. She has spent four years deciding that it was a note about a road out of town, because that is what he was, and then, on other days, that it was four lines long and said nothing at all, and then, on the worst days, that it was the only sentence the whole thing ever came to, and that she had left it in a prayer book on a shelf in Warsaw with a maid who was dismissed in October 1939 for want of money.

***

On the twenty-seventh of December, at six in the evening, she walked the length of the boulevard Raspail in the rain with a hat she did not need and a handbag with nothing in it, and stood for eleven minutes on the pavement opposite the Hôtel Lutetia.

There were two lorries under the portico and a sentry under the awning and the windows of the ground floor lit with the hard, brown, varnished light of every German office in Europe, and through the third window from the left, behind a palm in a green glazed pot, a man in field grey stood at a table with his hand flat on a piece of paper; and she was too far away to see his face and did not need to, because she had spent four days understanding that the only person in Paris who could read her was in that building, at a desk, with a file.

She did not go in. There was no arrangement to go in, no pretext, no reason that would survive being written down in a day book: the mistress of an Oberst of the Militärbefehlshaber does not walk into the Abwehr's Paris headquarters eight days after Christmas to see a Hauptmann of the intelligence section about a well in Mazovia. She stood on the pavement with the rain coming off the brim of her hat, and she looked at the lit windows, and she counted them, and then she walked away east along the boulevard to the Mission, and sat in the fourth row in the smell of cold stone and wet wool, and did not pray, and waited for Wiktor.

He was in the doorway of the building on the rue Saint-Honoré at half past seven, with his stick and his collar up and his spectacles fogged, and they walked for four minutes along the empty street in the dark without speaking, because that was the rule for the first four minutes.

"The Colonel is posted in February," Wiktor said. "East of the Vistula. It is in the paper: the Militärbefehlshaber is stripping the Paris staff for the new arrangements in the Government General, and your Oberst is on the list because he speaks Russian and because he has a reputation for moving a supply column, and the house that is to be given him is a white villa on the Frascati Street, with a garden and three trees, requisitioned in 1940 from a family called Zaleski."

"The Frascati," she said, and said nothing else for a moment, because the Frascati was four hundred metres from the palace on the Nowy Świat where she had been born, and because she knew the way that pavement sounded under a boot in the cold.

And Wiktor looked at her sideways through the fogged glass and said: "You have said the name of the street twice, in a voice that knows how the pavement sounds. I did not tell you how the pavement sounds, my dear colleague, and I have been keeping that in the same drawer as the other facts about you."

"And?"

"And the Abwehr is finished. The Admiral is out, and his people are being taken over by men who do not read Polish, and there is a young officer of the Paris station whose file has been opened in Berlin, and whose career will be short if he does not find something for them in the next six months." He turned his stick a quarter-turn on the pavement. "That is the good news, and you may do what you like with it. The other news is that your father is ill in Warsaw — a lung, and a bad winter, and your sister is seventeen and taking the papers now, which you did not know and which is a fact you will not be told again. And that is the news you can do nothing about, and the reason you are going to go with the Oberst to Warsaw in a fur coat, and write to me once a month, and remember the rules. Good night, Krystyna."

"Wiktor."

"Go home," he said, and went off up the street with the stick, and it was four months until the sixteenth of February, and she walked back to the avenue Henri-Martin in the rain, past the dark windows of the shops on the rue de Rivoli, and did not think about any of it, and got into the bed of a German colonel at eleven o'clock and lay there with her eyes open in the dark, and listened to the sentry on the gravel, and it was eleven minutes, and then eleven minutes again.

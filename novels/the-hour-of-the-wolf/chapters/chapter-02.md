# Chapter 2: Salt and Silk

Boin, Robes et Manteaux had three windows on the rue de Miromesnil and a bell over the door that rang in the key of F, and had rung in the key of F since 1926, when Madame Odile had bought the shop with the dowry of a marriage that had lasted four years and a settlement that had lasted longer.

"A little to the left," Odile said. "You are not a door. Stand like a woman."

"I am standing like a woman."

"You are standing like a soldier. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and it costs you nothing." Odile had a mouth full of pins, which did not affect her diction, a trick she had learned in a workshop at fourteen and had never explained to anybody. "Left. Now breathe in and hold it, and do not tell me you cannot breathe, I have seen you breathe at the Ritz with that German's hand on your back, and that requires more breath than a fitting."

There was one thing on her that no German had ever looked at, and it hung on a cord of black silk inside her dress and had hung there since the eighteenth of September 1939: a silver signet with a flat face, and on the face the head of a greyhound above three willow leaves, which is the crest of the Stankiewicz family of Mazovia. It had been her father's. He had given it to her in the hall of the flat in Warsaw on the morning the German removals men carried the sofa out through the door and asked the family, in the manner of men doing a job, whether the piano was to go in the first lorry or the second.

Odile saw the cord at her throat on the day of the fourth fitting of that winter, when the dress came off her shoulders, and asked what it was, and Krystyna said, "My mother's."

Odile looked at it for about four seconds and then said, in the voice she used for dress alterations, which never changed: "If they find it on you, they will have your family name inside an hour, and if they have your name they will have the flat in Warsaw, and if they have the flat they will have your father and your sister. A woman in this trade who carries her own name round her neck in an occupied city is a woman who has decided to be found." And then she pinned the shoulder and said nothing else about it for the rest of the war.

The ring stayed where it was. It stayed through the fifth fitting and the sixth; through January and the arrests and the bicycle in the passage on the rue de Saint-Honoré; through a bedroom in the avenue Henri-Martin and a dance in the ballroom of the Majestic and eleven hundred questions in the office of an Oberst who would have given a year of his pay for the name of the family that crest belonged to; and the reason it stayed was not sentiment, and it was not defiance, and it was the reason that had been given to her in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement in the autumn of 1942 in a sentence she had written down in her head and had never once been able to obey: *you will find that there is one thing of your own that you will not be able to leave behind, and I am telling you now, before you find it, that it will kill you.*

The atelier was cold, because coal went to the Germans and to the concierge and to the shop on the corner that had a cousin in the prefecture, and so the room was warmed by an iron stove that Odile fed with the wood of dismantled hat stands and, in the last resort, with an enormous and unsellable mahogany dresser that had been standing in the courtyard since August. The smell of the place was chalk and sizing and scorched silk and the ghost of a hundred perfumes that had been sent to be copied: Guerlain, Chanel, Lelong, L'Heure Bleue by the gallon, worn by women who came in fur in the morning and stood on the same four boards as German officers' mistresses and Jewish dressmakers and the wives of men who were in prison at Fresnes, all of them equal in the only way this particular room recognised, which was that Odile made them all stand still.

Krystyna stood still in the grey dress that was being made for the Christmas supper at the Majestic. It was the fourth dress of the winter and it would cost more than the flat on the rue Saint-Honoré in which she had first been taught to hold a pen without pressure. Odile had a theory about the dress — she always had a theory — and the theory was that a mistress who was seen in the same dress twice was a mistress with a limited budget, and a mistress with a limited budget was a mistress who might be paid for, and a mistress who might be paid for was a woman who would be asked, eventually, what she had done before the war in Vienna.

"Pin the left," Krystyna said. "The left is hanging."

"The left is perfect. You have a shoulder like a farm girl on that side. It comes from carrying things." Odile took the pins out of her mouth and looked up at her, and there it was: the small, level look that the woman used twice an hour on everyone and which was the reason the network had put an atelier at the centre of its Paris survival. "How is your mother?"

"Dead."

"I know she is dead," Odile said. "I am asking whether she is dead this week."

"This week she is dead."

"Good. Then stand still."

Two women in the back room, whose voices came through the curtain as rustling and French, were working on the evening bodice. The curtain was blue cotton and had been bought in 1937 and bleached by the sun on one side, and behind it, in a space of perhaps eight feet by ten, Madame Odile Boin kept, in a drawer under a stack of the fashion magazines of a better year, the tools of a trade she had learned at fifty from a Pole who came in one day pretending to be a client looking for mourning clothes for a cousin and who had stayed eleven hours and left at two in the morning with a rouble watch and a promise.

There was a press. There was a tin of paper of four different weights, which Odile could distinguish by touch the way another woman can distinguish children. There was a physician's stamp of the department of the Seine, four rubber stamps of the German military administration in various states of wear, a bottle of ink that was not the ink sold in the shops, and an iron, because sometimes a document needs a crease and creases do not lie.

None of it was in the shop when she had visitors, which meant that none of it was there for about half the hours of the day.

"You are to have a new card," Odile said, in the voice she used for dress alterations, which never changed. "The one you have is August's. There is a woman at the prefecture who has started to write letters, and letters keep."

"Which woman?"

"The one whose machine you are wearing. Do not concern yourself." Odile marked a seam with chalk. "Also I have a fourth stamp. The one with the mark of the Ortskommandantur of the fifteenth of June. It is very good. It is from a German's desk. It cost a great deal of money and no one's life, which is the best one can say for it."

"Whose life did the others cost?"

"Ah," said Odile, and was silent for the length of one pin.

The bell in F rang at half past ten, and into the atelier with a draught of the street came a man in a dark green overcoat with a stick, fifty-odd, grey at the moustache, with spectacles repaired at the bridge with black thread, and a folded newspaper under the arm, which he put on the cutting table as though the table belonged to him, which in a sense it did, because he had found it.

"Madame," he said to Odile.

"Monsieur," Odile said. "You are early. You are never early."

"I am becoming old, and one of the symptoms is that one is early." Wiktor Janowski took off his hat and his spectacles and wiped them on his handkerchief with the unvarying three passes, and looked at Krystyna the way a man looks at a horse he has bought and not yet paid for. "My dear colleague. You are looking well."

"I am looking well," she said. "It is the work."

"Come," he said, and went behind the blue curtain, and she followed him, because the rules said that the fitting was for Odile and the conversation was for the curtain, and the rules were not suggestions.

There was one chair. He sat in it and hung the stick on the back and she stood, because she was in a dress that was pinned, and because he never, in three years, offered a woman his chair.

"Report," he said.

She reported. She gave him the sheet of blotting paper, right to left and left to right, with the dates and the sector numbers and the sentence about the labour requisitions, and she gave him the two facts about the household that she judged to be facts of trade rather than facts of gossip: that the Colonel had, since October, kept a locked drawer in the desk as well as the safe, and that his orderly's day book had a page for the nights she was called to the house late, which meant that somebody outside the house had asked for such a page to be kept.

Wiktor took it all in without a single question and then asked four questions, all of which were about numbers, and wrote nothing down, and at the end of it said: "The two westernmost."

"Feldkommandantur."

"Not to the Kreiskommandantur."

"Not to the Kreiskommandantur. Which means the labour is being requisitioned not for the works themselves but for the Todt people, who are outside the military chain, and who are building something the army does not want the district commands to look at."

"You have been thinking."

"I have been lying in a bed at three in the morning listening to a man talk about his brother's timber."

"Then it comes to the same thing, in your case." He took off his spectacles again and looked at the window, which was grey with rain. "It is good. It goes tonight, and it goes the long way. Do you understand what you have brought?"

"Something about a wall."

"Something about a wall," Wiktor said, with the flat distaste he kept for anything that sounded like a phrase. "You have brought the inspection schedule of two of the batteries on the Atlantic coast, and the fact that a certain organisation not under the army's command is requisitioning labour on the western coast through the Feldkommandantur, and that the army's district commands have been told not to interest themselves. In London, there is a man in a basement whose whole job is the coast of France. When he reads what you have brought, he will go upstairs and stand in front of a map for forty minutes. I know this because he is the man I used to be."

"He was taller."

"He was," Wiktor said. "And better dressed, and he had a wife, and he did not talk about walls."

He stood, and took something out of the inside pocket of the green coat, and put it on the cutting table: a woman's compact, engine-turned silver, the kind of thing a woman buys once and keeps for thirty years, with a mirror in the lid and a powder tray inside.

She knew it. He had given it to her in the summer of 1942, in a room in the fifteenth arrondissement, with the shutters closed and a fan worked by hand.

"You will not be taken alive and talking," Wiktor said, in exactly the tone in which he had said it the first time. "This is not courage. This is tradecraft. Repeat it."

"You will not be taken alive and talking."

"Louder."

"You will not be taken alive and talking."

"Good." He picked up the compact and turned it over in his hand and pressed the little catch at the hinge with his thumbnail, and the false floor came up, a quarter of an inch deep, and in it, wrapped in a scrap of tissue, was a single tablet the size of a match head and the colour of chalk. "Do not carry it loose. Do not put it in a glove. Do not sew it into anything. When they search you they will search the seams, and they have found things in worse places than the seams of a woman who has been searched before."

"You have told me this."

"I have told you this twice. When I have told you three times I shall know that I am frightened." He shut the compact and gave it to her, and she put it in the bag that was hanging on the back of the chair. "It is the same tablet as the last one. It is not a game. It is not for the day you are arrested. It is for the third day, in the cellar, when you hear your own voice in the corridor saying things you have not yet decided to say."

"And Odile has one."

"Odile is a dressmaker from the eighth arrondissement and she has refused one for two years, and when they take her they will break her like a plate, and I will read about it in the German papers, and there is nothing at all to be done about it, and I do not wish to discuss it." He put his spectacles on. "Now. The papers."

She took the new Kennkarte out of the bag and looked at it, as she did every month: Marion Helene Herschlag, born in Herschlag, Lower Austria, twenty-nine years old, seat of the Austrian Red Cross's Vienna branch 1937–38, divorced from Oberleutnant Helmut Kurt Gessner, killed on the Eastern Front on 4 January 1942. A genuine Austrian woman had existed, briefly, in a hospital in Vienna, and had died of typhus in 1921 at the age of seven, alone, without a birth certificate that anybody had ever troubled to correct, and her name had cost the network four thousand francs to a clerk in a Kreisgericht in 1941.

"Say who you are," Wiktor said.

"I am Marion Herschlag."

"Where were you born?"

"At Herschlag, in Lower Austria. My father was a notary. My mother played the piano badly and taught me to sing."

"Why are you in Paris?"

"Because my husband was killed at Rostov and I could not stay in Vienna with the memories, and a friend of my mother's had a house on the rue de Rivoli and I came in the spring of 1942 to work as a companion to her, and she died in November."

"Who was the friend?"

"Madame Rosa Berg. She is buried at Père-Lachaise. She died in November."

"And who do you sleep with?"

"I sleep with Oberst Wolfram von Hagen, of the staff of the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich, at 19 avenue Henri-Martin, on Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays, and any other night he telephones."

"Repeat the second half of your account of the labour requisitions."

"Not to the Kreiskommandantur in the case of the two westernmost," she said. "Requests for labour to be submitted directly to the Feldkommandantur."

Wiktor nodded and put his hat on and looked at her for a moment with the expression that she had privately, in 1942, named the Ledger: the look of a man totting up a page he has written himself and found correct.

"You are not to think about the war," he said. "You are to think about the next thing. The war is a hundred and forty people in six rooms doing what I do, and if you begin to think about it you will be slow, and if you are slow you will be dead, and I will have to write a report about it, and my reports about women are always the same: capable, and lost through an error of sentiment."

"Which sentiment?"

"Whatever one it was." He turned for the door and then stopped with his hand on the curtain. "One thing. The Polish desk of the Lutetia has a new man. He has come from Warsaw through the Abwehr's own channel, which means he is either a fool or something we have not seen here, and the people who keep the emigrant lists have been asked to send their files up to the second floor. I have no more than that. Nobody in this city knows him. I do not know him. He does not know you. You will avoid him."

"His name?"

"I have given you everything I have," Wiktor said, "which is that you will avoid him," and went out through the curtain into the sound of the bell in F.

***

She walked up the rue de Miromesnil with the new card in the lining of her bag and the dress in a box tied with string, and it began to rain in the thin, vindictive way that Paris rains in November, and she put up her umbrella, which was a German umbrella from a German shop and had cost more than the shoes of the woman who walked past her on the pavement pushing a pram made of a wooden crate and four bicycle wheels.

That woman looked at her. It lasted a fifth of a second. It was the look that Krystyna received, in this city, perhaps forty times a day, and it did not vary: from the shoes up, in one movement, and then away, with the face closed, and then, sometimes, at the end of the street, a second look back, which was the one that mattered, though not for the reasons the woman thought.

It was not a look about wealth. There were poor Frenchwomen in Paris in 1943, and there were rich Frenchwomen in Paris in 1943, and the rich ones were eating at Maxim's and being photographed in the illustrated weeklies with their hair done by a man who had been released from a camp for the purpose. It was a look about provenance: it said, *that coat came from somewhere that is not a French drawer*, and it was, in its small way, an accusation, and Krystyna had learned to walk through forty of them a day the way one walks through a smoke-filled room.

The woman with the pram did not know, and could not have been expected to know, that the coat had been bought in the rue de la Paix with August's money from the Militärbefehlshaber's entertainment fund; that the shoes had belonged, before the fitting, to a girl of twenty-two from Lille who had been arrested in a bookshop in January and about whom nothing further was known; and that the woman in them, the twenty-fourth of November 1943, was at that moment carrying in her head, along with the dress and the compact and the Austrian papers, the inspection schedule of two batteries of the Atlantic Wall, which she would not be permitted to write down anywhere, and which she would have to keep safe for six more hours.

The pram-woman turned up a side street. Krystyna walked on, and something small and hard and familiar turned over in her stomach, which was not guilt — she had put guilt down somewhere in 1940 and had never been able to find the place again — but a thing for which she had no name, and which she had decided not to investigate because there was no profit in it, and which came back to her at night sometimes in the shape of her mother's coat.

Two years earlier, in Warsaw, she had been a girl who could not walk twenty metres without meeting somebody's eyes. That city had been a lit room. This one was a corridor.

She had once sat on the edge of a bed in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement with a girl from Lille and been asked, as a training exercise, to tell the story of her own life out loud in the third person while the other woman interrupted her.

*She was born in Warsaw in 1919,* she had begun, *the middle child of a family that had money before the war and a name after it.*

*Why did she leave?*

*In September 1939 the city was bombed for three weeks and the family decided that the daughter should be sent south to relations while the son went with his regiment and the mother stayed with the father, and she went with a small case and a coat, on the fourth day of the bombing, from the Eastern station, and the train was stopped twice by the air raid and once by a rumour, and it took nineteen hours to go a hundred kilometres.*

*And who sent her?*

*Her mother. Her mother stood on the platform and put the coat on her and said, do not look back, and then held her husband's arm so that he would not call out, and she did not look back, and she has spent four years not looking back in a great many different places.*

*And after?*

*Hungary. Six months in Budapest, in the flat of a cousin of a cousin, doing a little teaching of French to the children of a woman who had a passport and no scruples. Then Italy for four months, waiting for a passage that was bought twice and never came, and in the spring of 1940 the last of the money and a place in a group of people going to France, where the Polish army was being re-formed, and France was the road to everything else, and the road to France went through Yugoslavia and took nine weeks and cost her the last thing she owned, which was a ring.*

*And in France?*

*The army she had come for had already gone into the line and been broken, and the men she found in the second week of June 1940 were men of a country that no longer existed, sitting in a room in Toulouse with a typewriter and a set of instructions from a government in London, and a captain who looked at her for a long time and then asked her, in Polish, whether she could hold a piano tune, and she said yes, and he said, then we shall find somebody to teach you to sing.*

*And what does she do now?*

*She sings,* Krystyna said, *and she sleeps with a German colonel twice a week, and other things which are of no interest to you.*

The girl from Lille had written something in her notebook and said nothing for a while, and then she had asked the one question the exercise was designed to produce: *which part of that is true?*

*All of it,* Krystyna had said. *That is why I was chosen.*

At half past three she was at the Ritz, where the Germans were, and where the hats were.

***

The tea at the Ritz in the third winter of the occupation was an operation conducted by about forty Frenchwomen of a particular class and by the wives of German officers of a particular rank, and the two kinds of women sat at the same tables and spoke to one another in the manner of people who have agreed to consider a mutual acquaintance dead.

There were eleven Frenchwomen in the room that afternoon and five Germans. There was real cream, which had not been in a Paris shop since 1940, and there were petits fours that had been made by a chef who had been making petits fours in the Place Vendôme since 1921 and who had, in the intervening years, developed a small tremor of the left hand that could be seen by anyone who watched the trays. The fire was lit. The mirrors were polished. In the corner, a Hungarian played the piano, and played it well, and played the sort of thing that is played in hotels, and had been, in 1934, in the front rank of his profession, and knew it.

"Darling," said Céline Marchand, "come here, you have to see what the Krüger woman is wearing. Come here. Stand where I am standing, do you see, in the light, look at her back."

"I can see her back from here."

"You cannot see it from there, from there she looks almost normal. Look at the back. It is a dressmaker's crime. Do not laugh."

"I am not laughing."

"You are laughing inside. You have a laughing face, it is a curse, it is why the men watch you at the door. Have you eaten? No, you have not eaten, you never eat, and I have eaten four of the small cakes and I am going to have to be let out of my corset with a jack." Céline Marchand had been born in Neuilly, had married a man who owned a factory at Asnières that had made agricultural machinery and now made parts for the German army, and had spent the entire occupation being delighted with herself in public, and was, in Krystyna's professional estimate, the single most dangerous Frenchwoman in the eighth arrondissement, because she noticed things about other women's clothes and other women's accents with the accuracy of an intelligence officer and the conscience of a magpie.

"The Krügers are going to Berlin for Christmas," Céline said. "She told me at least four times, in the way that means the opposite, which means he has been recalled, which means something has gone wrong at his office. Robert says the whole administration is going to be rearranged in the spring, he heard it from a man at the Majestic, and that is *very* good for us, because if they rearrange the administration, they will have to buy the whole thing over again, and Robert's contracts all come up for renewal in March." She sipped her tea. "We are going to do extremely well out of this war, darling, and then afterwards we are going to die of shame, and I intend to be in Biarritz when it happens."

"Then you will need a new coat."

"I have already ordered it," said Céline, delighted, "from your woman on the Miromesnil, she is too expensive and too slow and I love her, she refuses to make me anything with a pocket, she says a woman with a pocket stops thinking about her waist. Marion, may I ask you something?"

"You may ask."

"Are you all right?"

Krystyna turned her head and looked at her. Céline was forty, blonde by chemistry, with a small dark root showing that her hairdresser had been unable to obtain something, and her eyes were perfectly kind and perfectly empty, and she was the sort of friend that the operation had been built to produce: a woman who would introduce Marion Herschlag into every drawing room in Paris, who would defend her when she was not there, who would tell her everything she heard, and who would never, not once, ask a question that mattered.

"I am perfectly well."

"Because you look tired, darling. Not tired, that is the wrong word. You look — " and Céline made a gesture with her teacup, hunting for the word, and found it, as she always did — "you look *elsewhere*. Twice this afternoon I have been talking to you and you have been somewhere else. And your German is so good, but when you are tired it goes — here, like this — " and she put the cup down and said, in the voice of a woman quoting an old servant, with an accent laid on like paint on a wall, "'*A bułka, if you please.*'"

The room did not stop. The Hungarian played. The fire went on burning at the Ritz's expense.

Krystyna heard her own voice, twelve days earlier, in a room in the Hôtel Majestic, saying to an orderly with a tray: *a bułka, if you please*, and hearing herself do it, and covering the whole thing with a laugh and a story about a nanny from Lemberg who had taught her to curse and to pray and to ask for bread. It had been nothing. It had lasted four seconds and the whole table had laughed with her, and she had gone home afterwards and stood under the running tap for twenty minutes, and had decided — this is what she had decided, standing there — that it had been nothing.

"You do that," Céline said. "That is a Polish word for a roll. My husband's first wife was Polish, God rest her."

"Your husband's first wife was Belgian."

"She said *pumpernickel*," Céline said, with immense satisfaction, "in a Polish accent, and my mother-in-law went to her grave calling her the Pole. A bułka. And where does a Viennese girl out of a notary's house get a Polish word for a roll?"

"From a nanny."

"From a nanny," Céline said, and burst out laughing, "and from nowhere else, because if it were from a husband I should have had to be jealous, and if it were from a mother I should have had to be interested, and one mustn't be interested, darling, it is the only rule of the period." She took Krystyna's hand and squeezed it, and her fingers were warm, and there was real feeling in it, which was the worst of it, and Krystyna looked at this woman with her dark root and her expensive gloves and her factory at Asnières, and thought — as she thought, more and more often that winter — *you are going to be very useful to somebody, one day, and it will not be by your own choice.*

***

The concierge of the house on the rue Saint-Honoré was called Madame Roussel, and she had a lodge with a brown curtain and a stove and a cat, and she kept a ledger.

The ledger had been in the lodge since 1937 and had been kept first for the landlord, who wanted to know about unpaid rent, and then for a period of two years for nobody much, and then, from the autumn of 1942, for a purpose: a column of arrivals and departures, in the concierge's own hand, which was slow and round and had been made at a school in the twelfth arrondissement in about 1892.

It recorded what a concierge in a good building in the eighth arrondissement would record, which is everything: the coalman, the doctor for the second floor, the priest for the fifth, the Englishwoman's letters before the Englishwoman left. And, since October, it recorded — in a column that had been added at the right-hand edge, in a hand that was pressed a little harder than the rest — the hours at which Madame Herschlag, second floor left, went out in the evening, and the hours at which she came in.

Krystyna saw the column on the twenty-fourth of November, at half past six, standing in the lodge with her key in her hand, being handed her post by a woman who had been three years at the same post, who kept the stairs clean in a building that was not hers, who had a nephew in the Resistance, and who had never in her life been asked a question by a German that she could not answer with a shrug and a shrug's worth of truth.

"Madame Herschlag," the concierge said. "There is a man asking for you. Here, in the afternoon, on Tuesday, and again this morning."

"A man?"

"German," said Madame Roussel, in the voice of a woman reporting a bill. "Not a soldier. He came in a hat and he asked me who lives on the fourth floor and he asked me how long you have had your rooms, and he asked me whether there are many Poles in the building, and he asked me whether I could read, madame, if you can believe it, whether I could *read*, and I said yes, monsieur, I can read, and he wrote it down."

"Did he give a name?"

"No name." The concierge pushed the ledger an inch further away from the edge of the counter, and Krystyna did not look at it, not once, and never would; she had memorised the whole of the relevant spread in October in the eleven seconds it had taken the concierge to find the second-floor post.

"And the column?" she said. "The one at the edge."

"That," Madame Roussel said, "was there before the man came. It was there in October. A gentleman asked me to keep the hours of the people who go in and out at night, because of the blackout, he said, because of the thefts in the building, and he had a little list of the tenants and he ticked the ones he had spoken to, and when he had gone I looked at the list, and madame, every name on it was German or Austrian. Not one French name. Not one."

"Did you tell the man in the hat?"

"I did not," said Madame Roussel, and closed the ledger with her hand flat on it, and looked at Krystyna for the first time, straight on, which was a thing she had never done in three years. "My nephew is in the country. He writes to me about his chickens. He writes a great deal about his chickens. If you understand me, madame, then we understand each other, and if you do not understand me, then I have said nothing about chickens and it is of no consequence."

"I understand you," Krystyna said.

"Then good evening to you," said the concierge, and took up her knitting.

She went up two flights with the dress box under her arm, and let herself into two cold rooms, and stood in the dark without putting on a light for the length of one cigarette, which she did not smoke but held, and thought about the man in the hat, who was neither a fool nor a Frenchman with a grudge, and who had asked a concierge to keep a record of the night movements of the German and Austrian tenants of a building on the rue Saint-Honoré, and about the fact that a man who starts with the German tenants is looking, precisely and patiently, for something that is German and is being used against the Germans, and that when he finishes with the German tenants he will arrive, with his notebook and his careful questions, at her.

She had five days, by her own reckoning, before the letter arrived, and she would spend all five of them doing what she always did, which was work.

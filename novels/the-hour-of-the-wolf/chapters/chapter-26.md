# Chapter 26: Epilogue: The Hour Between

On the first of August 1945 the city of Warsaw had about three hundred and eighty thousand people in it, which is a third of what it had had in 1939 and about twice what it had had on the second of October 1944, and it lived in a shell.

It lived in the cellars of burned houses and in the ground-floor rooms of flats with no upper storeys and in the corridors of buildings with no roofs, and it lived in eleven thousand temporary dwellings that were made of brick from the ruins and beams from the ruins and, in the first winter, doors from the ruins; and it walked everywhere along paths that had been beaten through the middle of the streets because the pavements were full of glass, and along corridors in the middle of the rubble fields that had been the streets of the city of Warsaw, in which a person could walk for four hundred metres between walls that were two storeys high and see nothing whatever except brick, and, at intervals, a chimney standing by itself in the middle of the whole of it, like a thing put there by a man with a sense of humour who had been shot in 1943.

There were graveyards in the middle of the junctions.

That is the thing that nobody who did not see that city in 1945 can put together, and it is the first thing the eye noticed: that a city which had buried about two hundred thousand of its people in two months, in courtyards and cellars and gardens and along the edges of squares, in the middle of a battle, had then had to live in the same space as its own dead for the whole of a winter; and in the spring of 1945 the committees of the city had begun the work of opening the temporary graves and moving what was in them to the consecrated ground of the cemeteries, and the work went on for three years, and while it went on, the courtyards of the city of Warsaw were full of careful, temporary, numbered little mounds, with paper labels and wooden pegs and, in eleven thousand cases, a bottle buried at the head of the grave with a name in it, written in pencil on the back of a food card.

There were also, on the first of August 1945, two policemen of the Polish state in grey coats standing at the corner of the Marszałkowska and the Świętokrzyska, and their work that morning was not the work of the traffic; and the whole of what they were doing was watching about four hundred people go past on their way to the places where the barricades had been, with flowers, in the first hours of the morning, on the anniversary of a rising which the security organs of the Polish state had been instructed to regard as an affair of the reactionary elements, and which the population of the city intended, that morning, to observe by walking.

At the front of the crowd that went up the Marszałkowska there was a woman of twenty-five in a grey coat, in the heat, with a handbag, and she walked the whole length of the street from the Plac Zbawiciela to the Świętokrzyska at the pace of a person going somewhere, and nobody looked at her twice, and nobody in that crowd of four hundred people knew who she was, and that, after five years and eleven months, was the whole of what she had ever wanted.

***

Her name was Krystyna Stankiewiczówna and she had been out of the country's western provinces since the spring, and the whole of what she had done between the road to Pruszków in October 1944 and the first of August 1945 was four things.

She had spent the winter at Krasnystaw and then in the villages east and south of it, as Maria Nowak, in a room of a farm with a family of eleven that had taken in the population of a street over three weeks in October and had been given, in return, nothing whatever except the company of the people it saved; and she had taught Latin in a village school for two months in the spring of 1945, because the woman who had been the teacher was in a hospital in Lublin and because the school had a Latin primer of 1931 in a cupboard and a woman who could read it.

She had worked from the spring, as a clerk, in the office of the Polish committee in a town on the Vistula, where she had counted the returns of the properties of the district and catalogued 1,100 case files of the German administration's requisitions of the years 1940 to 1944, which was the only paper on earth that a person with her training was fitted to work on, and which she did with the whole of her capacity, in a room with four people and a stove that smoked, and which is why the requisitions of the district of Kozienice are among the most exactly catalogued of the whole of the country and were cited in eleven judgements in 1947 and 1948.

She had sent, in November 1944, through the priest and the church and the arrangements that had been made at a fence in a factory yard on the Wolska in October, the papers of the destruction of the city of Warsaw to the office of the Polish state in Lublin: the four hundred and forty pages of the schedule of the demolition of the city, the evacuation orders, the eleven-page list of the buildings with the Royal Castle and the waterworks and the library of the University in it, and the file from the safe of a villa on the Frascati Street, and the notebook of an officer of the Sicherheitspolizei with nine pages of chart in it and the name of a woman written on the first page; and the whole of that material was in the files of the Commission for the Investigation of German Crimes in Warsaw in 1946 and was read aloud, in German, in a court in Warsaw, in the rooms of the former German administration on the Nowy Świat, in the presence of judges of the Polish state and of observers of four armies, in the year 1947.

And she had come back to Warsaw on the twenty-eighth of June 1945, on a train with her sister, over the bridge over the Vistula, which was a bridge built by the engineers of the Polish army after the German engineers of the Reich had been able to destroy everything in the city of Warsaw except the parts of it that other people watched them not destroy; and the train had gone into the station at Praga and the two of them had walked over the river in the heat, and the whole of what the city looked like when it came up over the embankment is not to be written down in this chapter either, because every one of the four hundred thousand people who came back to that city in that year wrote it down in some form and the book does not need to compete with them; and the whole of what this chapter has to do is to say what happened to the people, and to stand in two places in the city on the first of August 1945 for a full page each, and to put two people in a courtyard with a chalk anchor on the wall.

***

Hanna Stankiewiczówna was nineteen years old, and she worked in the records office of the department of the reconstruction of the city in a building on the Krucza Street that had three floors and no windows on the north side, and her work was the registration of the properties of the city of Warsaw in four card indexes and the answering of the letters of the people who wrote in from all over the country to ask whether anything was left of a street; and she was thin, and she had a cough that had started in the winter of 1944 and had not gone away, and she had a habit of holding her pen the way her father had held his; and she was being courted, in that summer, by a boy of twenty-two of the AK of the Mokotów district who had lost his left arm at the shoulder on the twenty-second of August 1944 and who worked in the same building, in the department of the water supply, and who was, in the opinion of the whole of the office, the most cheerful person on the third floor, and who had learned to write with his right hand in a way that Hanna said, in the voice of her father, was better than the handwriting of the whole of the rest of the department.

Maria Brandt was fifty-nine years old and she lived in two rooms in a building on the Krucza Street.

She had come out of the Warsaw rising alive, having spent the whole of it in the cellars of the Wola and the Śródmieście and having been taken, with eleven thousand others, to the transit camp at Pruszków and released from it in November 1944 as the widow of a German technical director of the firm of Lilpop, Rau i Loewenstein, and she had spent the first three months of 1945 in a village near Skierniewice with a family who had kept her because her Polish was the Polish of that country and because she could do accounts; and she had come back to Warsaw in April with a bundle — and with the two things that had survived her whole life entire, which were a photograph of her wedding day of 1911 and a rosary of her mother's.

The photograph was framed and it stood on the table in the room and it faced into the room, because a woman of fifty-nine who has spent four years with a picture turned to the wall in a flat in which a man who signed papers for the German administration came home at eight o'clock has earned the right to have it facing out.

She had, on the first of August 1945, two subjects and no others.

The first was her son, who was alive, and who had a leg that ached in the wet, and who had been read into the registers of the Polish state in the spring of 1945 as a Polish citizen of the name Stanisław Konrad — which was the last piece of paper in the whole of the war, and which had been arranged through four hands over eleven months, on the basis of a card in a file and the testimony of a woman who had worked for four months in the same room with him in the ruins of the city of Warsaw — and who was, on the first of August 1945, a translator in the office of the government of the city on the Krucza Street, in a room with three other people, with a salary, and a room of his own in his mother's flat.

And the second was a photograph of a man in a hat with two children in front of a works in 1928, which stood next to the wedding photograph, and about which she said nothing at all, to anybody, in the whole of the rest of her life; and which her son found in her effects in 1971, with a note in Polish in her hand on the back of it, and the note said: *Albrecht Brandt, of the works on the Burakowska, killed in the Wola in August 1944, and his hands were the hands of an honest man.*

And Iga Zaleska was thirty-one years old and she was alive and she was thin, and she worked in the same office on the Krucza Street, in the records office, three desks from Hanna; and she had been released from the prison on the aleja Szucha in January 1945 in the confusion of the retreat, having been in it for sixteen months, and the whole of the sixteen months had been interrogations about a ledger that she had never once mentioned and a list of names that she had kept in her head, and on the day she came out she weighed forty-one kilogrammes and she walked with a stick; and she had, in the whole of the book, one smile, and it is on the twenty-eighth of June 1945 in a corridor of an office on the Krucza Street when a woman of twenty-five came up the stairs with a sister and stood in front of her and said nothing at all for four seconds, because there is nothing to say to a person who has kept a ledger; and the smile was small and it lasted about two seconds, and then she said, in her flat voice, in Polish: "Stankiewicz. You are late. Sit down. There is work."

And Antoni Krupa was dead.

He had been arrested on the eleventh of November 1944 in Warsaw, in the ruins, in the flat of a woman on the Hoża Street, by officers of the Soviet and Polish security organs; and he had been held in a cell on the Koszykowa Street and interrogated about the Home Army's intelligence service and about the four hundred people whose names he had carried in his head for five years; and he died in that cell on the second of December 1944, at the age of forty-seven, and the book knows nothing else about it, because there is nothing else that is known, and the woman who was told of it in July 1945 in a corridor sat down on the floor of that corridor with her back against the wall and stayed there for about four minutes, and then got up and went back into the room and registered eleven more properties of the city of Warsaw.

***

She walked to the Mokotowska Street at eleven in the morning of the first of August 1945, and the walk took her fifty minutes, and the whole of it was through what the city was: pavements that were heaps, a tram line that ran for four hundred metres and stopped in the middle of nothing and began again on the other side of a field of brick, a queue at a water point with two men in it and four children, a woman selling tomatoes out of a bucket on the corner of the Krucza, a building with its front gone and its staircase standing in the open air with the ironwork going up into the sky, and, at the corner of the Mokotowska and the Wilcza, in a heap of rubble that had been the corner of the Mokotowska and the Wilcza, a man of about seventy with a hammer, sitting on a brick, taking the bricks out of the heap one at a time, knocking the mortar off them with the hammer, and stacking them in a clean, square, tidy stack of about four hundred bricks beside him, because there is a brick in the ruins of Warsaw and the brick is worth something and the whole city was rebuilt in that year by people who took it out of the heaps with their hands; and he looked up at her and said, "Good day," and she said, "Good day," and he went on knocking the mortar off a brick.

The courtyard of the building on the Mokotowska Street was exactly where it had been, which is to say that the gate with the iron bar at the bottom that a person had to lift was still there, and the pump was there, and the shed was there, and the wall of the shed was there, and above the place in the corner where a man of sixty-nine had been buried in a sheet in a hole dug by four men on the third of October 1944 there was the chalk anchor that his daughter of seventeen had drawn on the wall in the fresh mortar of a patched bullet hole.

It had been re-drawn since. That was the first thing she saw; and the second was that it had been re-drawn the same week, because a chalk mark on a wall in the open air of a Polish courtyard in 1945 is a mark that lasts about a rainy fortnight and no longer, and the mark in front of her was about four days old, and it had been done carefully, with a piece of chalk the size of the last joint of a thumb, in the way in which a person draws a thing that has been drawn four hundred times: the P and the W and the curving line of the cable, with the tail of the P crossed.

There was a piece of paper under a stone on the ledge of the shed window, and it said, in the square hard handwriting of a man of thirty who had been taught to write by a father with a Latin pen: *2/X. The grave is registered with the committee, number 11 402, of the courtyard of the Mokotowska 34, name Jerzy Stankiewicz, lat 69, and it was moved on the 11th of June 1945 to the Powązki, the military part, the row of the civilians, the fourth from the wall. There is a stone. It has his name and the two dates and no other words, because he told Hanna in 1943 that a stone with four lines of Polish on it in a city like this one is a stone that somebody is going to have to argue about, and he wanted none of that. I have paid for it out of the translation work. P.B.*

She stood in that courtyard for eleven minutes with the paper in her hand.

***

She walked the length of the Marszałkowska Street in the afternoon of the same day, from the Plac Zbawiciela to the Świętokrzyska, in the heat, with about four hundred people who were going the same way; and at the corner of the Krucza and the Marszałkowska, where the barricade of the first of August 1944 had stood, there was a heap of brick and a lamp post leaning at an angle of about forty degrees, and there were flowers on the brick: eleven bunches, tied with string and with paper, in the way in which flowers are put down in a country where a person who wants to remember a thing does it early in the morning and does not stand about afterwards.

That is the corner where Basia Marciniak was killed.

She had been nineteen years old, or seventeen; the file said nineteen and the girl herself had said seventeen twice, and a person who lies about her age upward is a girl who wants to carry the dispatches; she was killed at about noon on the eighth of August 1944 by a German rifleman in the tower of the church of the Holy Cross at a distance of about three hundred metres, with a canvas pouch with the buckles and the leather on her, and in the pouch there were three messages, and one of the three was in the handwriting of a woman who was standing at that corner in the heat of the first of August 1945; and there was nobody in the whole world, on that afternoon, in that city, in that crowd of four hundred people, who knew that; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood at that corner for four minutes in the middle of the flowers and the brick, and did not cry, and did not say anything, and then took the third thing out of her bag, which was a piece of chalk the size of the last joint of a thumb that she had taken out of her sister's drawer that morning, and drew, on the plinth of a wall at the corner, in the manner of a woman who has watched a thing being done four hundred times, the anchor.

***

And the man at the well was sitting on a heap of bricks in the courtyard of a house on the Mokotowska Street at four in the afternoon with a cane across his knees, in a Polish coat of the size of a man larger than himself, with his bad leg out in front of him and a bottle of something he was not drinking on the ground at his side, watching the corner of the courtyard as if he had been watching it since early in the morning, which he had.

He was twenty-eight years old. He had been read into the registers of the Polish state in the spring as a Polish citizen of the name Stanisław Konrad; he had spent the winter of 1944-45 in the ruins of the city of Warsaw as one of the three hundred people who lived in it through those months, reading the administrative orders of the German garrison of the city and passing eleven of them a week to the people who were going to be in it in the spring; he had been in that city on the seventeenth of January 1945 when the Soviet army and the Polish army came into what was left of it; he had walked out of it in February with a fever and a bad leg, and had come back to it in June; and he had a job in the office of the government of the city, and a room in his mother's flat, and a walking stick, and one photograph of four people on a lawn in front of a house with a portico in the summer of 1938, which he had kept in a drawer since the twenty-sixth of September 1944 and had never once shown to anybody.

He got up when she came into the courtyard. He got up slowly, because of the leg, with the cane in his right hand and the other hand on the wall; and the two of them stood four metres apart in the courtyard of a ruined building on the Mokotowska Street in the city of Warsaw on the first of August 1945, at four in the afternoon, in the heat, with a stack of bricks against the wall and a chalk anchor over the place where her father had been buried in a sheet, and there was a period of about eleven seconds in which neither of them said anything at all, and in which the entire history of the war, of Paris and of the avenue Henri-Martin and the Majestic and Odile's workroom and the well in Mazovia and the corridor with the mirror and the cellar on the Śliska and the two lamps over the mouth of the canal and the black water under the Saxon Garden and the ruined flat on the Mokotowska and the road to Pruszków, stood in a courtyard four metres wide between two people who had been holding the whole of it in their heads, in two languages, for four years, and neither of them was able to make a speech, and both of them knew that the other was not able to make one, and there is no way in the world to write the four seconds in which they both decided that it did not matter.

"You are late," said Phillip Brandt, in Polish.

"I walked the whole length of the Marszałkowska," she said.

"I know. I have been at this corner since eleven this morning, on the chance." He put his hand out, and she came across the four metres and took it, and they stood like that in a courtyard with a chalk anchor on the wall: a woman of twenty-five with a small, empty, silver compact in her handbag, and a man of twenty-eight with a cane and a bad leg and a photograph of 1938 in a drawer in a flat on the Krucza Street.

"I will tell you the whole of what there is to tell," she said. "It will take about four days. There is a bench by the wall and I have brought the bread, and my sister comes to her office at six, and the woman who keeps the coats on the Ogrodowa is alive, and I have been to the Powązki and there is a row of the civilians, fourth from the wall, and Maria told me on Thursday that she keeps the photograph of the wedding facing into the room."

"I know all of it," said Phillip Brandt, "and none of it from you, and I would like to hear the whole of it again, in your voice, in Polish, over about four days."

And then he said her name.

He said it once, in the courtyard of a ruined building on the Mokotowska Street in the city of Warsaw, on the first of August 1945, at four in the afternoon, in the heat, without any mask over it and without any German within eleven hundred metres, and he said it in the way in which a person says the name of a member of his own family who has come home: "Krystyna."

And the hour between, which is the hour of the night that the wolves have, the hour when the guard changes and the watch house is quiet and the whole ledger of the world is closed, and which in the city of Warsaw in the year 1944 had lasted eighteen months and had contained everything that either of them had ever done, ended in that courtyard; and there were two people alive in the middle of a field of brick in the middle of a country in the middle of a century, and the sun was on the wall where the chalk anchor was, and the bread was in the bag, and the work was in front of them, and neither of them said anything else about any of it for the whole of the rest of their lives.

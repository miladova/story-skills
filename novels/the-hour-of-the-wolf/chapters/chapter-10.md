# Chapter 10: The Brother

They could not go indoors, because there was no door in that city that could be shut on the two of them, and so the whole of what they had was ten minutes in the gateway of the courtyard on the Mokotowska Street, in the snow, in the dark, at twenty minutes past midnight on the eleventh of March 1944, two sisters standing a metre and a half apart and speaking in whispers with the whites of their eyes showing.

Hanna talked first because Hanna could not help it, and it came out in bursts, out of order, in the way a person empties a drawer onto a table: that she had known at once, at the first word, before the woman turned round, from the shape of the shoulders and the way she stood in the snow with her hands out of her pockets; that she had followed her from the courtyard a quarter of an hour before and had stood at the corner and watched her stand under the window for eleven minutes without counting, and had gone to fetch Tadeusz and come back, and that Tadeusz was at that moment in the arch of the courtyard on the other side of the Wilcza, waiting, because they had agreed that if it was not her he would walk past the end of the street and she would go home and they would never speak of it again.

"You knew at once," said Krystyna.

"At the first word," said Hanna. "At the way you said *Krysia* — no. Before. When you turned round. You turn your head the way you always turned it, sideways first and then the chin, and nobody else in this city turns their head like that, and if I have noticed it then so has somebody else, and you must stop it."

"Hanna."

"And you must not wear that coat into this street again, and you must not come at night, and there is a man on the corner of the Wilcza in a leather coat and I know him and he knows me, and if he sees you twice he will sell the information for a hundred zlotys and a bottle, and Tadeusz says—" She stopped. "He is angry."

"Where is he?"

"In the arch. He has been there twenty minutes. He said you were to come to him and not the other way round, and he said—" and here Hanna stopped again, and her face did something complicated in the dark, and she said, in a completely different voice, quieter and older: "He said, if it is her, I will see her alone first."

So they stood in the gateway for the ten minutes that the two of them had, and Krystyna asked the questions, and Hanna answered them, and the whole of the family's four years came across those eighteen inches of frozen air in a series of practical sentences, delivered in the order a seventeen-year-old thinks they should be delivered in, which was not the order of importance.

Mother died in the flat in the second week of March 1941, of pneumonia, after three weeks of a fever in which she had stopped recognising the neighbours on the fourth day and had never stopped recognising her daughters; the doctor did not come, because the doctor for that district had been arrested in January for signing a false Kennkarte, and the doctor they found on the fourth day wanted two hundred zlotys and a bottle of spirit and did not come either. Hanna had been sent for the priest at six in the morning. She came back with the priest and a neighbour, and the priest was too late by a quarter of an hour, and she has not been able to walk up the stairs of that building without hearing it ever since.

Father taught Latin, four boys, three afternoons a week, at the flat on Żelazna Street of a man who had been his student at the Gimnazjum in 1924 and who now had a pharmacy's licence and a black market and a soft heart; twenty zlotys a lesson and a kilo of bread a week, and the boys came with their own chairs because there were not enough in the flat, and one of them was the son of a German-speaking accountant and was learning the Latin for the same reason that Poles had been learning Latin since 1795, which is that the examined languages of an empire are useful and the dead one is a country.

Tadeusz was in a sabotage unit of the Home Army in the Śródmieście, and had derailed two trains and burned a warehouse of leather on the Wola side in October, and had been carrying the work for a year and a half, and drank on Fridays with his section and was quiet on Sundays with his father, and was, Hanna said, in the flat voice of somebody reporting an inspection, "not all right, and he will not be told anything by anybody, and since mother died he has only got one subject and it is you."

And the story: the typhus, the hospital in Toulouse, the month of June 1940, the mass once a year in the church on the Mokotowska Street, the photograph in the Latin dictionary, the letter that had come to the flat in 1941 from a Polish welfare bureau in the south of France with four lines in it and no signature, which their father had burned in the kitchen stove and had told the neighbours was the last notice from the hospital.

"You never believed it," Krystyna said.

"Which part?"

"Any of it."

"I believed it for eleven days," said Hanna, "and then I went to the church and looked at the list, because they read out the names of the dead of the parish once a year and they read yours, and I looked at the register of the parish and there was no burial for a Krystyna Stankiewiczówna and no notice from any hospital anywhere, and I was thirteen, and I went home and I sat on the stairs for two hours and I did not tell anybody, not ever, not once, not to this day." Her chin came up. "I have known for four years that you were alive. I have not known one hour of where you were. The first year I thought you were in a camp. The second year I thought you were in the Resistance and would send for me. The third year I thought you were in a bed somewhere with a German, because there are women in this city who are in beds with Germans and my father says they have all been shot, and I did not care." She wiped her nose with the back of her hand. "You are in Warsaw with him. You came to this courtyard in a coat worth three years of my father's lessons and stood under our window for eleven minutes, and I want to know if it is true, and I want it not to be true, and I want you to tell me that it is not true and I want to be able to believe it, and I am seventeen and there is nobody in the world I can ask."

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the gateway of the courtyard of her own childhood at twenty-five minutes past midnight in the snow, with a girl's breath going up between them, and did the mathematics of the next ten seconds; and the mathematics said: if you lie now, you will have to keep the lie for the rest of the war, and you will not manage it, and she will be the loose end that hangs you; and if you tell her the truth, you have put a seventeen-year-old courier in the possession of the fact that has killed eleven people in this city already, and she will carry it in a coat with no pockets; and the answer, which was not a good answer, was: she has known for four years; give her the truth, and give it to her in the smallest possible pieces, and make her complicit, because complicity is the only lock in a country where nothing can be kept.

"Hanna," she said, "I am a Polish officer's agent in the service of the government in London, seconded to the Polish intelligence in France in 1940, and since June 1943 I have been working out of the house of an Oberst of the Wehrmacht, and the arrangement is that I use what is in that house, and the arrangement has been running for nine months, and my handler was taken in Paris in February and I am here alone and I was recruited by the people you are standing at the end of the street from, ten days ago, on the Śliska Street, in a cellar under a workshop, and I have brought them nothing yet, and that is the whole of it, and you will never say one word of it to our father, or to Tadeusz, or to anybody you have ever met, because the moment it is spoken outside your teeth it is a death sentence on four people, and I do not mean a figure of speech, I mean that they will come at three in the morning and take all four of you out of that flat and shoot you in the courtyard, and they have done it eleven times this winter, and the notices are on the kiosk at the corner."

Hanna did not move.

"Say something," Krystyna said.

"Mother knew," said Hanna.

"What?"

"Mother knew. In the last week, when she still knew things, she said something to me in the night about you, and she said it in a voice that a person only uses about the living, and I have never been able to remember the sentence, only the voice. And then she died, and I have been alone in that flat with it for three years, and there was nobody to give it to." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand again, hard, the way you wipe a child's face. "I am not going to cry in this courtyard. There is a man on the corner. Give me your hands."

They held each other's hands in the dark for a moment, both of them in gloves, and then Hanna let go and stepped back and looked up and down the empty street and said, in an entirely businesslike voice, "Now the mark."

"What mark?"

"Your mark. Stand in the gate and watch the corner, and if the man in the leather coat comes past the lamp, cough twice."

And she took out of her coat pocket, along with a key and a lump of string, a piece of chalk the size of the last joint of a thumb, and went to the wall of the gateway, the wall beside the gate itself where the plaster had come away in a patch as big as a plate, and drew on it, quickly, in six or eight strokes, a figure that Krystyna could not see from where she was standing; and then she stood back to look at it, and put the chalk in her pocket, and came back.

"That is the anchor," she said.

"I know what it is."

"I draw it here every time I come out of that gate. I have drawn it here for two years and I have drawn it on the Wilcza and on the wall of the coal shed, and the caretaker washes it off and I do it again, and if anybody asks him he says the boys do it and he does not know who." Hanna's face in the dark was entirely serious and about eleven years old. "It is the anchor, and it is my signature, and it means this house is ours. And I want you to understand something, Krysia, because you have been away in France: they read the walls. They have got Poles to read the walls for them, the blue police and the szmalcownicy and a hundred kinds of scum, and a wall that has an anchor on it is a wall that is ours, and if there is going to be a raid on this courtyard I would rather the anchor was on the wall and the men who come to take my father looked at it and knew exactly who lived there, because on the day they come the one thing I have got left in the world is the knowledge that they are the ones who are wrong."

***

The arch of the courtyard on the other side of the Wilcza was eleven metres deep and had a lamp-bracket over it with no lamp in it, and the man standing in it was a good deal bigger than the boy she had last seen in the doorway of the flat in September 1939 with a railway warrant in his pocket and his mother's scapular sewn into the lining.

He had a stolen German greatcoat on over a grey sweater and his hands were out of his pockets. He had a broken front tooth that showed when he breathed the cold air, and a jaw that looked as if it was being held shut by main strength, and he was standing in the way a man stands when he has been standing in the same place for half an hour and is afterwards going to deny it.

"Hanna wanted to do this without me," he said. "I want that said first, because if you are going to hate anybody about tonight, it should be me and not her, and I want her out of it."

"Antek—"

"First thing," said Tadeusz, "is that you will not call me that. Second thing," and his hand came out of the dark and took hold of the sleeve of her coat, at the wrist, hard enough that she heard the seam, and turned her, not violently, into the light, such as it was, of the arch — "what do you smell of?"

"Let go of me."

"You smell of their cigarettes," her brother said. "And it is in the wool, at the shoulder, so it is not from a corridor. It is from a room, and it is from a long time in a room, and there is a smell under it, and I know what that smell is, and do you know how I know it? I put a boot on a man's chest in a cellar on Dzielna Street in November and I stood over him for four minutes and this is what my own sleeve smelled like afterwards, and I burned the shirt. Now tell me that you are working for us and not for them, and tell me it in a way that I can repeat to our father on Sunday."

She looked up at him in the arch, at a head taller than hers, with the greatcoat's collar turned up and a bruise fading along the jawbone that could have come from a doorframe or from a wall or from nothing at all.

"I am working for us," she said.

"Prove it."

"I cannot."

"You mean you will not."

"You are standing in a courtyard at midnight in a fine rain of snow with me in your hand and I have not heard the car. A car in this street after curfew would have brought a patrol in four minutes, and you knew that when you took me by the sleeve, so you have already decided to be quick, which means you were forty minutes deciding to be quick, and I am telling you the truth and you do not want it, you want a feeling, and I cannot give you the feeling, Antek, because the feeling is the one thing that is not for sale in this business, and every man who has ever had the feeling has been taken through the gate of the Szucha and has died in the disinfection room with his own initials on a form."
 
He let go of her sleeve.

"There," he said. "That is exactly what the Major said on Wednesday, in almost those words, and he took his spectacles off to say it, and I hated him for it and now I hate you the same way, and that is the shape of my life." He looked down the street, left, right, and then at the wall. "He sent for me on Wednesday. The Major. He told me that my sister is alive, and that she is in the service of the government, and that she is doing work nobody else in Warsaw can do, and that if she touches this family again she will be shot. And I said, good, and I asked him whether I might be the one to do it, and he said no, and I went home and I have not slept since." He breathed out, slowly, and it came out of him as steam. "Sit down. No — do not sit down, it is wet. Stand there against the wall and let me look at you for a minute, because I intended to do this quickly and I find I have got to know what your face is now."

She stood against the wall of the arch and let him look at her, and he looked for a long time, at her face, at the coat, at her hands, the way a man looks at a house he has been told is his and does not remember.

"You used to have the mark on your chin," he said. "The one from the sleigh. It is gone."

"It is a scar. Scars go."

"Not that one. Mamusia used to start every story with that scar." He was quiet for a moment and then he said, in a completely different tone, one that had nothing in it but the flat professional interest of a man in a stairwell in November: "What do you let him do to you?"

She did not answer him, because there was no answer that was not either a lie or a weapon, and because she had learned in the flat above the laundry that the only way to be asked a question you cannot answer is to let the silence stand in the room with an honest face; and her brother looked at her for four seconds and then hit her once, in the face, with the back of his hand, hard enough that the wall was what she felt, and the snow on the wall-brick went into her collar, and she held on to the corner of the brickwork with one hand and did not fall down and did not say anything at all.

"You never cried," he said. "Not even when the horse threw you."

"Antek."

"Do not." He had his hand up in front of his face, and the hand was shaking, and he looked at it with a kind of detached disgust, as if it belonged to a machine he had been given to look after. "It is shaking. It has been shaking since November. Mostly it does not matter, and twice it has mattered, and one of those times was a man on Dzielna Street and I have to believe that it mattered in a good cause." He put the hand in his coat pocket and held it there. "If they take me, it will be at the flat, and it will be in the morning, and Hanna will be the one who sees it out of the window over the courtyard, and she will not be allowed to go down, and I have told her that if it happens she is to take the papers and put on the coat with the fur collar and go to the Żelazna, to the chemist's, and say that she has come about the medicine. That is a thing you should know, in case you ever hear it, and in case you ever have to do anything about it."

"They will not take you."

"They will take all of us," said Tadeusz, without any drama at all. "This city is going to be taken apart by somebody in the next twelve months, and we are going to be in it when it happens, and I am 29 years old and my father has one lung and my sister is a seventeen-year-old courier, and I have thought about it every night since mother died and I have got exactly one comfort, which is that I will be doing the thing that is in front of me and not the thing that is far away. Come on. I am going to do a stupid thing now, and you will not argue with me about it, and it will take four minutes, and then you will go back to the Frascati and I will walk Hanna home and we will not see each other again until this is over."

***

He took her up.

Sixty-one steps: he went first in his boots, on the outside of the tread, because the treads were wooden and the building had ears; and she followed him in her Paris shoes, and on the third landing she put her hand out and touched the iron of the banister rail in a place where the paint had come off in 1936 and never been replaced, and found it exactly as it had been, and had to stop for a moment on the stairs with her brother's back going up in front of her.

The door of the flat opened on a corridor with no light in it: only the line of light under the door at the end of it, and the sound of a boy's voice reciting, and then the deeper voice that said, in Latin, *rectius vives, Licini, neque altum*, and corrected the boy's quantity with two words, and then the boys' laughter, and then the deeper voice again, going on; and the flat around it, in the dark, was the following things.

The brass umbrella stand, the only piece of the old house's furniture that had come with them from the Nowy Świat in 1940, standing where it had always stood, by the door, with two umbrellas in it that had not been used since 1939, and one of them broken.

The pianino in the corner of the drawing room with one key broken since the autumn of 1940, when the ceiling above it had come down in a bombing and taken the case with it; and on the top of the pianino, the photograph of Krystyna's mother at thirty in a white blouse, and a candle, and a tin of something, and the chair next to it that nobody had moved since March 1941, which was not a chair anymore but a place where a chair was kept.

The father's books, in rows, along the skirting board on both sides of the corridor, and standing in the doorway of the room where her parents' bed had been, because there was nowhere else in the flat for them and there had never been anywhere else for them; and the smell of the place — cabbage and floor polish and coal and the damp plaster of the back room and, under all of it, the smell of her own childhood, which is the smell of one particular flat out of all the flats in the world and which no amount of anything can be mistaken for.

And four metres away, past the end of the corridor, the door of the room with the light under it, with a class of four boys behind it, and her father's voice going on with the ninth ode of the first book: *tu ne quaesieris, scire nefas, quem mihi, quem tibi* — the poem about not asking what the gods have in store, taught by a man of sixty-four with one lung, in an occupied city, to four boys with their own chairs, at eight in the evening, in a flat where the electricity had been turned off twice that winter for arrears.

She stood there four metres from that door and did not go in.

Tadeusz stood in the dark beside her with his arms folded and his hand still in his pocket and did not say a word while she stood there, which was, she understood later, the largest thing her brother ever gave anybody.

Then he said, quietly: "The terms."

"Say them."

"Hanna knows. Hanna will keep it, because Hanna is the best of the three of us and always has been. Father will not be told while he lives." His voice did not change at all on the sentence, which was the only way he could get it out. "And if you ever come to this flat in daylight, or on the arm of a German, or in that coat, then everything between us is finished, and I will shoot you myself in the courtyard in front of Hanna, and I will be right to, and I want you to know that tonight, standing in this corridor, while you can still look at me." He unfolded his arms. "It is an ugly bargain. It is the best one available. Take it or go."

"I take it."

"Then that is the whole of the family," said Tadeusz, "and we will not speak of it again."

He took her down the sixty-one steps himself, and at the gate he put his hand in the pocket of the greatcoat and took out a folded piece of paper, and put it in her hand, and closed her fingers over it with his own.

"What is this?"

"It is the list. The Major gave it at the briefing on Wednesday and it is not supposed to exist, and I wrote it out in the tram on the way home, and if you are ever asked about it you will say you have never seen my handwriting, and I have not seen yours since 1939." He looked at her. "That is what they want out of the administration this spring: the liaison officers' postings, the engineer's reports on the bridges, the fuel allocations, the railway movement tables for the district, the lists of the firms doing work for the garrison. All of it paper, all of it in the offices, all of it useless without somebody who can walk into those rooms twice a week and be looked at as a woman and not as a spy."

She unfolded the paper in the light of the empty lamp-bracket.

The handwriting was her brother's, square and small and pressed hard into the paper the way a boy writes who has been taught to write by a father with a Latin pen; and the list ran to fourteen lines, in two columns, and the last line of it was not a document at all.

The last line was a name, underlined twice, with three words after it in brackets.

*Oberst Wolfram von Hagen (der Wolf)*.

She memorised the paper in the light of the empty lamp-bracket, once through, and then again from the bottom up, which is the way Wiktor had taught her in the flat above the laundry: the list, the two columns, the fourteen lines, the postings, the engineer's reports on the bridges, the fuel allocations, the railway movement tables, the firms with contracts.

Then she folded it and put it inside her left glove, and her brother, without another word, took out of his pocket a second scrap of paper, half the size, and gave it to her and closed her hand on it and walked away up the Wilcza to where their sister was standing in the shadow of a doorway a hundred metres off, and the two of them went away together without looking back.

The second scrap had one line on it, in the same square hand, and no punctuation: *Pani Grabska, Ogrodowa 18, flat 3, back stairs, second bell, she keeps coats.*

She walked home through the back streets with her hands in her pockets.

At the corner of the Bracka the patrol car was standing without lights and there were two Feldgendarmes on the pavement with a torch, and one of them turned the beam on her when she was still twenty metres away and held it there, and said something to the other one, and the other one stepped out into the middle of the street and put his hand up, and she stopped; and the whole of what happened in the next four minutes happened in a language that had been invented for the purpose of making a woman in a fur coat stand still in the snow while two tired men decided whether they should go home.

The papers were in order. They were extremely in order. *Herschlag, Marion. Austrian. Civil employee of the military administration, Warsaw.* They had a photograph, and a stamp, and a triplicate number, and a small red card behind them which was the pass of a household of the garrison command, and the Feldgendarme looked at all of it twice with the torch held under his arm, and then at her, and then at the papers again; and he asked her where she was coming from at midnight, in German, and she lied to him in German in the accent of a woman from Vienna, which she had; and he asked her what was in her glove.

"The glove is empty," she said, and turned her hands over, both of them, palms up, in the snow, with the left hand holding the left glove and the left glove holding the two pieces of paper in its finger.

The torch went over her hands and stayed on them for a second and a half; and the man in the street said something to the man with the torch and laughed; and the man with the torch said, "Go home, Fräulein," and stepped back, and let her go; and she walked on up the street with her knees entirely steady for thirty metres and then, at the corner by the villa's garden wall, she put her back against the bricks and stood in the dark and shook for eleven seconds, the way one does.

Then she took the two pieces of paper out of the glove.

She tore the list into strips and the strips into pieces and put them in her mouth, and chewed them, and swallowed them, standing in the dark against the wall of the garden of the house she was kept in; and paper is not a thing that can be eaten and it does not go down, and she stood there for four minutes with her hand flat against the wall, working at it, mouthful by mouthful, all fourteen lines of what the Home Army wanted out of the German administration in Warsaw, and the last line of it, twice underlined, in her brother's small hard handwriting, between her teeth: the name of a man she was going to get into the bed of within the hour.

Then she went into the house through the pantry door, and up the back stairs, and past the open door of the Colonel's room, in which he was asleep on his back with his hands open and the breathing of a man who has never had to be afraid in a bedroom; and she undressed in the dark, and lay down, and lay awake with her eyes open and the taste of paper in her mouth, and listened to the sentry on the gravel going round the garden on his eleven minutes.

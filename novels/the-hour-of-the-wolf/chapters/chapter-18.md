# Chapter 18: The Eve

The last dinner in the villa on the Frascati Street was on the thirtieth of July 1944, and it was for two, because von Hagen had sent word from the office at four that he would be late, that he would not bring anybody, and that the lady was to dine with him at nine; and at nine they sat at the table for twelve with the four places laid at one end of it and the candles lit, and Janina served them in the way she had served for four months, which was with her eyes on the dishes and her mouth shut, and the two of them ate a soup and a piece of veal and a compote of plums from the garden, and drank a bottle of the wine from the cellar of a family in the Żoliborz, and talked.

He began with the farm.

His brother's farm was in the Uckermark, four hundred hectares, rye and potatoes and a wood of about sixty hectares with a road through it, and it had been in the family since 1762 and had come to the brother in 1926 because the brother was the farmer and von Hagen was the officer; and in the last three weeks of July the brother had written twice, and both letters were in the tunic pocket of the man at the table, and both of them were about the same thing, which was whether to send the horses west now or in September. "He has eleven horses," von Hagen said, "and he has written to me twice, and the whole of his question is whether the Russian army will be in the Uckermark in October or in January, and he is asking me, at this table in Warsaw, on this date, as if I were a man who knows something, and I have written back twice and both times I have told him to keep the horses and to buy salt."

He talked about the horse he had had as a boy: a bay, thirteen hands, with a hard mouth and a habit of kicking in the shafts; and about the winter of 1917 in a school in Potsdam with no coal, and about the way his mother had cooked in a stove in a corridor; and Krystyna, who had spent two years listening to German officers talk about their childhoods and had learned what it was for, put her fork down when he came to the horse, and asked him a question about the horse, and listened to the whole of the answer.

He talked about Klaus.

The last letter had come in March 1943 and had been written on the seventeenth of January, in a cellar at Gumrak, by a boy of nineteen with a bad hand and the handwriting of a boy of eleven; and the letter had six sentences in it about the weather and the food and the men in the section, and then the seven lines that his father had read every week for sixteen months, and the seven lines were about a dog that had been at the airfield when he was posted and about a photograph, and one line about the horses, and at the end of it, *vati, I am not very good at this*; and there was no punctuation in the last three lines of it at all.

He said all of this with the composure of a man who had said it to himself many times, and the composure was the thing that told her; and at the end he took the letter out of the tunic pocket of a coat that was hanging in the study, where he had put it in March 1943 and had never kept it anywhere else, and he unfolded it on the table cloth between the compote and the wine and showed her the handwriting, and said: "He was left-handed and they had made him write with the right at the school in Potsdam, and he never got over it, and it made his hand what it is, and I have often wondered whether it was worth it, the school. It was worth it for me. It cost him six years of his life in a room he hated." He folded the letter. "That is the whole of what I know about my son for certain."

And her, with her hands still in her lap and the pearls at her throat and the whole of the city's August coming towards the two of them at ninety kilometres an hour out of the east, said: "I would have liked the dog."

"Yes," said von Hagen, with the letter in his hands; and then he put it in his pocket and rang the bell for Janina, and said the coffee was to be brought to the study, and they went through into the study and drank the coffee among the maps.

***

At eleven he went to the safe in front of her for the first time in nineteen months.

He did it because of the papers on the desk: a bundle of pages of the plan he was to take to the garrison's chief of staff in four days, and a signal that had come in from the district command, and a set of maps of the Vistula bank with the crossings marked; and he did not put them in the safe because he was cautious. He put them in because he was tired, and because the study of his own house after a good dinner is the one room in the world in which a man with a plan in four days' time does not want to think about a locked drawer.

He moved the lithograph of the Lilpop works off the wall on its hook, and the safe stood behind it — a Viennese insurance office's safe of the nineteen-twenties, dark green, with a brass dial and a lever, and she had lived in that room for five months and had watched that lithograph and had learned from a book in Paris in 1942 that a Viennese lock of that class has a spring in the dial that gives at the third turn and a lever that can be opened with a bent nail if the door has not been closed with the handle turned twice, and had never once in five months had the opportunity to look at the front of it from closer than four metres.

He turned the dial; and she stood where she was, with her coffee cup in her hand, at the corner of the desk, in the lamplight, with the distance she was permitted; and the whole of her attention, in the eleven seconds of it, was not on the door of the safe but on the place where the key was when he had done.

There was no key. It was not a key; it was a word and a dial and a brass lever, and the word he spoke when he turned the dial was a date under his breath, and the date was not his own or his wife's or his son's, and the date was the twenty-ninth of March 1917, which is the day a boy of fifteen at a school in Potsdam was told that the school would be closed in June and that he would be going to the war eighteen months before the rest of his year, and she stood in the room with a cup in her hand and heard the date said twice, once as a word under the breath and once as the small click of the dial coming to rest, and she wrote the date on the inside of her skull in the way she had been trained to write dates, and she never told anybody in her life that she had it.

He took the whole bundle out, and put it on the desk, and worked at it for two hours; and at half past one he put the papers back, and shut the door, and turned the lever, and turned the dial with the back of his hand so that the numbers were not left at the word, and hung the lithograph back on its hook, and blew out the lamp and went up to bed.

And at three in the morning she came down in her dressing gown, in the dark, with her heart going in a way that she had learned in nine weeks in a corridor in Paris to disregard, and she moved the lithograph off its hook, and put one ear against the wall of the corridor for eleven seconds, and turned the dial to the twenty-ninth of March 1917, and the dial gave at the third turn, and the lever came down under her thumb, and the door of the safe came open on the first attempt as if a person had designed it for her.

The plan had been written in July and revised on the twenty-sixth and it was in three parts, folded in a cardboard cover with a ribbon, in a hand that was not his and with his own marginal marks in pencil in three places, and she sat down on the floor of the study with the safe open and the lithograph against her knee and read the whole of it from the first page to the last in the light of the moon through the blackout curtain, with her index finger moving on the paper and her mouth shut, for forty minutes.

The first part was the order of battle of the Warsaw garrison: the units of the Wehrmacht and the police, their quarters, their strengths as of the fifteenth of July, the three garrisons in the city and the four outside it, the artillery with the calibres, the armoured elements with the number of the vehicles, the depots with the ammunition state in rounds per weapon and in units of fire, and the marching battalions on the Vistula line that could be recalled to the city within eleven hours of an order.

The second part was the engineering: the state of the crossings of the Vistula at the four points, the two railway bridges and the two road bridges with their approaches, the demolitions that had been prepared and loaded under each of them by the Poles in 1939 and by the Germans in 1943, the two depots of explosive at the Citadel and at the airfield on the Wola side, the water works and the filters on the Czerniakowska, the gasworks on the Wolska, the power station on the Wybrzeże, the telephone exchange on the Zielna, and an appendix of eleven railway stations with the trackage that it was intended to destroy if the city was given up; and in that appendix, in his pencil, in the margin, next to the Gdańsk station, was the German word *Bedingt*.

And the third part was the evacuation of the German administration: the scheme for the movement west of the offices, the families and the files; the routes; the train schedules; the four points at which the columns were to cross the Vistula; the date on which the first of the civil transports was to leave, which was the fifth of August; and the whole of it written on the assumption that the city would be held as a fortress while the administration and the families went out behind it, and the last page of it was a table of the offices in the order in which they were to go, and the second of them was the counter-intelligence section of the garrison command.

She read it twice; and then she put the whole of it back in exactly the order it had been in, and closed the door, and turned the lever, and turned the dial with the back of her hand, and hung the lithograph back on its hook; and then she went up the stairs and into her room and lay in the dark until six, with her eyes open, holding, in her head, the whole of a German fortress plan of the city she was born in, and the only thought that kept going through her mind was not fear and was not triumph and was not even Anna or her father, but the second of August, which was the day on which the second of the offices was to leave, and which meant that von Hagen would be in Warsaw for two days after the transports began, and that he would have the whole of the plan with him until then; and it was at eleven minutes past five, with the grey coming into the window, that she made the decision, which was that this was the work of the next day and a half, and that everything now came down to hours.

***

At six she came down dressed for the morning, with her hat and her bag and the coat over her arm, and she found him in the hall.

He was standing in the doorway of the study with his tunic open and his hair wet and a cup of coffee in one hand and the silver compact in the other, and she stopped on the third step of the stairs with her hand on the rail, and the whole of the twelve metres between them was entirely quiet.

"This was on your dressing table," he said. "I went up for my cigarette case because I had left it on your window sill last night at eleven and I could not find it in the dark, and I found this instead, and I looked at it, and I hope you will forgive me."

There are four seconds in such a moment and they run in a way that is different from the way the rest of a life runs; and she spent the first of them looking at his hands — at the cup and the compact in a man's hands that were cracked across the knuckles and had a duelling scar's worth of small damages on them, the hands that had lain on her shoulder at night, the hands that had slapped a woman of forty-five across a dining room for a cold plate — and the second in looking at his face, which had no suspicion in it at all, which is the thing that nobody who has not lived that life will believe; and the third and fourth in deciding, with the cold, fast, exact machinery that was the whole of the training and the whole of what she had become, that there was nothing to be decided, because a man who has not opened a compact in the thirty seconds he has been standing with it is a man who is not going to open it.

"I bought it in Paris," she said. "In the rue de la Paix in December. It cost nine hundred francs and I have never told you the price, and if you are going to stand in the hall with my powder and my puff in your hand then I am going to be late for my appointment."

And von Hagen laughed, and opened the lid of the compact with his thumb and looked at the powder and the puff and the little mirror, and did not look at the bottom of the case, and did not press the floor of it, and closed it, and said, in the tone of a man who is comfortable and has been amused early in the morning: "I like knowing where your things are. I have kept everything you have ever left in this house; there is a drawer in my desk and it is the top one on the left, and the whole of you is in it — a glove, a letter from a dressmaker, a hairpin, and this." He put the compact in his trouser pocket. "I will put it in the drawer tonight. Do not take it. I like the arithmetic of it: if I know where your things are, I know where you are."

She came down the last three steps and stood in front of him in the hall and let him kiss her forehead, which he had begun doing in June and did every morning, in front of Janina, in the way of a man who has decided that his household is his household and does not care who sees him in it.

At eight he went out to the car; and at the door he turned, with his gloves in his hand, and said the thing he had said three times in the week: "Keep your things ready. Nobody knows anything yet, but if it comes, we may have a day's notice and we may have an hour of it, and I do not intend to leave the lady of this house standing in a street in Warsaw with a suitcase while the sappers are walking around the bridges with a plunger." He put on his cap. "There is a sealed pass in the drawer of the study in the top left, and if you ever need it while I am not in this house, use it, and do not ask anybody anything."

"I shall be here at one for luncheon," she said.

"Yes," said von Hagen, and got into the car and was driven away up the gravel and through the gate and out into the rubble and the sunshine of the last ordinary week of the German administration in Warsaw.

***

She walked to Mokotowska Street at twenty past nine in the morning, in the hat and the grey coat, with the papers of the garrison's plan in her head and no paper anywhere on her body, and it took her twenty minutes by the back streets and she went in at the gate of her own courtyard at the hour when the whole of that city is out of doors: the caretaker sweeping, two women at the pump, the coal man's boy, and four children playing a game with a bottle cap on the flags.

Three hundred and forty grams of paper had been in her hands in the study at three in the morning, and she had taken, in the end, exactly none of it out of the safe and out of the house; and everything she carried now was in her head, where nobody could take it from her, and she had spent the walk home from the villa on the day before writing it down in the inside of her own skull in the way Wiktor had taught her, which is by section and by page and by number and not by meaning, so that no man with a knife and a rubber truncheon could ever get the whole of it out of her in less than four hours.

Her father was at the table in the corridor room with the books along the skirting and a Latin primer open on the tablecloth and a plate with the end of a bread on it, and four chairs drawn up in front of him, and one of the four chairs had a boy in it, a boy of about fourteen in a jacket that was too big for him with his hands folded on the table, and her father was saying, in the patient voice of a man who has said the same thing a hundred times and enjoys saying it a hundred and first: "—therefore, when you have read *iuventus*, you will find that it is not the young man and not the youth but the thing that is common to all of them, which is the age itself; and the reason the Latin does that and the Polish does not is not that Latin is better, it is that Latin was the language of men writing about a whole state and a whole people, and the Polish was for three hundred years the language of men writing letters—"

And then the door of the room at the back opened, and Hanna came in with a jug in her hand and stopped in the middle of the kitchen door with her mouth open and her hands going up, and the jug came down onto the table with a bang and the water came out over the Latin primer and the boy of fourteen jumped up with his chair going over and Krystyna, in the doorway of that flat, put her finger up in front of her mouth.

Her father had turned round.

He sat there with his hands on the arms of the chair and looked at her over the top of his spectacles for four seconds; and he was white, and thin, and there was a stain on the tablecloth and the boys of his class were looking from one of them to the other with their mouths open; and he stood up, holding the edge of the table with one hand as a man of sixty-nine stands up, and came round the table, and stopped one pace short of her, and looked at her face, and then at her coat, and then at the door behind her, and she saw him do the arithmetic — the coat, the hat, the hour, the pass in her bag, the story of the daughter who had died of typhus in a hospital in Toulouse in 1940, which he had built with his own hands in a kitchen four years before and had maintained with the whole of his will ever since.

And he walked out of the room without a word.

He went past her, in the doorway, so close that she smelled the soap and the wool of him, and his hand came out and took hold of the doorframe of the kitchen door for a step, just once, the way a man does when he has stood up too quickly, and he went into the back room, and the door did not quite close, and through the gap she heard the sound of a man of sixty-nine years taking hold of himself in the dark, and then the sound of a chair being moved, and then nothing at all.

There were four minutes.

Hanna had her hands over her mouth and her back against the kitchen door and the tears coming straight down her face, and the boy of fourteen was standing at the end of the table with his jacket hanging off him and his Latin primer upside down, and the second pupil, who was in the far corner, had got up and put his chair in front of him like a man behind a fence; and Krystyna stood in the doorway of her family's flat and did the arithmetic of those four minutes in the following way: she took off the hat, because her hat was worth eleven months of her father's lessons, and she put it on the table beside the jug, and she took off the grey coat, and she said, in the flat, ordinary Polish of a sister: "Who is this?"

"Olek," said Hanna, in a voice that came out of a throat that was not working. "This is Olek Górski, and the other one is Tadeusz, that is Tadeusz Wrona, he is the son of the accountant, and they come on Tuesday and Thursday, and they have their own chairs because there are not enough chairs, and you are not to say anything, and you—" and then she stopped and put her face on her sister's shoulder and stood there for about eleven seconds, and the two boys watched two women hold each other up in a kitchen in Warsaw in July 1944, and neither of them said a word, and the whole of that flat kept the secret from that hour on, because a boy of fourteen who is taught Latin by a man of sixty-nine in a kitchen in his own flat with his own chair has already learned the one lesson; and Hanna, when she lifted her head, said, in a whisper, into her sister's ear: "He is shaking. He has been shaking since March and it is not the cigarettes, I count them, and he does not eat the meat, he gives it to me, and he has stopped going out for the newspaper, and yesterday he asked me what a person should do if the whole city were to be—" and she stopped, and then she said the sentence that Krystyna carried out of that flat with her: "He has been waiting for you every day for two years. He knew you would come. That is what I cannot forgive the two of you for."

And before she put the coat back on, she stood for a moment in the room that had been her mother's and her father's and was now his alone, with the bed and the chest of drawers and the chair with the coat on it; and she took the cord off her neck with both hands, and the signet ring came off it, her father's ring with the greyhound and the willow leaves that the German removals men had carried the sofa past in 1940; and she opened the top drawer of the chest, where there was a pair of her mother's gloves of 1936 in tissue paper, and she put the ring inside the left glove of that pair, and shut the drawer, and stood with her hand flat on it for about a second and a half.

She had carried that ring against her skin in a city under occupation for four years and eleven months, in defiance of the only good advice she had ever been given in the trade, and she left it in a glove in a drawer in a flat on the Mokotowska Street on the thirty-first of July 1944 for one reason, which she said out loud in that room, in Polish, to the chest of drawers, because it was the last sensible sentence she was able to say in the whole of the next eleven weeks: "I am not going to be found with it."

She put the coat back on; and she went to the door of the back room and stood in it, and her father was sitting on the edge of the bed in the dark with his hands on his knees and his spectacles off and his face turned to the window with the blackout paper on it.

"Tatusiu," she said.

"I know what you are," said her father, without turning. "I have known since 1940; I know it the way a man knows the weather in his own bones; and I have told nobody, and I shall tell nobody, and I am not going to ask you anything, not one thing, because a man who has asked a question is a man who can be made to answer one. Go home to whatever it is you have got, and do not come here in daylight again, and if I ever see you in the street with a German I shall spit on the pavement and I shall do it in front of people, and you will not turn your head."

She went out through the kitchen, and Hanna came down the stairs with her as far as the second landing, and gave her, on the landing, in the dark of the stairwell, a piece of paper folded four times: the news that the section's couriers were carrying that week, in a hand she did not know, which was the district command's bulletin on the state of the front at Radzymin and Wołomin and the four words at the bottom about the direction of the Red Army's armour. "You will need it," Hanna said, "and I am not going to ask you what for, and you are to burn it when you have read it, and if you are ever caught with it on you I shall be shot in a courtyard with the rest of them, and I have decided that is an acceptable price, and you are not to argue with me about it, and you are not to tell Tadeusz, and you are not to tell Tatusiu." She kissed her sister's cheek, on the landing, in the dark, quickly, with her eyes shut; and then she said, "What is your name now, in the German papers?"

"Marion Herschlag."

"Marion Herschlag," said Hanna, pronouncing it exactly as a German would, and then, in Polish: "Marion Herschlag, you are the worst thing that has ever happened to this family. Come back in the winter." And she went up the stairs.

***

She delivered the plan on the thirty-first of July between eleven and four, in three batches, in a place on the Śliska Street, and the method was the method of the cellar and the method of four years: nothing written, nothing carried, nothing said twice.

The first batch was the order of battle: the units, the quarters, the strengths as of the fifteenth, the artillery with the calibres, the armoured elements and the numbers, and the ammunition in rounds and units of fire, and the marching battalions on the Vistula line that could be back in the city in eleven hours. She gave it to Krupa in a room with a blackout curtain and a table and a candle, standing up, in the order in which it had stood in the pages, with the numbers written as pairs and the unit designations spelled as sounds, and he wrote it down in a shorthand that was his own invention and had been taught to eleven people in five years, and when she had finished he pushed the pad across to a major of the artillery who had been sitting in the corner without speaking, and the major read it for eleven minutes, and then looked up and asked her four questions about the calibres, and she answered all four, and he looked at Krupa and nodded once.

The second batch was the engineering: the crossings at the four points, the two railway and two road bridges, the demolitions prepared in 1939 and 1943, the two explosive depots, the waterworks and the filters, the gasworks, the power station on the Wybrzeże, the telephone exchange on the Zielna, and the appendix of eleven railway stations with the trackage marked for destruction, and the word *Bedingt* in the pencil margin next to the Gdańsk station, which she gave them exactly as a word, and then, because a word without a context is a stone, gave them her own reading of it — that a German engineer writing *conditional* next to a station on a list of demolitions is a man writing to his superiors that the order is either impossible or unwise, and that in either case the man who wrote it will not be asked again.

And the third batch was the evacuation scheme: the offices in the order in which they were to leave, the routes, the schedules, the four crossings for the columns, the first civil transport on the fifth of August, and the position of the counter-intelligence section of the garrison command as second of eleven offices to go west, and the date that mattered, which she put in front of them in one plain sentence: that the second of the offices was to leave Warsaw on the second of August, which meant that the man who held the plan would be in the city with it for one day more, and that after that he would be on a train with it, and that no man in that room was ever going to see the original of it again.

There was no gratitude in that room. That is the thing about the delivery, and it was the only piece of work of her whole war in which she took a pleasure that she never once repented of, and the pleasure was not in being thanked: it was in watching an institution work with a good tool. Two men bent over the table with a lamp and a sheet of paper, the quartermaster with the pencil asking her for the ammunition figures a second time and a third time, the woman at the window with the blackout curtain and the boy at the door with his ear on it, and at the end of the third batch, when the papers were copies in shorthand and the originals were in her head and nowhere else, the artillery major took his spectacles off and said the sentence that the whole of the movement had been building towards since the middle of July:

"The twenty-fourth hour, then. And God help us all."

And Krupa said to her, as she was going out, at the door, with the two of them standing in the yard of a building on the Śliska Street in the sun: "You will go home tonight and you will keep your hours and you will be in the villa tomorrow and you will be there at five o'clock, whatever it costs, because a woman who disappears from that house on the first of August is a woman whose disappearance will be reported in an office in twenty minutes." He looked at the yard, at the coal shed, at the two boys carrying a crate. "It is the twenty-fourth hour at five tomorrow. You will not be told anything more than that, and you will not ask."

***

The last hour of the ordinary life of Warsaw began at about four in the afternoon of the thirty-first of July 1944 and lasted, by the reckoning of the people who lived through it, about eleven days: the eleven days during which everybody in that city who did not know afterwards said that they had known.

The city on that Monday afternoon was the most ordinary thing in Europe: the queues at the food offices and the water pumps; the trams, crowded, with people standing on the steps; the pedlars on the Świętokrzyska with their stalls of cigarettes and shoelaces and gherkins; a wedding at the church of the Holy Cross at four, with the bride in white and the guests on the steps and the cars with their ribbons; the children in the courtyards playing a war of their own with sticks and bottle caps and one real helmet; the women at the windows with the washing and the conversation; the boys selling the *Nowy Kurier* and the kiosks and the ice cream in the heat; and over the whole of it, in the middle of the afternoon, a heat of the sort that Warsaw gets four days a year, with the asphalt soft and the smell of the river and of the dust off the ruins coming up the Nowy Świat.

And under the ordinary thing, in the same hour, in the same streets, the movement of an army was coming out of the cellars.

It came out of the courtyards in the milk churns and the suitcases and the sacks of potatoes with a rifle in the middle, on handcarts with a grandmother in front and two boys behind, in the pockets of women who walked slowly with the shopping; it went up the stairwells of the Śródmieście in the afternoon in the hands of girls of seventeen, and it went into the flats, and onto the tables, and into the hands of the men who would be carrying it in eleven hours; and in the cellars along the Zielna and the Śliska and the Mokotowska there were men with lamps counting magazines, and the women of the courtyards stood in the doorways in the heat and watched the handcarts go by, and the whole of the city was doing the thing together, without a single speech, without a single word said out loud by anybody who did not have to say it.

On the Marszałkowska Street at four o'clock, standing on the pavement in the sunshine with a shopping basket she had bought for the purpose in the Kercelak in May and never used, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna looked at the city she was born in and did the arithmetic that she had done every day for four years, and it came out, on this day, in the following form.

That she had brought the plan to them, and that the plan had told them what the Germans had and what they had placed under the bridges and what they were going to do with the offices and the families; and that the plan, being a true and complete document, was going to make every hour of the next sixty days more expensive and better and more exactly planned than it would have been without it, and that the same plan was going to be used by men of nineteen years of age in a city of burned streets and no water, and that most of them were going to die of the arithmetic that was in it.

That the whole of her life for four years had been a set of arrangements to make this hour possible, and that in this hour she was a woman in a hat with a shopping basket and the papers of the Reich in a bag, and that when she walked into the courtyard on the Śliska that evening she would be a courier with a card and a pseudonym on it and a white armband, and that Marion Herschlag — the Viennese woman, the pearls, the piano, the Colonel, the bed, the hats, the song, the whole of the five years — would still be walking home to the villa on the Frascati Street, because there was one more day of her to be lived, and the last day of her was the next one.

And that in the villa on the Frascati Street, at half past nine tonight, a man of fifty-four was going to come home from the office and would find the lady of his house at the table with the compote of plums and would tell her about the eastern front, and that the lady of the house would smile at him, and that the man had a son's letter in his pocket and a farm in the Uckermark and a plan in his safe in her own handwriting's hearing, and that he had neither the first idea nor the last idea of what was going to be done to him.

At a quarter to five, in the sunshine, at the corner of the Bracka Street, in the direction of the German quarter, she saw Phillip Brandt coming towards the centre of the city in civilian clothes, with a bag in his hand and a folded German paper in his pocket, walking in the way of a man who has walked out of one life and has not yet arrived at the next one; and the two of them stood on opposite sides of a street full of people who did not yet know what was about to happen to them, in the flat heat of four o'clock in the afternoon of the last day, and looked at each other for about four seconds, and did not cross.

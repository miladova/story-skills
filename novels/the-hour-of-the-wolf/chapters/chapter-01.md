# Chapter 1: The Hour Between

The sentry walked the gravel every eleven minutes. She had timed him in October and again in November, and he had not changed, and that was the trouble with the Germans: they did not change. Eleven minutes, the long way round the garden, past the statue of the stag with its nose broken off in some earlier, better argument, and then a pause at the gate while he worked his feet in his boots to keep them warm.

She could hear the whole house from this bed. The kitchen pipe that ticked. The clock in the hall, which was two minutes fast and which the Colonel, who had set the other five clocks in the building to the second, had left alone, because it had belonged to the previous owner of the house and he considered it a French matter.

The man beside her slept on his back with his hands open, the way a man sleeps who has never had to be afraid in a bedroom. Fifty-four years old, hard in the shoulders and hard in the hands, grey at the temples and cut close at the neck. Saddle soap and cold tobacco and, very faintly, under both, lavender water, which was the one thing in this house he did not know he smelled of.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna lay still beside him in the dark of a requisitioned house on the avenue Henri-Martin and did what she had done every night for five months: she took the house in and held it.

She had never slept in this bed. That was the first rule and the last, and she had learned it in another city and paid for it in a colder currency. At three she would get up and use the water closet so that the house heard her do it; the maid would hear her, and the maid would mention it in the morning in the way servants mention things. She would come back along the corridor with her socks in her hand. She would go into the study at the end of the passage, where he worked when he could not sleep, and which was the only room in the house with a shutter that did not close all the way. And she would count to ninety and go back to bed, and at half past five she would stand up, dress in the dark, walk down the back stairs — skipping the third step from the bottom, which had told on her in July and never would again — and out into the morning with Rutz holding the gate and the whole of Paris, grey and wet and entirely occupied, on the other side of it.

Between now and then there was the rest of the night and the man in it. She lay with her forearm against his ribs because he liked that, and she kept her breathing slow because he was a light sleeper and because she had once spent a fortnight learning to breathe like a woman in love, and now it cost her nothing at all.

He woke at twenty past two the way he always woke when he wanted her, without a word and without any interval between sleep and appetite. His hand came over her hip and pulled her onto her back, and his weight came with it, and for a while the room was only the two of them and the ticking pipe and the sentry's boots going round the garden.

She had learned him the way she had once learned irregular French verbs: by repetition, without affection, until it could be done in the dark. He was not a brutal man in the way of the men in the illustrated papers. He was worse in a smaller way: he was a man who had decided what belonged to him, and who did not consider, at any point in the twenty minutes that followed, what the thing he owned might want. His hands were the ugliest part of him, cracked across the knuckles from a boyhood spent with horses he no longer had. He used them on her the way a man uses a good tool, unhurried, thoroughly, with satisfaction at the end and none at all in the middle.

She made the sounds he had taught her to make and meant about a third of them, and even those she did not mean in the way he believed. There was a particular pleasure in being wanted by a man like this — she had stopped lying to herself about it in the second month, because a lie you tell inside your own skull is the only kind that costs. She liked his weight after the war's thin men. She liked being the one thing in a Prussian's day that he could not organise. She liked the exact moment when his breath changed and the German in him went out and left only the animal, which was the moment she could have put a knife in his throat and which she had never once, in five months, been able to fully enjoy for what it was.

When it was finished he lay on her for a moment and then rolled away and put his forearm over his eyes, which was the closest he came to tenderness.

"Sleep," he said.

"Yes," she said.

She stayed on her back and looked at the ceiling. The cornicing had been done by someone in 1890 who cared about it: plaster laurel, a hundred-odd leaves, the last four of them botched where the man's hand had tired. She counted them the way other people counted sheep. She had got to ninety-one when his breathing went long and slow and the house, which had been holding its breath, let it out.

The clock in the hall struck the half hour, two minutes fast, and she put her feet on the floor.

***

The corridor was cold. Paris in November 1943 was cold everywhere except in German rooms, and the Germans warmed themselves with coal that came from France and would have gone to French houses in another century, and the fact of it had stopped shocking her in the summer and had not stopped being true.

She went to the bathroom first and ran the tap and washed her hands and looked at herself in the glass above the basin. Twenty-four years old. Dark blonde hair flattened on one side from the pillow. Grey eyes, her mother's, with nothing at all behind them, which was not true and did not matter. She had a good face for the work. It was the sort of face that men described as soft and women described as expensive, and both of them were wrong in the useful direction.

Then she went along the passage to the study, walking on the balls of her feet, not because she needed to but because the practice was the point.

The study was the Colonel's, but it had been somebody else's first. You could still see it: the bookshelves with the empty spaces where the books that mattered had been taken away in a hurry, the prints of horses on the wall, one of which hung crooked and had hung crooked since June because he did not notice pictures. His desk was oak and enormous and French, and on it, squared to the edges, were his blotter, his pen tray, his reading spectacles, and the file he was working on, which tonight was a brown folder with a string tie that he had tied in a particular knot because the man who tied it had tied it for the Führer and he did not care to undo another man's knot in front of a Frenchman's desk.

She had not touched the folder. She never touched the folder. The folder was the reason that other women in other houses got shot.

What she touched was the blotter.

It was a Frenchman's blotter, made of green leather, the size of a newspaper when opened out. A tidy man uses a sheet of blotting paper until it is soaked and then throws it away. The Colonel was not a tidy man. He was an orderly one, which is a different disease: he used each sheet for exactly one week, wrote the date in the corner on the Monday, and then, instead of throwing it away, laid it face down in the drawer of the table beside the desk, where a stack of a hundred and something such sheets now lived in chronological order, going back to a Parisian winter that had been somebody else's.

The present sheet lay in the blotter. She stood beside the desk with her hands behind her back, which was the posture she had settled on after some thought, because if the door opened she would be a woman looking out of a window with her hands behind her back, and because a woman with her hands behind her back is the least suspicious object in Europe.

The window was black. The shutter did not close. And in the gap between shutter and frame there was a slice of avenue, and in the avenue there was a lamp, and by the light of that lamp, four feet away, the sheet of blotting paper was legible the way a mirror is legible.

She read it from right to left. She had been taught to do this in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement, with a girl from Lille who thought she was teaching a Belgian, and she had never once failed at it since, though it was slow and it made one's head ache behind the eyes.

*Of the coastal sector works, the schedule has been reported to the Commandant. The batteries at Saint-Nazaire and La Rochelle will be inspected on the seventeenth and eighteenth by the officer of the Inspectorate, with a delegation from the Todt organisation. Requests for labour are to be submitted directly to the Feldkommandantur, not to the Kreiskommandantur, in the case of the two westernmost. The matter of the requisitioned horses at Nantes remains open.*

And under it, a hand she knew better than her own: a marginal comment, in pencil, that read: *Kessler again about the horses. Send him the second paragraph only.*

She read the whole sheet twice, twice right to left, and then once normally to be certain of the sentence about the labour requests, because the sentence about the labour requests was the one her network would want and because it contained a number of men, which was the kind of fact that got passed to London inside another fact, and because somewhere in London at the end of a chain of such facts there were men with maps who would use it.

Ninety seconds. She gave herself ninety seconds and then she went back the way she had come, and in the corridor she heard something that stopped her heart in the way a hand stops a bell: the maid's door opening two floors below, and a voice, and footsteps going down towards the kitchen.

She stood in the dark and did not breathe and counted the plaster laurels backwards from ninety-one, and when she reached one she went into the bedroom and got into the bed as if she had never left it.

He was awake.

"You were a long time," he said, in the dark.

"The water was cold."

"It is always cold."

"Yes," she said. "And I always hope it will not be."

He did not laugh, but he moved his hand onto her wrist, which was the closest he came to laughing. The hand was heavy and dry and lay over the small bones the way a lid lies on a pot. She had learned to keep her own hand relaxed under it, which had taken three weeks to master, and which now she could do in her sleep.

For a while there was only the sentry, and the pipe, and the clock in the hall deciding, two minutes early, that the hour could go and hang itself.

"I had a letter from my brother," the Colonel said.

She said nothing. She had learned that too. When they talk in the dark, a woman who says nothing gets more than a woman who asks, because the asking is the same as the leaving, and a man in the dark wants to keep talking to somebody who is not going anywhere.

"Kranzow is to be sold," he said. "The Pomeranian thing. My grandfather bought the land in fifty-nine, and my father planted the avenue, and the avenue is two hundred metres of linden and it will go into the ground with the man who buys the timber. My brother writes to me about the timber. He is fifty-nine years old and he writes to me about the timber."

She turned her head on the pillow towards him. The light from the street lamp came through the shutter in four bars, and in that light she could see the top of his forearm and the line where the pillow had pressed into his face and, beyond him, the photograph on the chest of drawers with its back turned towards the bed, which was the only picture in the house he kept facing away.

"Klaus would have gone back," he said. "He wanted the horses. He was a boy who liked things that were alive. His mother used to say it would be the ruin of him." A pause. "She was right about the things she was wrong about."

She knew better than to say anything about Klaus. She had said something about Klaus in September, one sentence, in the right voice, and he had got out of the bed and stood at the window for eleven minutes without speaking, and she had watched the shape of his back and understood that she was being shown something, and that being shown it was a test he was not aware of setting.

So she lay still, and after a while he did what he always did when he had come close to it: he turned away from his son and opened a file.

"You know they are stealing from us," he said.

"Who is?"

"Everyone. In this country, everyone. The French steal the coal and the clerks steal the coal the French leave behind. There is a whole department of my office that does nothing but count what is gone." He was awake now in the particular way of a man who has found something he can be busy about. "Do you know what I have spent the winter doing, my dear?"

"Seeing that the officers are properly shaved," she said.

"Ah. That is the Second Bureau." He moved onto his back and put his other hand behind his head, and the bed creaked in the place where it always creaked. "I am hunting one person."

Her body did nothing. It had been trained to do nothing, in another flat, with a girl from Lille, and it did nothing now with a completeness that would have frightened her if she had had any room in her for fear.

"A leak," he said.

"A leak of what?"

"Documents. Orders. Lists. Everything that is worth stealing and a great deal that is not." He said it with a kind of tired professional relish. "Two years, more or less. Some papers from the Kommandantur in the spring of forty-two, which we put down to a French clerk who hanged himself in Fresnes. Then the coastal material last winter. Then in the summer, three things in six weeks, which is what made them take it seriously. We have brought in the Sicherheitspolizei, which means we have brought in a number of people who cannot read a military appreciation but who can read a railway timetable, and who have been very busy arresting Frenchmen who were selling butter."

"And you are the one who catches the thief."

"I am the one who is asked, in Berlin, at intervals, whether the thief has been caught." He put his hand back on her wrist, and this time it was not a lid on a pot; it was a consideration. "It is not a Frenchman. That is what makes it interesting. A Frenchman sells what he can to whom he can and then he buys a restaurant. This one is different. This one is not selling. This one is giving, and to whom is a question with an unpleasant answer, because there is only one organisation in the west that is not selling anything, and it pays in other currencies."

She said, in the voice she had spent two years perfecting — warm, slow, a little foolish, with a shrug in it — "So it is a Communist."

"That is what the Sicherheitspolizei says. It is always what they say. It saves them a great deal of thinking."

"And what do you say?"

"That it is somebody who is very close to a great deal of paper." He said it in the way he said everything, as though the sentence had been typed. "And that if a woman came into my office and read my desk, I should not know. Which is why the desk is locked."

He waited, and she understood that she was being given an opportunity to laugh, so she laughed, and he took his hand off her wrist and put it on her hip, and the conversation was over.

Then he said, already half asleep, "The SD has given it a name. Their files are written by Romantics. They call it Fräulein."

"Fräulein?"

"It is a woman, according to a partial intercept, or a man who telephones like a woman, which is a joke the Sicherheitspolizei is not capable of making." He yawned, enormous and domestic. "Fräulein. Two years of stolen orders and a Frenchman's suicide at Fresnes and eleven arrests, and somebody in a cellar on the avenue Foch sat up one night and put Fräulein on the cover of the file. When we catch the person, I shall make a point of telling him. It is the kind of thing they write about afterwards."

She lay in the dark with her hands flat on the sheet beside her, in the posture she had learned from a girl from Lille, and she thought about the name.

Fräulein.

She thought about it the way you think about a coat you have been wearing in a photograph you did not know existed: it fits, it is hers, and there is nothing in the world to be done about it now except to go on wearing it and to make sure the photograph is never developed.

"What do you think?" he said.

"About what?"

"About the thief. Come. You have an opinion about everything. You have spent five months hiding your opinions from me in the way of a woman who has a great many of them."

She shifted onto her side to face him. In the four bars of street light he was looking at the ceiling, magnificent and ruined, a man who had been beautiful at twenty-five and had kept the bones.

"I think," she said, "that a woman would do it better."

"You think a woman would do better than a French Communist clerk?"

"A woman would do it much better." She put one finger on his chest, in the fine dusting of hair going grey, exactly where she knew he liked it. "You have always told me that we have no head for the long game. That we cannot keep accounts. That we weep at concerts."

"I have never said that at a concert."

"You said it at the Opéra."

"I would have been right at the Opéra." He was smiling in the dark. She could hear it in his voice, which had gone down the half-tone it went down before he gave her something she did not want: a name, a compliment, a piece of his history. "Very well. You would have done better than the clerk. What would you have done?"

"I would have stolen nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing at all. I would have listened. I would have lain in a bed like this one for two years and listened to a man talking about his brother's timber, and then I would have gone home at Christmas and said it all out loud to somebody in a kitchen, and no one would ever have found a single sheet of paper, and the Sicherheitspolizei would still be arresting the men who sell butter."

There was a silence of about three seconds, and in the middle of it she heard her own blood go past her ears, because the sentence had come out of her mouth whole and it was the nearest thing to the truth she had said in this house.

And then he laughed. It was a real laugh, a German laugh with the consonants falling off the end of it, and he pulled her over onto him with both hands and looked at her in the light from the shutter with an expression she had seen on him exactly twice before, and which she never once saw again.

"You are a treasure," he said. "You are my little lark."

She lay on his chest and let him stroke her hair and felt, in the middle of the satisfaction and the disgust that lived together in her chest the way they had since June, the entirely professional relief of a woman who has just been given a name she did not ask for and cannot refuse.

In the corridor, the hall clock said half past three.

"I must go," she said.

"It is the middle of the night."

"The car comes at half past six, and I have to be dressed, and your man looks at me."

"Rutz looks at nothing."

"Rutz writes it all down in a book, my dear."

He grunted, which was his way of agreeing with something he did not intend to do anything about, and she got out of the bed, and dressed in the dark, and found her gloves, and stood for a moment at the window with the four bars of street light on her hands.

By six she was on the pavement of the avenue Henri-Martin with her collar up, walking east towards the river, and the sky over Paris was the colour of a man who has been drinking all night. Rutz had held the gate for her and said *Fräulein Marion* in his careful way, and she had said *good morning, Emil*, which he liked, because she had one time asked him the name of his village and remembered it, and because the Germans are the easiest people in the world to be cruel to by being kind.

She walked twenty-five minutes to the rue Saint-Honoré. The streets were filling: the men on bicycles with the wooden tyres, the women in their queues outside the shops, the old man who had sold horse-meat from the same cart since before the war and had graduated in the course of the war to something worse. Two German soldiers came out of a bakery eating something hot and one of them looked at her and then away, and she felt, as she always did at that hour, exactly half alive: the night done, the delivery not yet made, and every fact she was carrying in her head sitting in the middle of her skull like a stone in a shoe.

The Polish Mission on the rue Saint-Honoré had a shabby door between a shop that sold notions and a shop that had sold books and now sold nothing. Inside, in the grey air of six forty in the morning, there were six or seven people at the early mass: three women in black, a man with one leg, a boy in a coat that had belonged to a bigger boy. She knelt in the third pew on the left, and the cold came up out of the stone into her knees, and she put her hands on the rail and waited.

The chaplain came along the side aisle at a quarter to seven, in a soutane with a scorch mark at the hem, and knelt down beside her as though he had come in for the same reason as everybody else. He was sixty and had the small, tidy hands of a man who had once taught history in Poznań. He did not look at her and she did not look at him, and they prayed side by side for three minutes, and then he said, in Latin, the words of the psalm.

She answered him in Polish, in a voice no louder than a breath, and she gave him everything in order, as it had been given to her: the sector of the works, the two batteries, the seventeenth and the eighteenth, the inspector from the Inspectorate and the delegation of the Todt organisation, the labour requests that were to go to the Feldkommandantur and not the Kreiskommando, the two westernmost. She gave it to him as a list, without verbs, the way she had been taught, because verbs are what a man under torture remembers.

He took it in without moving, and at the end of it he said one sentence.

"You are to have nothing to do with the Lubianka."

"What?"

"The Polish desk at the Lutetia has taken on a man from Warsaw." A silence, and then the priest's voice, dry as a ledger: "He is working the emigrants' lists. He is new, and he is good, and nobody in this city knows what he is. You have never met him. Avoid him."

"Who told you this?"

"The man who tells me things."

"And if I do meet him?"

"Then you have never met him," the priest said, and got up, and went away to the sacristy with his hands folded and his soutane dragging on the flags, and Krystyna stayed kneeling in the cold with her palms flat on the rail, thinking about a name on a file in a cellar on the avenue Foch, and a boy from Warsaw in the Hôtel Lutetia, and the fact that she had to be back in a bed on the avenue Henri-Martin at nine o'clock that evening, in a nightgown, in a room that smelled of saddle soap and cold tobacco, because the man she was going to lie down beside had just told her, with his hand on her hip, exactly what he was looking for.

Outside, it had begun to rain.

***

She had a room of her own in the ninth arrondissement, three flights up, let to a widow who believed her lodger was a Viennese widow who did something in millinery and who was away a great deal. Two rooms, a window on a courtyard, a stove that smoked, and a bath that had to be filled from a copper on the ring because the geyser had given up in the second winter of the occupation and nobody would come to mend it.

She filled it to the depth of four inches and got in, in water hot enough to raise the colour on her thighs, and began the second half of the night's work, which was the part nobody had trained her for, because it could not be trained.

She washed, methodically, in the order that had become a rite: hands, face, arms to the elbow, the whole of her chest, and then, with more soap, the parts of her that had belonged to somebody else since twenty past two that morning. She did not wash him off because he disgusted her. She had examined that, in the first month, honestly, in the way you examine a wound in order to know what to tell the doctor, and she had found no disgust in it at all, or very little, or not enough to matter, and the smallness of the quantity had frightened her more than a larger one would have done.

She washed him off because she had to be at a fitting in the rue de Miromesnil at eleven, and because the woman who would be at that fitting had to have skin that smelled of nothing in particular.

Afterwards she sat on the edge of the bath and pared her nails with a small pair of scissors and thought about the sheet of blotting paper. She held the whole of it in her head, right to left, the way it had gone in: the coastal sector works, the two batteries, the inspectorate, the Todt delegation, the labour requests, the horses of Nantes and the second paragraph only. She turned it over twice more and put it away in the part of her memory she had spent two years building, which had rooms in it, and in which the paper now lived in the left-hand drawer of a desk in a house on the avenue Kléber, next to a station timetable from the spring of 1942 and a photograph of a boy in a cavalry cadet's uniform, which was the only photograph she had ever memorised by mistake.

Then she took the pearls out of the drawer of the night table and put them round her neck, and looked at herself in the mirror of the wardrobe door.

He had given them to her in July, in a box lined with grey velvet, and he had told her the price, and the name of the jeweller on the rue de la Paix, and how the man had had them from a family that was no longer permitted to live in the eighth arrondissement. A man who gives a woman pearls on the occupation's money and tells her what they cost is not giving her jewellery. He is giving her a receipt, and he wants her to have it, because a thing with a price on it can be counted, and the Colonel's one great fear in the world, which sat behind everything he did, was of a thing he could not count.

She had worn them for four months. They were warm within five minutes and they made her neck look longer than it was, and when she wore them she was treated, by waiters and shopkeepers and German wives and once, memorably, by the wife of a general at the Ritz, as the possession she was pretending to be: an expensive object, kept indoors, of no opinions.

She looked at the pearls and then at her own face above them, and it happened again; it had been happening more often; it was the only thing about the work that had frightened her since the first month, when she had stopped being frightened of the rest.

She smiled.

It was a small, private, entirely genuine smile, of the kind she had met on the face of a man in a telephone exchange in Bordeaux in 1941 when he had been given exactly what he wanted, and it had been on her face before she knew it was there.

Not for the pearls. For the names. She had been in that house for half a year with a file on a shelf in a cellar on the avenue Foch that was hunting her and did not know her name, and she had stood in a study at three in the morning with her hands behind her back and read the Reich's business off a piece of blotting paper by the light of a street lamp, and the whole business — the cold corridor, the pipe that ticked, the hands with the cracked knuckles, the smell of lavender water — the whole business was, and she had promised herself in Warsaw that she would never use the word again but it came anyway, rising up through the soap and the water and the pearls like something in a thaw, *enjoyable*.

She put both hands flat on the washstand and made herself look at the face in the glass for a full minute, and it looked back at her: twenty-four years old, grey eyes, her mother's, a small white scar on the left wrist from a stirrup in a summer that was four years dead, and a mouth that was very good at saying yes.

Then she got dressed, and pinned her hair, and put on the coat she had worn to the fitting last week, and went out into the rain at eight o'clock in the morning to be fitted for a dress to wear at a German Christmas party, and on the stairs she took the pearls in her left hand, once, and squeezed them, and let them go.

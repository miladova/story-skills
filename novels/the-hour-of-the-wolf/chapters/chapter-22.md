# Chapter 22: The Canal

The hole in the floor of the cellar on the Kilińskiego Street was a metre square and it went down two metres and a half into a brick shaft with iron rungs in the wall, and at the bottom of the shaft there was a tunnel of brick, and the tunnel was the sewer of the city of Warsaw.

The first forty metres were the worst because they were the last forty metres of air. The air in the mouth of that passage was air that had been in a cellar in a burning district, which is a thin, hot, exhausted air with smoke in it and the smell of lime and wet plaster, and the passage it went into was a place where the air had no business to be at all; and the difference between the two was not a difference of degree, and every person who went down that hole in those three nights and came out at the other end has described the same four seconds: a smell that is not the smell of what people think a sewer smells of, which is filth, but the smell of something older and deeper and more complete, which is gas and water and the enormous wet body of a city, and a darkness that is not dark but is a substance and has a pressure to it and pushes on the eyeballs.

There was a guide at the bottom with a lamp and a rope.

The guide was a man of about forty called Wacek who had worked in the tunnels of the water service and the sewers of that city since 1928 and who had been brought across two nights before out of the Śródmieście for this and for nothing else; and the whole of his method, which was the method of the sewer-men of Warsaw in the summer of 1944, was this: the rope was tied every eleven metres to a ring or a rung or a bracket, because a straight rope in a passage with bends in it is a rope that kills people; the chain was hand on the rope with the left hand, right hand on the shoulder of the person in front; the rule was that nobody spoke, ever, for any reason, including to say that they were drowning; and the first twelve metres established the order, because the water in the first passage came to the knee and then to the waist, and a person who is going to be unable to do it is a person who is unable to do it in the first twelve metres.

Krystyna went down the rungs at eleven minutes past seven in the evening of the first of September 1944, with her coat left behind in the cellar on the Kilińskiego Street and her armband in her pocket and her papers with the priest, in a pair of trousers and a jacket that had belonged to a boy of seventeen who had been killed on the Miodowa Street; and the water took her at the third step down from the bottom of the shaft, and it was cold, and it was over the knee, and the rope was in her left hand, and the man in front of her was the captain with the wounded hand, and the woman behind her was the woman of fifty who had never gone down to a cellar in a bombardment, and behind her were thirty-eight more people, and in front of her, in the black, at the other end of four kilometres of the sewer of the city of Warsaw, there was a street in the Śródmieście with a lamp in it and a reception with blankets and hot tea made from something that was not tea.

***

A person in that passage has nothing to do for two hours, and the mind goes into the only room it has.

The map in her head — and there was a map in her head of the sewers of Warsaw, put there in eleven days by a man with a lamp who had drawn it in chalk on the floor of a cellar on the Kilińskiego Street for the four people who were going to lead the groups, and put there the way she had been trained to put in maps: by junctions, by turns, by the number of steps between the chain and the next shaft — was, in the passage, useless in the way a photograph is useless in a fog.

The passage was not a line on a map. It was a series of facts about a body: the floor rising and falling; the floor of the collector going from the soft slime where the water was still to the hard ribbed brick where it ran; the ceiling coming down so that a person of her height had to bend forward and hold the rope with the left hand and the shoulder of the man in front with the right; the shafts going up at intervals to the street, four metres up, where there was a slot of air and light in the daytime and where, at night, in the middle of a bombardment, there came down a thin chimney of noise — the flat crack of a rifle, the long roll of the artillery, a building coming down with a sound like a man's cough, and one shaft in the Saxon Garden where the air that came down had the smell of the burned grass and the roses of a garden above a sewer, which is not a smell that any living person should have to identify in the dark.

And she counted, because there was nothing else to do with the mind, and because counting is the thing that the trade had put into her that was worth keeping: the junctions, the turns, the rises, the steps, the distances between the rings on the rope; and after the first half-hour the counting became something else, and what she was counting, in the dark, with her left hand on the rope and her right hand on the shoulder of a man who was going to be dead in eleven days, was the eleven things she had ever said to her brother that she could not take back.

***

It went wrong at the junction under the Saxon Garden.

The passage came up into a chamber where four collectors met — a place about four metres wide and two and a half high with a stone shelf along one side, and the whole of the sewage of the north-west of the city coming through it at the speed of a man walking; and in the chamber the water was at the chest, and the air was the worst air in that four kilometres, because the junction did not draw, and because the gas that lies in such a place lies there in the way that a dog lies at a door.

They came into the chamber at the end of the first hour and eleven minutes in the following order: Wacek the guide with the lamp; the captain; eleven wounded who could walk; Krystyna; the woman of fifty; the two boys; and behind them the rest of the column, with the wounded man on the stretcher four groups back, because the stretcher could not be carried through the first passage at all and had to be got through the second on the shelf.

And halfway across the chamber, in the black, with the water at her chest, with the lamp of the guide already in the next passage and only the light coming back off the water, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna heard a voice out of the wall on her left, from the mouth of the second collector, which came into that chamber from the north-east, and the voice said, in Polish, in a whisper that was the volume of a shout under the circumstances, in the voice of a boy who had been sent by her father to a school on the Mokotowska and had once put a mouse in her bedroll at Wierzbów in September 1938:

"Krysia?"

***

There were nine of them in the first group, out of the nineteen who had gone in at nine in the morning: three men of the sabotage section with a lamp that had gone out and no matches, two stretchers with a wounded man on one of them who had died in the second passage at about eleven o'clock, two men holding the other end of the second stretcher, Tadeusz Stankiewicz, and a boy of about sixteen who was past speaking.

They had been in the passage for ten hours and twenty minutes. They had been turned back twice by a collector that had filled with debris, they had gone two hundred and forty metres up a wrong branch and come back, they had lost the rope twice, and for the last four hours they had been carrying a man who had clearly been dead since eleven o'clock, because there is a rule in a sewer and the rule is that a body is carried, and because the man who gave that rule out of his own mouth in the briefing of the first group was Wacek the guide, who had lost four friends in those tunnels since the first day of August, and who had said it in the cellar on the Kilińskiego Street: *You do not leave a man in the water. Take him out. If he is dead he is still a man, and I will not have Polish people floating in the sewer of this city.*

The two columns met in the chamber under the Saxon Garden and were sorted out by Wacek in four minutes with a rope, a lamp, and eleven words, because that is what he was for, and there was no embracing in it, and nobody in that passage asked anybody what had happened to them.

Tadeusz Stankiewicz stood in the chest-deep water with one hand on the rope and the other on the end of the stretcher, and looked at his sister in the light of a lamp that was going out, and said, in Polish, in a voice pitched for a passage with a hundred people in it, at a distance of about a metre, the following three sentences, which are the only three sentences that either of them ever said to the other about the whole of the five years:

"You will take the front end of this stretcher. Not the back. Your hands are better than his." And then: "The man on it is dead and we are carrying him out of this tunnel anyway, and I am going to do it because I told a man in a cellar that I would, and if I am killed doing it I want it known that it was a promise and not a fit of the Stankiewicz conscience." And then, in the same voice, at the same volume, with the water going past them both at the speed of a man walking: "You have had five years, Krysia. You are going to have to have the rest of it without me."

They carried the stretcher for forty-one minutes.

The second passage ran from the chamber under the Saxon Garden towards the south-west, and it was the lowest and the wettest of the four passages, and it was the one in which the water rose, because since half past eight in the evening the Germans had been pumping the whole of the volume of the river side of the network into that branch to flood the sewer crossings, which is a thing they had done to the city's tunnels before, and it meant that the level in that passage was rising while the column was in it; and for the last eleven minutes the man on the stretcher, who was dead, was above the water, because four people were holding him above the water — and the reason the four of them were holding him above the water was that Tadeusz Stankiewicz had been given the front end of a stretcher by a guide and had decided, in a tunnel, in the dark, that the promise to a man in a cellar had a price and that the price was going to be paid.

The thing that killed him was not the water and not a bullet and not a shell.

It was the pump.

At about twenty minutes past nine, in the passage between the second and the third shaft, the water came up the tunnel in a single surge of about eighteen inches in four seconds — the way it comes when a valve is opened somewhere behind a wall with a man standing at the handle — and the surge took the stretcher's back end round, and the two men at the back went under, and the front end came round with them, and Tadeusz Stankiewicz went down in the black water with the handles still in his two hands and the man who had been dead since eleven o'clock on top of him.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had her left hand on the rope and her right hand in the collar of her brother's jacket; and she held the rope and the collar in the dark while the water went past both of them at the speed of a man running, and the whole of what happened in those eleven seconds was four sentences, of which nobody else in the world heard three.

The first was hers, and it was his name.

The second was his, and it came up out of the water at the level of her ear, in a voice that was entirely the voice of a man giving an instruction to a subordinate about a matter of paper, and it was: "Let go. That is an order, and I have been giving you orders since you were four."

The third was hers, and it was the word *no*, said once, in Polish.

And the fourth was not a sentence and was not a sound that a person can spell; and at the end of the eleven seconds the current that had come up the passage under the pressure of a pump in a room in the Saxon Garden took the body of Tadeusz Stankiewicz out of his sister's right hand and into the black water and away down a branch of the sewer of the city of Warsaw that the guides did not use and that could be entered only by a person who was already dead, and the man of thirty-eight who had been on the stretcher went with him, and the two of them were found in March 1945, in the outfall on the river side, in a place where eleven other men and two women of the Rising were found in that spring, by men of the city's water service who had been told where to look by a guide called Wacek who had survived the crossing of the second of September.

***

The rest of the crossing took another hour and forty minutes and she is not able to remember most of it, and the parts she remembers are not the parts she would have chosen.

She remembers a child being carried on a man's shoulders through a passage in which the ceiling came down to a metre and a half, and the child's hands on the man's forehead, and the man bent double and taking the whole of the child's weight on his neck rather than let go of the rope.

She remembers the last three hundred metres, in which the passage widened out and the water went down to the knee and then to the ankle and then to nothing but slime, and in which the woman of fifty, who had come the whole of four kilometres with her hand on the shoulder of the person in front of her and had not made a sound, sat down in the passage and said, in Polish, in a small voice, "I am going to have a rest now," and had to be picked up and carried by two boys of fifteen and seventeen for the last two hundred metres with her arms round their necks, and has never in her life known to this day that she was carried.

She remembers the air, and where it came from: at about half past ten, in the fourth passage, a draught began to come along the tunnel from the far end, and the draught was cold, and it smelled of smoke and of the outside of a building and of a night in September, and the whole column, in the dark, in the water, with the wounded and the dead, made a sound that was not speech and not weeping: forty people in the sewer of the city of Warsaw breathing at once.

And she remembers the mouth of the canal on the Warecka Street, which is a thing she had been told about in a cellar and had understood, and which is not a thing that can be understood: a circular opening of brick about two and a half metres in diameter in the embankment of an open cutting, and beyond it, eleven metres away, a stone ledge, and beyond the ledge the street, and in the street, at eleven o'clock at night on the first of September 1944, in the middle of a bombardment of the district of the Śródmieście, there were lamps.

Electric light. Two lamps on a cable strung across the street, swaying in the wind of the shelling, burning in the middle of a city at war: the AK's people had run a cable from a working generator in a cellar on the Nowy Świat and had hung two bulbs over the mouth of the canal to bring two thousand people out of the ground, and to a person coming up out of four kilometres of black water with the sewage of the city on her hands and her brother in her mouth, those two lamps were the single most beautiful thing she saw in the entire war, and she stood under them at the top of the steps of the canal mouth and put her hand out and touched the bulb with two fingers, and it was hot, and she laughed, once, in a way that frightened two men standing next to her and that she could not stop for about eleven seconds.

And it was there, in the last hundred metres, in the open, in the light of those two lamps, that the German machine gun on the other side of the district, working up and down the Warecka Street from a window at a range of about six hundred metres, hit the column that came out of the canal behind hers.

***

Phillip Brandt came out of the canal mouth at twenty minutes past eleven at night on the first of September 1944, in a group of thirty-one people, in the second-to-last group to pass that night; and the first thing he saw in nine days of tunnels and twenty-seven days of the Rising was the face of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna in the light of two bulbs over the mouth of the sewer of the city of Warsaw; and the second thing was a burst of machine-gun fire that came over the ruins of the street and took the lamp off the wire and then went along the column on the steps.

He was hit twice: once through the top of the left shoulder, and once in the left thigh, four centimetres above the knee; and the first of the two was nothing and the second of the two was everything, because the round that went through his thigh went through the muscle and did not touch the bone and did not touch the artery, and that is what every doctor who ever looked at that leg has said, and it means only that the boy lived to be sixty-one and walked with a stick; on the night it meant a man on his back on the steps of the canal mouth at the top of a passage of sewage, bleeding in a way that cannot be described as moderate, with a woman's two hands on the wound, and eleven people in the street shouting and one lamp swinging on the wire.

They got him out of the cutting. It took four people and eleven minutes and one barrow, and the barrow is the detail that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna carried for the rest of her life: it was a barrow that had been left in the yard of the building on the Warecka Street by the boys of the AK, and it was the barrow of a man who had been in the vegetable trade before the war, and it had a sack over the bottom of it, and in that barrow Phillip Brandt was wheeled two hundred metres through the smoke of the Śródmieście on the first of September 1944 with his hand on the side of it and his head back and the two lamps going further away behind him.

The AK's people had a house on the Zielna whose cellars were a dressing station with eleven doctors in it, of whom nine were women, and the cellar had four tables and a hundred and forty wounded in eleven days and a bucket that had to be emptied by a boy of thirteen; and the woman who came to the barrow at the door of that cellar with a cigarette in the corner of her mouth and blood to both elbows was a doctor of about forty, and she looked at the leg for about eleven seconds in the light of a torch and said, in Polish, in the tone of a person who has four hundred of these to do before Friday: "Not the artery. Take him down. Suture and a drain, and if the gas comes, he loses it."

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, in a jacket that had belonged to a dead boy, with the sewage of the city of Warsaw on her hands and the whole of the passage and the surge of water and the four sentences still going round in her skull in the order in which she could not stop them, stood in the doorway of the dressing station with her hands at her sides and asked her question out loud, in Polish, in the hearing of the doctor and two orderlies and a woman with a list, and it is the only time in the entire war in which she asked anybody for anything in her own name:

"Will he live?"

And the doctor looked up at her, with the cigarette in her mouth and the torch in her hand, and looked at her for about a second and a half: at the sewage, at the wet, at the boy's coat, at the face; and then she looked down at the leg, and put her thumb on the artery above the wound the way a pharmacist puts a thumb on the scale pan, and felt it, and took the cigarette out of her mouth and said:

"You are carrying his papers in your pocket, I should think. Well. He will keep the leg, and the leg will keep him, and neither of them is going to be much use until Christmas, and in this city between now and Christmas there is going to be a very great deal more for him to be afraid of than that." She put the cigarette back. "Get out of the doorway, comrade. You are blocking the light for the man with the jaw."

***

The order came to her on the fourth of September, at eleven in the morning, in a room on the fourth floor of a building on the Świętokrzyska Street with two windows and no glass in them and a great deal of paper on the floor in bundles, and it was brought by a boy of fourteen with a satchel and it was in the form in which such things came that September, which is a quarter of a sheet of paper with a heading and four lines of instruction in a hand she had never seen: that the intelligence section of the Śródmieście was to be reorganised as of the fifth of September; that the person using the signature *Maria* — which was the pseudonym she had been given in the cellar on the Śliska Street in July and had used for one month and eleven days — was to report for duty with the counter-intelligence section of the staff, in the second office of the building on the Śródmieście, and was to bring with her the section's German translations and the file of the captured documents of the Old Town sector, and that she would be working in a room with a man called *Konrad*, who was a translator.

She read it twice, standing at the window of a building on the Świętokrzyska Street with the whole of the roof of the city burning to the north of her, and folded it and put it in her pocket and stood at that window for about four minutes.

And the reason she stood there is that a person in her trade learns to read an order twice, once for what it says and once for what it implies; and what the second reading said was this: that a staff in a besieged city had taken a decision on the third of September to put the only two German translators of the whole of the Polish rising into one room, four streets apart from a building on the aleja Szucha in which there was a chart with eight names on it and a column headed *language* and a question mark in a German hand against the name of an officer of a counter-intelligence section who had, on the first of August, ordered every post in his quarter to find a woman in a grey coat and bring her home; and that the same staff knew, because she had told them herself in a cellar in July and had written it in her own hand on a card, exactly who she was and exactly what was in the file that had been following her out of Paris since the fourteenth of January; and that the decision had been taken anyway, and that it was the correct decision, and that she was going to spend the last six weeks of the rising in a room with the man she loved, four streets from the hunter, with the whole of the city shrunk to eleven streets, and that there was no version of the two of them walking through that month in which the two of them were not going to be found.

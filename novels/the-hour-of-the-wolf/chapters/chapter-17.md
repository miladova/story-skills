# Chapter 17: The Twentieth of July

On the twentieth of July, at eight in the evening, the villa on the Frascati Street was at dinner with two officers of the administration and their wives, and the radio was on in the corner of the dining room, as it had been since the winter, playing the light music that the Reich broadcast into the Generalgouvernement at that hour, and it stopped in the middle of a waltz.

The silence of a radio is a thing that a room notices before it knows why; and then the voice came, and the voice was not the announcer of the light music but the voice of the Reich's own bulletin service, and it was reading a statement of the Führer's headquarters, and the statement said that a small clique of ambitious officers, having no connection with the front and no support in the army, had attempted to murder the Führer; that the attempt had failed; that the Führer was unhurt and would address the German people that night; and that the guilty would be punished with the severity that their crime deserved.

There was no sound in the dining room of the villa for about eleven seconds. Krystyna Stankiewiczówna sat at the table with a fork in her hand and watched four German officers of the Warsaw garrison hear the whole of the sentence *the German army has tried to kill the German head of state* for the first time, in a room with their wives and a Pole in a black dress standing at the sideboard with a tray; and what she watched was not shock. It was calculation: it came onto their faces one after another like a shadow coming across a field, and the calculation was exactly the calculation that every officer of the Wehrmacht in Europe made that night, from the commander of a battalion on the Vistula to the quartermaster of a depot in Norway, and it had three parts: *who was involved; who is going to be asked about this; and is my name in a paper.*

Von Hagen, at the head of the table, listened to the whole of it with his hands flat on the cloth and his eyes on the middle distance; and when it was over he reached over and turned the volume of the radio up himself, in front of his guests, and said, in the flat voice of a man continuing a staff meeting: "The Führer will speak at ten. There will be no more discussion of this at my table tonight, and there will be no discussion of it with anybody in the street tomorrow, and if any officer in this house is asked about the events of the twentieth of July in this city in any office, he will say what every other officer of the garrison will be saying, which is that he heard it on the radio and has no opinion. Helene, the wine."

The Führer spoke at ten. The whole of the house listened to it in the drawing room, on the wireless, with the servants in the doorway: the voice, and the silence after it, and then the men standing up and the women standing up and the small, terrible relaxation that comes after a broadcast in a country where the broadcasts come from the top.

And in the twelve days that followed, the German administration of Warsaw did what every German administration from Paris to Riga did in those twelve days, which was to reach into its own files and find out who was next to whom.

Phillip Brandt's chief — a Major of the Abwehr's station in the city, a heavy, careful, decent Westphalian of fifty-six who had been at a desk in Warsaw since 1941 and who had signed three of the documents that went into the parcel above the cobbler's shop — was called to the garrison command on the thirtieth of July at nine in the morning and taken to Berlin the following day, and he was not accused of anything: he was *transferred for political examination*, which is a phrase in the German language of that year which means that a man is removed from his post, his papers are read, his telephone is listened to, his friends are asked, and his family does not know where he is; and he was released in November and put on a transport to Breslau and died in an air raid in 1945, and nothing about him was ever proven, and none of it was ever taken back.

And in the same twelve days, a Hauptmann of the section called Kurt Lindemann went to the office of the Sicherheitspolizei on the aleja Szucha twice, in civilian clothes, in the evening, with a folder under his arm, and gave two of his colleagues to the SD in the form of a memorandum about their political unreliability, and the two colleagues were removed within the fortnight, and Lindemann was promoted and given the room next to Phillip's and a chair with a better back, and began, in a manner so gradual and so friendly that it was three weeks before Phillip could be certain of it, to read what was in Phillip's tray in the evenings after Phillip had gone home.

***

He came to the flat above the cobbler's on the twenty-fifth of July with a bag.

"There is a list," he said, "of one hundred and forty officers of the Abwehr's old establishment in the Government General who are to be re-examined for political reliability. A hundred and forty: nine are from this city. My name is on page four. Nobody has accused me of anything; I am not under arrest; the paper says *for examination*, and the examination means a man in a room with my file and my family and my wife's — my mother's family, my father's old application for party membership, which I have told you about, and the fact that I speak Polish and was born in Warsaw, which in 1944 is a fact about an officer of German counter-intelligence that a man in a room in the aleja Szucha will find interesting." He put the bag down and looked at her. "And on the paper, in the third column, there is a note: *hearing, 4 August, Szucha, Section IV.*"

She stood with her back to the window and looked at the bag, and at him, and at the whole of the arithmetic in the room, and the arithmetic came out as it always did.

"You cannot go to that hearing."

"No."

"Because a man who goes into that room on that paper comes out with a file, and the file goes into a system, and the system is going to be in Berlin in September and in Warsaw in August; and the day the system reads your file after the arrest of your chief, you will be taken, and everything you have from me is then one long corridor with four doors in it and a man at the end with a typewriter."

"No," he said. "So I am not going to the office. As of tonight I am a Hauptmann of the Abwehr who has not reported for duty, which in the language of the German army is a deserter, and as of tomorrow morning there will be a paper in the garrison command with my name on it in a different column from the one I am on tonight. And I shall not be in the office when the paper is made." He sat down on the chair at the table with his hands on his knees. "That is what you came here for, in the end, and I want it said out loud in this room, because it is the only way I can make it true: the arrangement you have been running for four months needs a man inside the garrison command in the last week of July. To keep being that man, I have to go to a room on the aleja Szucha on the fourth of August and be re-examined; and the examination will take eleven days and will end with my file in a system; and in the last week of the war I will be either in a cell or on a list, and no use to you at all. If I do not go, I become a deserter tonight, and I am free of every file in the German army, and I can work from now until the end of this without a signature; and everything I can reach from inside a building I lose, which is the whole of the bridges' paperwork and the evacuation orders and the four copies." He looked up. "So the choice is between being a source who is a soldier, for four weeks, until they read the file; and being a man with no rank and no desk and no papers, who can only give you what he already has in his head and what can be got out of buildings at night."

"There is a third thing," she said.

"There is a third thing, and it is the reason I am sitting here with the bag, and the reason is that if I stay in the uniform after the twentieth of July, I am also a German officer in a city that is about to be fought over, and the woman who runs me is a Pole in the Polish underground, and my mother lives in Wola in a flat with a window; and every German in this city is going to have his name and his family in a card index before the summer is out, and the card that matters is not the one about the bridges. It is the one about my mother." He opened the bag and took out a folded paper, which was the establishment of the counter-intelligence section of the garrison command of the first of July, with the names of eleven officers. "I made a present to you of my access in April, and you have had everything I could reach, and in the last four weeks you have had four documents out of my room through the window in the morning. I am not going to be able to do that again after the twenty-fifth of August, because after the twenty-fifth of August this city will be a fortress with an entrance and an exit, and I shall be either inside it or outside it." He put the paper back in the bag. "So I want you to take me to the people who made the card with the name on it, and I want them to send me where I can be useful, which is outside the uniform and inside the city, and I want that arranged in the next three days."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You are asking me to take a German officer to a place where a German officer is a target."

"I am asking you to take a man who has been a German officer since 1939 to be discussed by four Poles in a kitchen," Phillip said, "and I am telling you, in this room, that I know exactly what the arithmetic is: they will look at me, and they will decide whether to put me to work or to put me in a door, and I am asking you to arrange the conversation, and I am not asking for a promise. If they decide on the door, they will decide it as an army decides things, and the paper they will write will be in a drawer in a cellar, and I shall be in a different drawer, and in fifty years somebody will read both of them and will know that everybody in the room was right." He looked at the photograph of two children at a well on the wall. "I am tired of translating. Arrange the conversation."

***

The summons came on paper, in a car, on the twenty-sixth of July at nine in the morning, at the gate of the villa on the Frascati Street, and it was addressed to *Fräulein Marion Herschlag, Austrian national, employed in the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison command*; and it was not an arrest. It was a request, in German, in the correct form, signed by a Sturmbannführer of the Sicherheitspolizei, inviting her to attend at the office on the aleja Szucha 25 at eleven o'clock on the twenty-sixth of July in connection with a file, with the assurance that the interview would be conducted in the form of an administrative clarification; and the car had been sent for her, with a driver and a soldier, and Rutz was not with it.

Von Hagen read the paper at the breakfast table, twice, and put it down and said, "That is the man from the terrace in May. Schleier? No — Kalbach. He has no right to summon a member of my staff without my approval, and he has not got my approval, and I shall have a word with his chief on the telephone this morning; and you will go, my dear, because if you do not go, in that building they will write the word *refused* in a file, and a refusal in a file is a brick, and a wall of bricks is how these people work." He folded the paper and handed it to her. "You will go, and you will say nothing at all except what is asked in the fewest possible words, and you will not answer a question that is not a question, and you will be back by three. And if anybody in that building is discourteous to you, I shall hear about it, and there will be consequences in a room with a carpet."

The room was on the third floor, and it had the window on the lindens, closed and latched; and it had a table with a cloth on it, and on the table a tray with tea and a plate of biscuits, and at the table, in his civilian overcoat with the collar unbuttoned, a man of thirty-eight with soft spectacles that had been repaired at the frame; and in the corners there was a typist with a pad on her knee, and a young officer of the SD in field grey with a polish on his collar who was introduced as an interpreter, and who spoke Polish, German and enough French to be a menace, and who spent the whole of the interview sitting on a chair at the side of the room with his hands on his knees and his eyes down.

Kalbach stood up when she came in and bowed, and asked after the Oberst's health, and asked whether the tea was too strong; and then he sat down and said, without any change in his voice at all: "Fräulein Herschlag. This will take about three hours. Everything you say is being written down by the young woman in the corner, and at the end of it you will read it and sign it, and if you do not agree with a sentence you may say so and the sentence will be struck out and initialled. I am telling you this at the start so that you know the form; there is no other reason. I am going to ask you about a great many things, some of which will seem to have nothing to do with each other. That is how I work."

"I understand."

"Then the first: the pearls."

"I beg your pardon?"

"On the twenty-sixth of April a strand of pearls was sold on the Marszałkowska Street by a woman in a grey coat with an Austrian accent, and in the same week a strand of imitations of the same length and pattern was bought at the Kercelak market by a woman in the same coat with the same accent, and the inventory of the household effects of the villa on the Frascati Street of June 1940 contains, at number 44, a necklace of pearls in the possession of the Oberst's household, which was a gift from the Oberst to the lady of the house in December 1943. I have all of that on paper, in German, in this room." He passed his fingers over the tablecloth, smoothing nothing. "What can you tell me about the pearls?"

"The pearls were given to me by the Oberst in Paris on the twenty-third of December 1943," she said, "and I wear them, and they are at this moment in the wardrobe of my room in the villa, in a box, and any officer of the section may see them at any hour; and if somebody in this city has sold a strand of pearls to a jeweller, it was not I, and if the jeweller has described a woman to you, then the woman is not Marion Herschlag, and you may have her hat."

"And the imitations at the Kercelak?"

"There are nine thousand women in this city who wear imitations," she said. "There is a market for them because there is a war."

"Precisely," said Kalbach, and made a small note, which was not a note, because he was watching her hands while she took the cup and set it down; and that was the whole of the pearls, in the record, for four months, and it was the pearl that killed her in the end, and it did it in this room, on this day, in the first four minutes.

The interview went on for two hours and forty minutes.

He asked her about her mother's name, and she gave it — *Leopoldine Herschlag, née Reiner, of Graz, deceased 1936* — in the tone of a woman repeating a fact about her childhood, and the whole of it took her a quarter of a second, because the file in the villa had it and she had known the file, and the quarter of a second, later, on a page in his notebook, became the second nail.

He asked her about Lausanne, and about the school, and about the woman from Bern who had taught her French, and she gave him the school and the street and the name and the woman, and answered every question twice, once with detail and once with a refusal to invent a detail she did not have, which is the hardest thing in the trade and which she did for two hours without one mistake.

He asked her about Vienna in March 1938, and she said that she had been in Lausanne, at the school, in the spring term of 1938, and that she had heard about the events of that month on the wireless in the school's refectory and had cried in a corridor, and that this was the whole of her experience of the events of March 1938, and that she could remember the corridor and the wireless and the crying and nothing else at all; and Kalbach listened to this answer with his hands folded and his spectacles on the bridge of his nose and thought about it for eleven seconds, because the answer had the one property which no rehearsed answer has and which no liar can fake, which is that it was a door being closed on a thing a person does not want to talk about; and he could not tell, and he never did tell, for the rest of his life, whether the woman in front of him was a liar with a copybook or a Viennese woman with an unhappy March and a well-prepared dossier, and the reason he could not tell was that they were the same answer.

He asked her about the department store on the Mariahilfer Strasse, and about the floor, and the department, and the manager's name, and whether the gloves were sold on the first floor or the second (the third, she said; the gloves were on the third, with the stockings, because the shop had been rearranged in 1937 and it is the sort of detail a shop girl remembers and a person who reads files does not); and then, at the end of the second hour, he put a piece of paper on the table and pushed it across.

"Whose handwriting is this?"

It was a sheet of the household book of the villa on the Frascati Street: the ordinary list of a house in 1944 — coal, eggs, a repair to the stove, the name of a chemist — written in her small upright hand, which she had written in that book every Monday for four months because Janina of the village near Sandomierz had never learned to write German; and underneath it, on the same sheet, in a different ink, was a typewritten line and a rubber-stamp impression of a date, from a document that had come out of the drawer of a German office in the Government General.

"This is the household book of the Oberst's house. The handwritten part is yours — it was established in five minutes by a clerk who has worked in that villa for a year, and there is no question about it. The stamp underneath is from a document which reached this office in May, submitted through your intervention to the Oberst's section by a lady of the household in the course of an enquiry, and which I am obliged to tell you has been examined by the forgery section of this building and is a fabrication of the Polish underground, produced in a workshop in this city, and the typeface of its stamp is the same typeface as a fabrication that this office took from a courier in the Rakowiecka Street in 1942." He looked at her over the top of his spectacles. "So the question is not who wrote the household book. The question is who wrote the stamp."

"I did not write the stamp," she said. "I gave the Oberst a document that came to me from a woman in the Building Section of the garrison administration, whose name and office I gave him in writing on the day, and I did not make the document and I did not examine it, and if it is a forgery then the Oberst's section and the forgery section of this building and the lady in the Building Section have all of it, and none of it is mine."

"Which is exactly what a woman would say," Kalbach said, "if she was either innocent or trained," and smiled, and closed his notebook, and let her go.

***

She walked out of the building on the aleja Szucha 25 at twenty to three with her bag on her arm and her hat on and her face doing nothing at all, and past the courtyard with the lindens and the cars, and past the sentry at the gate who saluted her; and she got into the car that the Sicherheitspolizei had provided, with a driver, and was driven home along the Ujazdowskie, and answered the driver's remark about the weather; and she went into the villa and up the stairs to her room and shut the door and took her hat off slowly, and stood in the middle of her own room for about four seconds, and then went into the bathroom and was sick, twice, without any noise, with the tap running.

Three things had saved her and none of them was innocence.

The first was the dossier: nine months of preparation by professionals in two cities, an identity that had been built in Paris in 1940 with a birth certificate from a parish register and a school record and a dead woman's school friend and four thousand francs of paper, which had withstood an examination by the Sicherheitspolizei of the Reich in a room with a typist and an interpreter for two hours and forty minutes.

The second was her hands: the sign she had been given, in the nine weeks in the flat above the laundry, of the person who is lying, is not the voice and not the eyes but the hands — a woman's hands will always go to the thing she is protecting, or to her own throat, or to the seam of her skirt, at the moment of the lie — and her hands had lain in her lap in that room for the whole of the interview, on her bag, still, and she had done it by a technique she had learned in a corridor in Paris in 1942 with a stopwatch, and the technique had cost four days of her life, and it had saved her whole life in a room on the aleja Szucha.

And the third was that Kalbach had made a mistake.

He had brought her in before the chart was complete. He had a word, an accent, two strands of beads, and a rubber stamp; and none of it was proof; and the whole of the material had been laid in front of her in a room with a teacup, and the man had watched her with patient, exact, terrible attention, and had asked her, at the end, the question that no file can answer — *who wrote the stamp* — and had let her go, because he could not hold her, and because a man who is certain and early is a man who has to spend the next four weeks watching a house on the Frascati Street with two men on bicycles in a city that is about to be fought over.

She washed her face; and then she sat on the edge of the bath for a moment with her hands flat on her knees, and did the thing she had learned from Wiktor Janowski in a flat above a laundry: she took the hour apart and put it back together, and wrote, in her head, in Polish, in a list, the six things she would now do, and did the first of them within four minutes, which was to go downstairs and tell the Oberst, over the tea tray, in a bored voice, that the man from the Sicherheitspolizei had asked her about a stamp on a forged document and had been too polite to say that he suspected her of making it, and that she had given him the name of the woman in the Building Section, and that she wished to be told whether she was to keep working in that section or whether she was now a person about whom the Reichssicherheitshauptamt had a note; and von Hagen, who had already spent the morning on the telephone to Kalbach's chief about the discourtesy of the summons, was incandescent; and by the end of that conversation Marion Herschlag was, in the mind of the man who owned her, not a suspect but a wronged lady, and the officer of the SD who had summoned her was a clerk with a grudge; and that was the sixth thing in her list and the most important, and she had got it done in eleven minutes, and it was, on the whole, the finest piece of work she did in the entire war.

***

The city's temperature, in those same weeks, was in the streets and not in the files.

On the twenty-seventh of July, at half past nine in the evening, in a tram on the Marszałkowska Street, a group of the Kedyw shot a German officer of the garrison in the back of the head and took his pistol and his papers, and went away through the tram; and the tram stopped with a dead German in it in the middle of the street, and the passengers got out and walked, and four hundred people on the pavements went on about their business and nobody had seen anything.

And on the next day, the twenty-eighth of July, the Germans took a hundred and twenty men out of the Pawiak Prison and shot them in the ruins of the ghetto, and put the notice up: the date, the district, the number of persons, and the names in two columns, in Polish and German, by way of reprisal for the shootings of German soldiers, and the responsibility lying with those who had done the murders; and the population of Warsaw read the notice in the ordinary way, standing at the kiosks in the heat, four and five at a time, and the queue at the window of the Pawiak on the Dzielna Street was three hundred women long, and it went round the corner into the Nowolipki, and the women in it had been standing there since six in the morning with their parcels, and the parcels were handed back over the counter at eleven, and there was nothing in the notice about any of them.

***

Phillip went home to Wola on the evening of the twenty-eighth of July, and she went with him as far as the corner of the street, which was against every rule that four people had written in a ledger, and stood at the corner by a wall for eleven minutes while he was inside the building.

She saw the courtyard: the gate, the pump, the coal cellar, the shed; two women at the shed, one with a bucket; three children on the stairs; a man asleep on a chair at a window on the ground floor. She saw the window on the third floor of the corner building with the light on in it, and in the light, after a while, two people standing: a woman of about fifty-eight with grey hair, in an apron, standing very straight, and a man of sixty-one with a leather satchel still on the chair behind him; and between them, in the window, from a distance of forty metres in the dusk, she saw the shape of what happened when somebody tells his mother something she already knew.

The woman stood in the window for about four minutes without moving. Then she went out of the light, and the man with the satchel went out of the light after her, and the window stayed lit and empty; and Phillip Brandt came out of the gate of the courtyard four minutes later and walked to the corner where she was standing with her hands in her pockets, and said, in Polish, in a completely level voice: "It is done. My mother has the money in a tin and my father has not said a word since Tuesday, and neither of them has asked me anything, and my father stood up when I came in and shook my hand, which is a thing he has done four times in my life, and then he went into the other room and shut the door." He looked down the street. "I have four days' clothes in a bag at the flat above the cobbler's and the key of the flat on the Zielna, and I have been told that the AK have a woman on the Wola side who does papers. I am going to be dead in about six weeks, and I have never in my life been as calm as I am tonight, and I would like to walk you to the tram and be a man who walks a woman to a tram."

***

Von Hagen came home on the last night of July at eleven with the smell of schnapps on his breath and his tunic unbuttoned and his hair down over his forehead, and he went into the study and sat in the dark, in the chair, without turning on the lamp, and stayed there for twenty minutes; and when she came to the door with her dressing gown on and asked him whether he wanted anything, he said, "Sit down. There is a thing I have not told you, and I am going to tell it to you now, in this room, which is the only room in this city where I have ever said anything true, because the walls of my office have four ears and each of them is attached to a man."

She sat down on the other chair, in the dark, in the smell of the schnapps and the leather and the cold ash of the study, in a house on the Frascati Street, four streets from the river.

"They have asked me to prepare a plan," said von Hagen. "Not the plan for the garrison; the plan for the city. What is to be held, what is to be given up, what is to be destroyed, and the order in which the destruction is to be carried out if the decision is taken. The bridges. The waterworks. The power station on the Wybrzeże. The telephone exchange. The gasworks. The railway junctions — the Gdańsk station and the Praga and the four turnings of the Vistula bank. I have been working on it for four days and I shall be working on it for another four, and I am to report it to the garrison's chief of staff on the third of August, and there are four copies in this city, and one of them will be in that safe at the end of this week." He turned his head towards her; she could see the shape of him against the grey of the blackout curtain. "I have been in this service for twenty-two years and I have read every plan that was made for the East, and there is one thing that all of them have in common, which is that none of them was ever carried out in the order in which it was written; but this city is going to be fought over in the streets, my dear, and I have been told to make the arithmetic of it, and I intend to be the officer who makes the Warsaw question an accounting problem that can be closed." He paused. "And I want you to stay with me through August. Whatever happens. If it comes, and if the whole thing comes apart, you will be in this house with a sealed pass in your bag, and if the house is in the middle of it, then we shall go out of it together or not at all. Will you stay?"

"Yes," she said, in the dark, in the smell of the schnapps, with the whole of what she knew in her mouth like paper.

And von Hagen reached out in the dark and found her wrist and held it for a moment, not in the way of a man who owns something, but in the way of a man who has, at the age of fifty-four, in the fifth year of a war he has been losing for three of them, discovered that the only person in the world he can talk to is a woman who has been paid to sit in the chair; and the terrible thing about it, the thing that she took to the end of her life and never once put down, was that she saw it, whole, in the dark, in the second before he let go of her wrist: he was not a fool and he was not a monster and he was the man who had slapped a Polish servant across the room in June for a cold plate, and he was lonely, and the loneliness was the last and the most dangerous thing about him, because a lonely man will tell the truth to the person he has decided to trust, and there was no version of August in which that did not end with one of them dead.

# Chapter 24: Water and Darkness

Phillip Brandt spent the first eleven days of September 1944 in a cellar under a house on the Zielna Street in the middle of Warsaw, lying on a mattress of straw and a Polish army greatcoat with a hole in his left thigh and a fever of about thirty-nine degrees, and he learned afterwards — from a woman who had been in that cellar and who told him in 1946 in a kitchen in another country, with a plate of soup between them — that the whole of what he said in that time was said in three languages and that the three languages were interleaved in a way that nobody in that cellar could follow, and that he had said the word *Mutter* eleven times and a number in German and a poem of Racine's, and that on the fourth day he had sat up with his eyes open and recited, in Polish, the whole of a Latin conjugation table in the order in which a boy of nine learns it in a schoolroom in Warsaw, and had lain down again and had not been able to remember any of it afterwards.

The wound was not serious, and the wound was entirely serious, and the two things were true at the same time in the way that they were true of everything in that city in that month.

The bullet had gone through the muscle of the thigh four centimetres above the knee and had not touched the bone and had not touched the artery, and the doctor of about forty with the cigarette in her mouth — her name was Zofia Wierzbicka and she had been a surgeon in the hospital of the Child Jesus before the war and she was going to be a professor in Kraków in 1955 and she was going to die of a stroke in 1971 in a flat with a balcony — had cut down into the wound on the second day with a scalpel and a pair of forceps and no anaesthetic at all and had taken out of it a piece of woollen cloth and a fragment of the copper jacket of the round, and had then poured into it the contents of a bottle of something that was not quite iodine, and had said, over the noise the patient was making: "You are a German, yes? Good. Then you will have been taught to hold still."

There was no sulphonamide in that cellar and there was no penicillin in the whole of that city and there was no question of a hospital, and the whole of the treatment of that wound for the eight weeks it took to close was: cleanliness, and air, and the fact that the man on the next mattress, who was a sergeant of the AK's sabotage section with a wound in the stomach that everybody in the cellar knew was going to kill him, spent the whole of the first four days in washing his neighbour's leg with boiled water that his mother brought in a bottle from a court two streets away, because a man with a stomach wound and three days to live had decided, on the first afternoon, that he would spend his time on the German.

That member of the AK died on the fourth of September at about six in the evening, having asked two of the boys to prop him up so that he could see the doorway of the cellar, and having said, in the last hour, in a very ordinary voice, that he had a wife in a village near Siedlce and that she was to be told that he had died of the shell, and that she was not to be told anything else, because she was a woman who could keep a fact and not a story.

And on the fifth of September, in the morning, his mattress was taken out of that cellar into the yard of the house, and two men dug a hole and put him in it, and the woman who owned the house, who was sixty-four years old and who had been in that cellar every day of the rising in an apron and had never once gone up to her own flat on the second floor, said, at the side of the hole, in Polish, the only sentence that was said over him, which is also the only sentence that was said over most of the people buried in that city in that year: "He is ours. Do not step on him when you go back in."

***

He began working on the eleventh of September, on crutches, in the cellar of the printing house on the Świętokrzyska Street, and the work was the work he had asked for in a corridor in the Warsaw rising on the eighth of August: German paper.

The material came into that cellar in four ways. It came off the dead, which is the largest single category in a rising: the paybooks, the letters, the identity cards, the orders in the pockets of men who had been killed in the streets, and the maps, above all the maps, which come off the dead with the folds in them still, so that a man who wants to know what the enemy has been looking at has only to open the paper at the fold.

It came out of the captured trucks and the armoured cars and the two staff cars of the first week, which had yielded a box of railway movement tables, eleven cans of film, three million cigarettes, and a bundle of the administrative orders of the German garrison of the city, of which nine were of real value and one — the file of the food supply of the German units in the northern sector — was, properly read, a document of the order of battle, because the German army of 1944 fed itself by the unit, and the units in a district can be counted in a warehouse list.

It came out of the prisoners, and in this way it also came out of a cellar in the Old Town where a boy of twenty-two from a signals company in East Prussia had told a woman who spoke his language where the artillery got its orders; and the paper of that interrogation had come out through the canal on the second of September with everything else, in a satchel, on the back of a priest.

And it came from him.

That is the third and the most useful of the four sources, and it is the reason the AK's staff kept a German officer with a wounded leg in a cellar under a printing house and gave him boiled water and bread and the occasional egg: because a man who had worked for four years in the offices of the German administration of that city and had read the paper of four of those offices for four months of 1944 had a certain amount of it inside his head; and on the twelfth of September, on the back of a Home Army message form, with a pencil, sitting on the floor of that cellar with his leg straight out in front of him, Phillip Brandt drew for them what he knew of the dispositions of the German units in the southern sector of the city of Warsaw in the last week of July 1944, and what he knew was not a guess: it was the establishments, the quarters, the strengths, the location of the two ammunition depots, the command posts in the three schools, the number of the police battalions, the fact that the artillery park of the garrison had been in the courtyard of the building on the Bagatela, and the fact — which the AK did not have and which cost three hundred men and saved two companies — that the two heavy batteries in the Mokotów field had been emplaced in the first week of August and had no overhead cover of any kind.

Krupa read the sheet on the fourteenth and said nothing to him about it, which was his way; and on the fifteenth of September, two men came into the cellar on the Świętokrzyska Street with a typewriter that had to be carried between cellars in a sack and a box of paper of the German type and sat down at a table, and typed out, in duplicate, one card.

The card said, in Polish:

*STANISŁAW KONRAD (pseudonym). Born 1917, Warsaw. Civilian. Employed in the intelligence section of the Śródmieście, from the second of August 1944. Transferred to the counter-intelligence section of the staff from the fifth of September 1944. No political affiliations. Confirmed by the officer commanding the counter-intelligence section. The person is under the protection of the Polish Home Army and is to be given the assistance of all units.*

And the second copy of it went into a tin box with eleven other copies and the box was buried on the twentieth of September in the cellar of a building on the Krucza Street under a floor of brick by two men who did not read the papers they buried, and the first copy stayed in the file of the counter-intelligence section, in a canvas satchel, which was carried by a courier from cellar to cellar for the remaining seventeen days of the rising; and that card — that piece of paper, with a false name on it and no photograph and a line about the protection of the Home Army — is the reason that Phillip Brandt walked out of the ruins of the city of Warsaw in the autumn of 1944 as a Polish civilian with an injury and not as a German officer in a column of prisoners, and is the reason he was alive in 1945, and in 1946, and in the whole of the rest of the century, and he kept a copy of the wording of it in his memory to the end of his life and could recite it, in Polish, at eighty years of age, on a bench, in a country he had not chosen.

***

In the office on the aleja Szucha, in the same weeks, in a building whose windows were closed because the lindens outside had been cut down for firewood by a company of the German engineers, the case that had begun on the avenue Foch in Paris on the fourteenth of January 1944 came to its end, and the end came not out of a stroke of genius but out of the ordinary business of a police office in a besieged city, which is the taking of prisoners and the reading of paper.

Kalbach had spent the rising doing what a counter-intelligence section in a city under military assault is for, and what that is, in practice, is this: it interrogates the prisoners, of whom there were by the middle of September about eleven thousand, and it sorts the paper taken out of the houses of the Poles, of which there were two hundred and forty crates by the nineteenth of September, and it hunts, in that paper, for names, because a name in a rising is a weapon, since every name in the files of the underground is a name that has a street and a mother attached to it.

The rising gave him four things.

The first was a woman.

She had been taken in a cellar on the Śródmieście on the fourteenth of August with a hundred and ten others and sent to the transit camp at Pruszków and from there released, because she was sixty-one years old and had no son in the fighting and had a card of the German labour office of 1941; and she had come back into the city on the twenty-second of August with a food card, and she had been pointed out to the authorities of the district three weeks later by a neighbour, in the ordinary way, as a person who had hidden an officer; and she was interrogated on the eighteenth of September for four hours by a young SS officer and one hour by Kalbach, and what she gave him, in the fourth hour, in tears, in the course of a story about a dressmaker, was the following sentence: that she had worked for eleven years in the school of the Sisters of the Sacred Heart on the Mokotowska Street as the woman who did the linen, and that there had been a girl in that school, a girl of the family of the former owners of the palace on the Nowy Świat, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, whom she had measured for a uniform in 1934 and who had grey eyes and a mole on the left side of her neck and had been reported dead in France of typhus in 1940 and had not died, because she had seen her.

She had seen her in the church of the Holy Cross on the eighteenth of August 1944, in the middle of the rising, at the office at six in the morning, standing in a queue of people waiting for the sacrament with an armband on her arm.

That was the first thing.

The second thing was the flat.

The third thing was a piece of paper out of a pocket of a dead Polish officer in the ruins of the Wola: a leaf of a notebook of the Polish underground's counter-intelligence section, with four words on it in a handwriting that Kalbach had never seen, which were the address of a cellar on the Śliska Street, the word *ledger*, and the date of the eleventh of July 1944.

And the fourth thing was the woman's name, which he now had in three different forms and two different languages and from four different sources, and which he wrote on the first page of his notebook on the eighteenth of September 1944, in his small hand, in a room with the window closed, and underneath the name he wrote, in the three lines that every police officer in the history of the world has written at that point in an investigation, the following: *the family: father, Jerzy, 69, wounded in the leg on 2 August, in the cellar of the building on the Mokotowska Street; sister: Hanna, 17, with the father; the flat: second floor, front, empty since the second of August. The woman's own movements since 1 August are unknown and there is no record of her in any of the districts under our control, and the reason for that is that she is inside the Polish lines and has been since the first days.*

He read the three lines twice, and then he drew, on the same page, in the way in which he had drawn a chart of eight names in a hotel room on the Krucza Street in May, a small table of four columns and eleven rows, and filled it in over the following four days, and the table is the last piece of his method that the world has, because the notebook was taken out of his pocket on the twenty-seventh of September by a man with a fever and a rifle and is in the archives of the Polish state in Warsaw.

***

The search of the flat on the Mokotowska Street was on the ninth of September 1944, and there were eleven men in it and one of them was Kalbach, and the whole of it took four hours and twenty minutes and it was done in the correct manner: the drawing of the plan of the flat, the inventory of the furniture and the effects, the photographs of the two rooms, the sealing of the door afterwards with a paper of the Sicherheitspolizei, and the removal of eleven objects, which were listed in the inventory in a German hand in the following order.

One: the photograph of a man of about sixty with a white moustache and a woman of about fifty, in a frame, of the year 1930.

Two: a Latin textbook of the edition of 1908 with a name written in the front, *Jerzy Stankiewicz*, and, underneath, in another hand that was a boy's, the same name with the date 1921.

Three: a book of the poems of a Polish poet of the nineteenth century with four pages turned down and one line underlined in pencil.

Four: eleven letters of the year 1939 in a bundle tied with string.

Five: a child's skipping rope.

Six: a woman's glove of 1936, of grey kid, in tissue paper, in the drawer of a chest in the back room.

Seven: a second glove, matching.

Eight: a photograph of four people on a lawn in front of a house with a portico, taken in the summer of 1938, of which the negative does not exist and which was, in 1946, the only surviving picture of the Stankiewicz family of Wierzbów.

Nine: a certificate of the mortgage of an estate in Mazovia held by a bank in Poznań, dated the third of July 1935, stamped, signed.

Ten: a box of guitar strings.

Eleven: a ring.

It was a silver signet with a flat face, and the face had on it the head of a greyhound above three willow leaves, and it had been found inside the left glove of the pair, in tissue paper, in the drawer of the chest in the back room, where a woman had put it on the thirty-first of July 1944 with her hand flat on the drawer for a second and a half and had said out loud, in Polish, *I am not going to be found with it*; and in the inventory it was written down in the German manner, with two words, as *ein Siegelring, Silber* — a signet ring, silver — and put in an envelope with the other objects, and the envelope was put in the case, and the case was on the desk of Kalbach's office in the building on the aleja Szucha when the Polish lines in the Mokotów district surrendered to the German command at eight in the evening of the twenty-seventh of September 1944, which is the day before that district's soldiers went into captivity and the day before the last of the rising's four districts stopped fighting.

***

The cellar was in the Mokotów district, under a house on the Madalińskiego Street, and it was four metres by seven, and it had a vault of brick, a well in the corner of it that gave a litre of water an hour and a smell of the 1890s, eleven people, a table, a lamp made of a bottle and a bootlace and a wick, a bag of rye, two buckets, a rifle of the Polish army of 1938 with eleven rounds, and, on the eighth of September, a woman with a bandaged left forearm who had come through three streets of shelling with a canvas satchel: Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, who had been hit in the arm on the sixth of September by a piece of the coping of a house on the Puławska Street and had had it dressed by a nurse in a cellar with the last of a bottle of spirit, and who had spent the last two days in the Mokotów living the life of the eleven thousand people who were still in that district, which is: to lie in a cellar, to go up for an hour a day for water, to learn to know the sound of every German mortar, and to wait for somebody to come and take the district or else to come and save it.

The Mokotów district of Warsaw held out for eleven days after the last of the Polish lines on the north had gone, and the sequence of those days is history that is written down in four countries, and the part of it that matters here is what happened in the cellar on the Madalińskiego Street on the twenty-sixth of September 1944 at about four in the afternoon of a grey day, which is: a German came down the stairs with a torch and a pistol and a white flag furled around his left arm.

He came down alone, and he came down slowly, because the stairs were broken at the fourth step and there was a woman standing at the top of them in the doorway with a jug in her hands, and because the German who came down those stairs was a man who had arranged, an hour earlier, through the Polish officer of the district who commanded the block of the Madalińskiego Street, that he would come down alone and armed and that he would come up alone and unharmed, and that arrangement is the reason the whole of the rest of this chapter happened in a cellar instead of a courtyard, and it is the reason that the man on the stairs was able to say, in Polish, at the bottom of them, with the courtesy of a clerk in a notary's office in Vienna, the sentence with which he had been rehearsing for a year and ten months:

"Good afternoon. I am looking for a woman of twenty-four years of age who was employed in the household of Oberst von Hagen, and I am ready to pay for the information in a currency that everybody in this district understands, which is that in one hour there will be no more artillery fire on the block between the Madalińskiego and the Odyńca, and this block and the two next to it will not be searched when the district is surrendered, and the eleven thousand people in these streets will go out of this city on their feet and not in a car with grilles, and I am authorised to give that in writing, and I have the writing here in my pocket."

There were four people in that cellar who could hear him properly: a woman of sixty-four who was the owner of the house; a boy of seventeen of the AK with the rifle; a priest without a parish; and a woman with a bandaged left forearm sitting on a stool at the table with a canvas satchel at her feet.

She did not get up. She sat with her arm on her knees and looked at him; and Kalbach, standing at the bottom of a broken staircase in a brick cellar in the Mokotów district with a torch in his left hand and the white flag on his arm, took his spectacles off with his right hand and began to clean them on the corner of his overcoat, and went on talking in the exact, small, level voice of a man giving an inventory.

"You will be interested, I think," he said, "in how I have the name; and I shall tell you, because it is my experience that a person is more likely to be reasonable when they know how little is left of the room they have been hiding in. I have it from a woman who worked in the school of the Sisters of the Sacred Heart in this street, who measured you for a uniform in 1934, and who saw you in the church of the Holy Cross on the eighteenth of August of this year with an armband on your arm. I have the flat on the Mokotowska Street, which I searched on the ninth of September: the rooms at the back, the Latin books, the photograph of 1938, and a silver ring with a greyhound on it in a woman's glove in a drawer. I have, since the fourteenth of January of this year, the record of forty-one German rooms of two cities and the language of a waiter in a café on the rue de Rivoli in Paris, and the account of a jeweller in the Marszałkowska Street concerning pearls, and of a seller of imitations on the Kercelak market concerning paste, and a household book in the hand of the lady of the villa on the Frascati Street, and the day book of the Oberst's orderly in which your absences are written down in hours for eleven days in April and May of 1944. I have, in this pocket, the order for your family: the cellar of the building on the Mokotowska Street is known to this office, and there is a man of sixty-nine in it with a wound in the leg and a girl of seventeen, and I have the authority to have that building searched on the day the district is given up, and I have not exercised it, and I am not going to exercise it, because it is not a fact about you that I want; it is you." He put his spectacles on. "The Oberst is dead. I have read the report and I have read the room, and there is no version of that night in which a woman of twenty-four with no weapon in the house shot a man of fifty-four in a corridor, so I conclude that the house contained a weapon and that you knew where it was. He was, if it is of interest, an officer of the old school, and his section has been broken up, and the file he kept of you has been in my hands since the day after his death, and the whole of what he knew is now written in the notebook in this pocket, in my hand, in nine pages, and I am going to deliver it to my office when I go up those stairs, whatever happens in this cellar, because it is already copied. So then." He looked at her, and for the first time in the whole of the four minutes something moved in his face that was not procedure: "The war is over in this city in five days. There is a divisional court and a police system and a set of ledgers that are going to be better organised in Germany than they have ever been, and I am a man of thirty-eight who has spent five years building card indexes, and I am telling you as a professional to another professional that there is no version of the next eleven years in which the file with your name in it is not in a drawer. So I have come down these stairs to offer you the only thing I have, which is that the file can have a differently-shaped end: a name, a signature, an account of the rooms, given to me in this cellar, in writing, and a transport to the west and a life under another name in a country where nobody has heard of the Nowy Świat. That is not mercy. I do not have mercy. It is arithmetic, and it is the closest thing to a fair offer that this war has produced in the east, and I am not going to repeat it."

And the woman with the bandaged arm sat on the stool and looked at him for about four seconds; and then she said, in German, in the accent of a woman from Vienna, in the flat voice in which she had bought a chicken from an orderly and asked a colonel about a bridge, one sentence:

"My name is Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, and I have nothing to say to you."

And Kalbach, who had spent a year and ten months of his life on that name, and who had a notebook with nine pages of handwriting in it and a chart of eleven rows in his case on the aleja Szucha and an envelope with a ring in it and a card index in his own head that was, at that moment, the only complete account of the woman's existence in the whole of the German Reich, stood at the bottom of a broken staircase in a cellar in a district that was going to be given away in five days, with the white flag of a negotiator on his left arm, and did the one thing that no file in the world had ever told him not to do, which was to open his mouth to answer.

The shot came from the corner of the cellar, from the mattress, from a man of twenty-seven in a Polish coat with a fever of thirty-eight degrees and a wound in his left thigh, who had crawled four metres across a brick floor with a rifle of the year 1938 while a German policeman talked, because he had understood, on the second of the four minutes, that there is no arithmetical solution to the man in front of him, that the rifle was in his hands and not in the boy's, and that the safety catch of that model is a small lever on the right side of the receiver that he had learned about at eleven years of age in the armoury of his father's works from a foreman with one arm; and he fired twice, from the floor, from a distance of two metres and a half, in the way in which a man who has never in his life fired a military weapon fires one, which is with the whole of his hand and the whole of his heart and both eyes open; and the two rounds went into Kalbach's chest at the level of the fourth rib, and the German with the file of the woman of the villa on the Frascati Street came down on the floor of a cellar in the Mokotów district, on the twenty-sixth of September 1944, at about eleven minutes past four in the afternoon, without having finished his sentence.

***

The three of them were alone in that cellar for about two hours, because the priest went up the stairs to hold the doorway and the woman of sixty-four went with him, and because the boy of seventeen was sent to the officer of the block with the news, and because there was nothing to be done about the body of a man of thirty-eight with two holes in his chest in a cellar in a district that had four days left.

What Phillip Brandt did in those two hours was the following, and he did it on his knees, on a straw mattress, with his leg straight out in front of him, in the light of a lamp made of a bottle and a bootlace.

He took the notebook out of the inside pocket of the dead man's overcoat: a small notebook with a hard cover and a broken spine, with nine pages of hand-ruled tables in it and the name of the woman on the first page and a chart of four columns and eleven rows which he could not read in the light and did not need to read, because a man who has been an intelligence officer for four years and has been an enemy for one of them can read a chart's shape from across a room.

He took the briefcase from the floor where it had fallen, and opened it and took out of it an envelope of the Sicherheitspolizei's office on the aleja Szucha, stamped on the eighteenth of September 1944, and in the envelope there were the objects of the inventory of the ninth of September, wrapped in a sheet of the office's tissue paper: a photograph of four people on a lawn in front of a house with a portico in the summer of 1938; a Latin textbook of the edition of 1908 with two names in it; and a silver signet with the head of a greyhound above three willow leaves.

And Phillip Brandt sat on the floor of that cellar and looked at that ring for about eleven seconds, and understood three things in the following order: that it was a family crest and that the name of the family was on it; that the woman to whom it belonged had carried it against her skin in an occupied city for four years and eleven months and had left it in a glove in a drawer on the thirty-first of July 1944 and had said, when she left it, the only sentence of that whole month in which she had been able to be frightened; and that he was a man with a fever and a leg wound and no property whatsoever in the world, sitting in a cellar with the body of the man who had spent a year and ten months hunting her, and that he had it in his hand.

He put it on a cord around her neck himself, with his own hands, in that cellar, in the light of a lamp made of a bottle and a bootlace, while she sat on the stool and did not move and looked at the wall; and his hands had not stopped shaking for about forty minutes and he had to do the knot twice; and neither of them said anything about it at all, then or afterwards, in eleven countries and fifty-one years.

And when it was done, and the notebook and the envelope were in the packing of her satchel, and the two of them were sitting on the floor with the body of Bruno Kalbach at the foot of the stairs under a coat of the Polish fire brigade of 1939, Phillip said the sentence that is the whole of what he had left, which in another book would have been a proposal and in this one was a work instruction:

"They will send somebody else. There is a whole system of these men and the system does not die with the man. We have three days at the most before the file in that building is read by somebody, and the file has nine pages of your handwriting and eleven rows and a note of your father's street, and I want the next three days arranged as follows: the papers in the satchel go to the section tonight, and I tell them what is in the notebook and they write it down, in their own hand, and the copy goes into four hands in this city, and after that, whatever is in the German file, there is a Polish copy of it that will be read in the alley behind a wall in 1945 by men with a different system — and you will not be in it, because the men who are going to arrest the people in that file are going to be arrested themselves, and the people who are going to read it are going to be us." He lay back on the mattress and put his arm over his eyes. "That is the whole of my plan. It is not a good plan. It is the one we have."

The three days turned out to be nine.

At eight in the evening on the second of October 1944, the guns stopped in the city of Warsaw by an agreement signed in a tent in the forest above the town; and in a courtyard on the Wilcza Street, at the end of the same hour, in the light of a fire, officers of a staff that had eleven hours left of its existence stood and burned paper, and the order went out to the sections, in the four surviving districts and in the three that had already gone, in the language of a document of capitulation: that the soldiers of the Polish Home Army were going into captivity, and the civilians were going out through the German lines to the transit camp at Pruszków, and the people whose names were on any German list were not going anywhere as themselves.

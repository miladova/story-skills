# Chapter 14: The Slow Fire

The arrangements were made in the fourth week of May, in a cellar on the Śliska, by a woman of thirty with a ledger and a man of forty-seven in spectacles, and they were made in the way in which such things are always made, which is as a piece of logistics.

Three places.

The room above the cobbler's shop on the Zielna, in the afternoon, on Tuesdays and Fridays, on the days when the Colonel was at the Saski Palace and the car was with him and the sentry at the villa's gate was nineteen years old and bored and could be managed; and the arrangement there was that nothing was ever to be written, nothing left, nothing eaten, and that the chair was to be put back against the wall at the end.

The cemetery at Powązki, on Sundays, in the morning, in fine weather only, in the crowd, because a cemetery in an occupied city is the one public place in which a man and a woman may stand four metres apart for half an hour in the open air and have nothing whatever to explain to anybody; and the rule there was that they never met at the gate and never left at the same time as each other.

And a café on the Chmielna, at the corner of the Szpitalna, where a person could sit for an hour for the price of a coffee and a newspaper, where neither of them ever ate, and where the mark was a coat: a grey coat on the hook inside the door, at the end, which meant *come in*, and no coat, which meant *do not*.

Two hours of the day. That was the arithmetic of it: two hours, twice a week, on days and at hours that were set by a girl of thirty who had never met Phillip Brandt, and who set them each week by a route that went through two other people, neither of whom knew what they were carrying, so that no single person in the chain knew both the hour and the man.

And the marks: a chalk line drawn under the second window of the courtyard on the Zielna, which meant the yard was watched; a light in the window of the third floor of a building on the Hoża Street, which meant that the street was quiet; a blue scarf in the window of a shop on the Bracka, which meant a change of hour. Nothing in writing, no paper, no name, and never the same coat twice on the Chmielna, because a woman in a fur coat is a woman whom four people in a café will remember.

That is what their love physically was: a set of arrangements, made by other people, entered in a ledger, subject to revision, in a city where a scratch of chalk on a wall could mean a man's death; and the fact that it worked at all in that city, for six weeks, in the fifth year of the war, is a piece of professional achievement that neither of them ever told anybody about, and there is nobody left alive who knows how it was done.

***

Powązki was the largest thing in Warsaw.

It was about seventy hectares of graves on the low hills in the north-west, with avenues of lime trees and chestnuts, a wall, three gates, the graves of the men of 1831 and 1863 and 1920, the graves of the writers and the actors and the two thousand men of the legions, and family chapels the size of small houses; and in the spring of 1944 it had, among all the other things in it, the mass graves of September 1939, the rows of little iron crosses for the soldiers, and a great many fresh graves of people who had died in the ordinary way in the four years since, which in that city meant being carried out of a flat by neighbours and buried by a priest with the coffin on a handcart.

And it had, on a Sunday morning in late May, the population of a city's weekend: old women with flowers wrapped in damp paper, children running in the avenues, men in coats that had been good in 1930, a priest in a black overcoat at the gate of the cemetery of the evangelicals, and, at eleven in the morning, an officer of the Wehrmacht in the uniform of the Abwehr walking slowly along the fourth avenue with his hands behind him and a Polish woman in a grey coat reading the names on the stones eleven metres away, in the sun, in the smell of cut grass and lilac and, from somewhere to the north, the smell of coal smoke.

They had between them, in the whole of an hour, about eleven sentences.

"Here is the question I want answered first," she said, in front of a stone with a photograph on it of a young man with a moustache. "What does your father say at table?"

"He says nothing at table, because my mother says nothing at table, and my mother's silence is the law in that flat. He says things in the doorway when he has his coat on and I am going out. He said to me on Tuesday, with his hat in his hand: *the men who rule with the paper will be gone in a year and the paper will still be in a drawer, and somebody is going to have to explain it, and it will not be me, because I have a son in the Abwehr.*"

"That is a joke."

"It is half a joke. He has been making the same half of a joke since 1940. What does your father say?"

"My father says Latin. He has four boys and a Latin primer and he says to them that a man who has the grammar of a language has the key to a country, and that a country that loses its grammar loses the country." She turned a page of her book, which was a book of 1911 with a broken spine, and which she had carried in her pockets for three weeks. "He was wrong about one thing. He taught me the grammar of French and German and the syntax of the poets, and I have spent four years using the syntax of the poets to say *good evening, Herr Oberst* in a voice that makes a man want to be at home."

"That is not nothing," Phillip said. "That is the whole trade."

"That is the whole trade."

They walked on. A family went past them with a wreath, four adults and two children in poor shoes, and the whole of the family looked at the officer once, together, in the way of a flock, and then away; and Phillip took his cap off and held it, which is what any German officer would have done in a cemetery in front of a funeral, and it also meant that his face was not in the family's memory.

Her turn came at the end of the avenue, in front of a chapel with a rusted iron door.

"There was a beet machine at Wierzbów," she said. "A beet harvester, bought in 1934 out of the mortgage money, with a name-plate on it that said the works: Lilpop, Rau i Loewenstein, Burakowska Street, Warsaw. It came up on the railway to Grodzisk and then by cart, in four pieces, and a fitter came with it from Warsaw and stayed eleven days in the servants' hall, and his name was Pan Ludwik and he was forty and he had a mole on his chin, and he took the whole thing apart twice and put it together again and showed my father how, and on the last Sunday he took me and my brother out to the field with him and we sat on the machine and he told us what every lever did." She put her hand flat on the ironwork of the chapel door, because it was cold and she wanted to feel something. "The smell of machine oil is the smell of those eleven days, and of that Sunday, and of the whole of the last autumn there, which is the only autumn of my life in which nothing whatever was wrong. Your father's works are in my father's last good year. That is the fact on my side of the table. You have the whole of it."

Phillip Brandt stopped in the avenue with his cap in his hand.

"My father's works," he said, "built the gun mountings for the Polish coastal batteries in 1938 on a licence from a German firm, and my father signed the licence in October 1937 in his office on the Burakowska Street, and I was in that office that afternoon with a copy of Racine in my pocket, and I have spent four years knowing that every piece of paper that a man signs in a country that is about to be sold is a piece of paper he will be asked about in a room with a lamp." He looked at the stones. "There is no clean year. That is all I have to say about 1938, and I shall not say it again, and I would like to be told about the beet machine instead, and how it stuck in wet ground."

It stuck in wet ground for the whole of the first season, and the man from Sandomierz who had sold them the seed said it was the ground and the estate agent said it was the machine, and they sat in the avenue in the sun for eleven minutes arguing about it, in two languages, and neither of them noticed that they had stopped doing anything else.

***

The patrol came on the Hoża Street on the fourth of June, at half past four in the afternoon.

He was late. That was the first fact and it decided everything: he was nine minutes late, which had never happened, and she was standing at the corner of the Zielna and the Hoża with her hands in her pockets, in the open, in the daylight, because the rule about the corner was that a person did not stand there and the rule about being late was that at seven minutes past the hour a person walked away, and she had broken both rules in the same minute, because of the nine minutes.

And then the boots: eleven men on the pavement coming from the direction of the Krucza in a line, in greatcoats, with that particular tread on paving stone that the whole of Europe had learned to recognise, and a car behind them at the kerb; and between her and the courtyard that had the doorway she was supposed to use there were forty metres of open pavement with nine people on it.

He came round the corner nine metres from her, saw the patrol in the same second in which she saw him, and did the thing that she thought about for the rest of her life.

He came up the pavement towards her fast and loud, and he said, in German, at a volume that carried to the corner and to the man selling newspapers and to the two old women at the tram stop and to the eleven men in greatcoats:

*"Verfluchte polnische Schlampe — willst du mir die Straße versperren?"*

And the filth of it, the word he had used, the worst word in the language for a woman, said at that volume, on the pavement of the Hoża Street in the afternoon, made the whole of the street turn around; and he took her by the upper arm and turned her and marched her to a doorway, and put her back against the wall of the doorway with one hand flat on the bricks beside her head and the other holding the lapel of her coat, and demanded her papers, and she, with her chin down and her hands shaking — and the shaking was not a performance, which was the thing she noticed about it afterwards with a certain professional interest — got the Kennkarte out of her bag and gave it to him, and put her back against the wall and said, in the Polish of a woman who was about to be arrested, in a voice pitched for the boots:

"I have done nothing, sir, I have a pass, I am a domestic, I am in service on the Mokotowska —"

"Shut your mouth," he said, and looked at the card. "Marion Herschlag, Austrian. Are you a Jew?"

"No, sir."

"Are you a Pole?"

"I am an Austrian, sir, I work in the house on the Mokotowska, my papers are in order—"

And the boots came past the doorway, all eleven of them, one after the other, and one of them glanced in and saw an officer of the Wehrmacht holding a woman's papers in the doorway of a building on the Hoża Street and shouting at her, which is a scene that happens eleven thousand times a day in an occupied city and is of no interest to any soldier in the world; and the boots went round the corner and up the Zielna and stopped at the corner of the Szpitalna, and stayed there for four minutes, and the two of them stayed in the doorway, in the position, with her back against the brick and his hand on the bricks beside her head, for the whole of the four minutes, saying nothing, and looking at nothing, at the collar of her coat, at his own hand.

"It is nothing personal," he said, eleven seconds after the boots had gone, in a completely different voice, without moving his mouth very much. "It is what an officer says to a woman who is in his way. If I had said one word less they would have remembered us; and if I had said it in Polish they would have remembered me."

"I know."

"I did not enjoy it."

"I know that too," she said, and remained where she was, because her legs were not yet entirely hers.

And that answer, which he thought about for eleven days, and which she thought about for eleven years, was not the answer he had expected, and he turned his head for the first time and looked at her, and then the boots came back up the Zielna and past the end of the street an inch behind them, and they waited for another ninety seconds, and then he let go of the bricks and stepped back and said, in a loud, tired, German voice, for the street: "Go on. Get out of my sight," and she went, up the Zielna into the courtyard and through the arch into the next street with her hands in her pockets and the whole of her body shaking, and they did not see each other for eleven days.

***

They talked about the end of the war four times, and the whole of it was in code.

He gave her the political arithmetic, in the way of a man of thirty who has been reading the papers of a general staff for four years and whose mind was made to hold such things: that the armies of the Soviet Union had been in Poland since January; that the second front had come in June; that in Lublin, since July, there was a committee of Poles with a Russian general standing behind it, and that the committee had been given a name that was the same name as the government in London and was not the same thing at all; that in the Government General the Germans were building four lines of field works with Polish labour and Jewish graves in the foundations; and that in every office in Warsaw, from the garrison's Ia down to the typists, men were arguing about one question in the smoking-room, in lowered voices, which was whether Warsaw would be defended as a fortress or given up as a ruin.

"Which?" she said.

"They do not know. That is the true answer and you can have it. The staff does not know, and the argument is between the men who remember 1914 and the men who have read the reports from Stalingrad, and the second set is bigger than it was and the first set has the rank. If I had to put money, I would put it on the city being fought for in the streets, because everything that has been built here for two years has been built for a siege, and because a man who has been told to make a fortress makes a fortress whatever the generals decide." He looked at the stones. "And the underground will rise, because everybody in this city knows that it will, and because the committee in Lublin will want it, and because the government in London will be told about it afterwards. And when I say everybody knows, I mean everybody: my clerk knows it, my barber knows it, the boy who shines shoes at the corner of the Bracka knows it, and it is in the safe in the section as a paragraph in a report from the army's own intelligence, in German, on paper with four copies, saying that a rising in the city is probable in the summer."

"And you will be in it."

"I will be in the building that is being besieged, in a uniform, and the twenty-eight days of a siege are coming to this city in the next twelve months, and I shall be on the wrong side of the wall, in the wrong coat, and the Poles will be shooting at the coat." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way an engineer says that a bridge will take a load of eleven tonnes and no more. "And you will be on the wrong side of the same wall, and the coat will be on a chair in a bedroom in a villa, and there will be nothing in the world that either of us can do about the arrangement."

"Then do not say it."

"I am not saying it. I am describing the material."

Neither of them ever said the word love — not in the cemetery, not in the room above the cobbler's, not in the four thousand sentences they exchanged in six weeks — and the reason was not delicacy and it was not fear of the censor. It was the arithmetic, and they both had it: he had a mother in Wola whose name was on a list, and she had a father with one lung on the Mokotowska Street and a sister of seventeen who had bought coal with a pianino; and a sentence said out loud in that city in that year was a rope with two ends in it, and the only way to keep the rope off the two ends was to leave it lying on the ground between them.

The photograph was in the flat above the cobbler's, on the wall of the back room, among eleven other photographs of the Wolski family and their relations, none of whom either of them knew.

It was an amateur print, about the size of a hand, fixed to the wall with two pins, of a boy and a girl, aged about eight and six, standing at a well with a bucket between them; the well had a wooden cover and a windlass, and the girl was holding the handle of the windlass with both hands and the boy had his hand flat on the rim of the well, and the two of them were looking straight at the camera, and it was summer and it was somewhere in the countryside and it had nothing whatever to do with either of them.

They stood in front of it on the first of June, for a long time, in a cold room in the fifth year of the war, and then she said the first honest sentence she had said to anybody since September 1939.

"It sticks at eleven metres," she said. "You let it down twice with a slack turn on the second, and then it goes."

Phillip Brandt did not say anything for a moment. Then he said, in Polish: "I know. I have known for six years." And he put his hand flat on the wall beside the photograph and left it there, and she stood a hand's length away with her hands in her pockets, and that was the whole of the physical content of the afternoon.

***

The dinner was on the sixth of June at the villa on the Frascati Street: eight people, the Oberst and his lady, two officers of the section, the civil administrator of the district and his wife, a captain of the Luftwaffe who was in Warsaw for a fortnight, and a Polish woman from the Kraków who had married into the German administration and who spent the whole evening being watched by everybody; and the food was good and the silver was the requisitioned silver of a family in the Żoliborz and the wine was from the cellar of somebody in the country, and the whole of it was the ordinary evening of the ruling class of a city in the fifth year of an occupation.

The plate of the civil administrator's wife was cold.

It is worth setting down exactly what happened, because in the memory of the people in that room it grew into two or three different stories, and in the file of the security police it never appeared at all. Von Hagen noticed the plate — he always noticed, he was a man who noticed the room — and he said something to Janina, who was carrying a dish in at that moment, in a voice that was not loud, and it was a sentence of four words in German about the temperature of a plate; and Janina, who was forty-five years old and had been in service since 1916 and had a bad wrist, and who understood about eleven words of German and those eleven were the ones she had been made to learn, said, "Ja, gnädiger Herr," in the way in which she said it eleven times a day, and turned to go.

And he hit her.

It was not a blow of any force — he was sitting and she was standing, and it was the back of his hand across the face, and it made the sound that such a thing makes in a dining room with eight people in it and a fire going; and it knocked her sideways one step, and she brought the dish up against her chest with both hands to save it, which she did, entirely; and there was a second of no sound at all in that room, and then the civil administrator's wife said something about the heat of the summer, in German, in a flat, ordinary voice, and the Luftwaffe captain laughed at the end of the table at some other thing, and the room went on.

Janina went out with the dish. It had not spilled. She carried it out of the room, into the pantry, and set it down on the table, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, who was sitting two places from the Colonel with the paste pearls at her throat and a glass of wine in her hand, sat there for the rest of the dinner and did not move and did not stand up and did not say one word, because there is no version of that evening in which the lady of the house stands up for the servant, and because the four hours of that evening were four hours of work, and because she was, at that moment, the only person in the room who had seen the whole of the sentence from the beginning: a man in a villa on the Frascati Street, in a country he was losing, on the sixth of June 1944, with the second front on the beaches of Normandy and the Russian guns three hundred kilometres to the east, taking out the whole of the war on the face of a Polish woman of forty-five with a bad wrist who had said the only two words she knew.

And after the guests had gone, and the fire was banked, and the house had settled, the Colonel came up the stairs behind her and put his hand on the back of her neck in the corridor, in the way he did, at the top of the stairs, when he wanted her; and she leaned her head back into his hand, and he said something about the civil administrator's wife's hat, and she laughed, and they went into his room; and it is worth setting down that the whole of the change from one room to the other took her less than a minute, and that the minute was the seam, and that nobody in the world — not her brother, not the Major, not the priest in Paris, not the man in the cemetery — was ever given a look at it; and that the woman who lay in the dark afterwards with the pearls on the chair in the box had, on that same afternoon, said the first honest sentence she had said since 1939, to a man ten metres away in a room above a cobbler's shop, about a well in an orchard in Mazovia that stuck at eleven metres.

***

On the Tuesday of the last week of May she came into the flat above the cobbler's at four and he was already there, with the coat off and the parcel of the day on the table, and there were eleven minutes before the hour at which they had to be out of the building by different doors; and they spent the eleven minutes on the thing that neither of them had yet been able to spend on anything else, which was the ledger.

"There is a notebook," she said.

"There is always a notebook. Which one?"

"Rutz's. The Colonel's driver, who has been with him since 1937 and who writes everything in it: the hours the car is used, the miles, the wine, the silver, the laundry — and the hours I leave the house and the hours I come back." She had her hands flat on the table, and she was talking fast, because eleven minutes is eleven minutes. "I saw him write it in Paris in December. *Fräulein Marion, collected 1.35.* He is a good man and he is not one of them and he writes it because a Furier of the old army writes everything; and it is the single most dangerous object in that house, and it has been kept for seven years."

"I know about day books," Phillip said. "There was a case in the Amt in the spring of a clerk in Bucharest who was caught by a day book in a hotel. Four hundred pages of a concierge's handwriting and one man's name appearing eleven times on the wrong nights." He looked at the paper on the table. "What do you want me to do?"

"Nothing. There is nothing that can be done about a day book. If it disappears, that is worse than if it stays. I am telling you because it is the material, and because you are the only person in this city to whom I can say the word *ledger* and be understood." She picked up the parcel and began to unclip the string. "And because if a certain kind of man ever sits down in a chair in that villa with a notebook, the first thing he will ask for is the day book, and the second thing he will ask for is Rutz, and the third thing will be what the orderly thinks when he is asked a question by a policeman, which is that he will answer it because he is Bavarian and has nothing to hide and thinks the whole thing is an enquiry about petrol."

"Then we have a timetable."

"Then we have a timetable," she said, "and it is not in our hands, and the only thing that is in our hands is that when the day book is read, it should be read in a city where I have never left the house on Tuesdays."

***

She came home that evening at a quarter past six to the villa on the Frascati Street and found a car in the drive that she did not know: a saloon, grey-brown, of a make that was not standard for the garrison, with a Warsaw number plate and a driver in civilian clothes at the wheel reading a newspaper, and mud on the running board.

And on the terrace, with his back to the door and the garden in front of him, there was a man of about thirty-eight in a civilian overcoat and a hat that was two years out of fashion, standing very still with a small notebook in one hand, cleaning his spectacles with a handkerchief held in the other; and as she came across the gravel he finished with the spectacles and put them on and turned his head slightly, in the way of a man who has heard a step behind him and who has decided, in advance, not to turn round until he has finished what he is doing.

Emil Rutz was standing at the foot of the terrace steps with his cap in his hand and his tunic buttoned and an expression of the most ordinary discomfort: the expression of a soldier of the old army who has been asked a question by a man in a civilian coat and who has answered it because he is a soldier and the man has a paper.

"—and the Fräulein has been with the Oberst since Paris, since last year, that is what I said, since June of last year, and before that I do not know, and it is not my business to know, and the Oberst's household is my duty—"

"Of course," said the man on the terrace, in a soft, small, exact voice, in German, without any accent that she could place at all. "Nobody is asking about anybody's duty, and I am grateful. For the record: *Fräulein Marion Herschlag, Austrian, in the household of Oberst von Hagen, since June 1943; employed in the section as a clerk since March 1944.*" He wrote it down, in the small notebook, in a small hand, and then looked up and saw her, and bowed, an inch, with the hat in his hand, and said: "Gnädige Frau. Forgive the intrusion. My name is Kalbach, and I am from the Sicherheitspolizei's office on the aleja Szucha, and I am here about a box of paper that came from Paris in March, and I have been told that it is a matter of some interest to the Oberst — which is to say, of course, that it is a matter of some interest to me."

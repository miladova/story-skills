# Chapter 23: The Hour of the Wolf

The letter came into the Polish lines on the nineteenth of September 1944 through four hands and a sacrament, and the four hands were: a German military chaplain of the garrison of Warsaw, who was a Catholic priest of the diocese of Trier and who had been in that city since 1940 and had buried more German soldiers than any man in the Government General; a Polish priest of the parish of the Holy Cross, who had known the German chaplain before the war at a conference in Munich in 1936 and who had been crossing between the lines since the middle of August with the permission of both sides because both sides needed a man who could carry a letter about the wounded; a woman of about fifty in a black coat who worked in the office of the German garrison's medical service and who had carried the German chaplain's satchel eleven times across the Warsaw streets since the first of August; and a boy of the Polish scouts of the Śródmieście, who had carried it the last eleven hundred metres, in a food tin, and who was twelve years old.

The letter was in German, on one sheet, in the hand of an officer of the old school, with the date and the place and no salutation and no signature, and it was addressed on the outside to *Fräulein Marion Herschlag, Austrian national, at present in the city of Warsaw*, and the whole of the text of it is the following eleven lines:

*1. I know that the woman who lived in this house from the first of March until the first of August 1944 under that name is a Polish agent of the Polish underground, and that she was so for the whole of the period, and I have known it certainly since the sixteenth of August, and I have the material to prove it in writing, in this house, and it consists of the following: the jeweller's statement concerning the pearls, and the paste, the statement of the woman of the Kercelak market, the household book of this house with your handwriting and the forged document and its stamp, the day book of my orderly with the hours of your absences on eleven occasions in April and May, and the account of the undersigned of a conversation in the hall of this house on the thirty-first of July 1944 at six o'clock in the morning.*

*2. I have not given the material to the Sicherheitspolizei and I shall not, provided that the following is done.*

*3. The woman will present herself at the gate of this house on the Frascati Street at eleven o'clock at night on the twenty-third of September 1944, alone, with a white cloth, and she will be received, and she will be permitted to leave the house and to return to her own people at six o'clock in the morning.*

*4. If she does not come, then on the twenty-fourth of September the material goes, in the form of a report, to Sturmbannführer Kalbach of the Sicherheitspolizei's office on the aleja Szucha, together with the following additional information, which I have established by the enquiries of my own section and by the police of the district: the family of the woman is the family of the former owner of the palace on the Nowy Świat and is at present in the cellar of the building on the Mokotowska Street, consisting of a man of sixty-nine years of age who has been wounded in the leg and is not evacuated, of a daughter of seventeen years of age, and of a son who is a member of the sabotage section of the Polish underground and is at present within the Polish lines in the district of the Śródmieście; and the address of that cellar is known to me, and a report with that address in it, given to the office of the Sicherheitspolizei, will result in a search of that building by the police of the district on the day after the report, and the consequences of such a search are known to the woman as well as they are known to me.*

*5. The woman will bring no papers and no arms, and she will be searched at the gate, and she will be given a receipt for everything that is taken from her.*

There is no sixth paragraph. The letter ended there, eleven lines, and the page had been folded in three and there was a thumb mark on the lower corner of the sheet in the ink of the signature that was not there.

***

The staff argued about it for one hour and eleven minutes on the twentieth of September, in a room on the fourth floor of a building on the Świętokrzyska Street, and the argument was not about her.

She was in the room. She had been brought into the room by the boy of fourteen at eleven o'clock and had sat on a chair by the wall for the whole of the hour and eleven minutes, which is the procedure, and the whole of what she heard in that hour was the sound of five men doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the following: that the material in the villa on the Frascati Street was, on the evidence, the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison's own file on the penetration of the German administration of that city — the pearls, the paste, the day book, the stamp, the handwriting, the letter of a German officer of counter-intelligence's own hand describing the method — and that a file of that kind, taken out of a German house and brought within the Polish lines in September 1944, was the only document in the whole of the rising that could be read by a Polish court in 1945 without a single witness; that the woman who was named in the letter was one of eleven people alive in the city who had seen the inside of that section; and that the price of the whole of it was the delivery of that woman to the gate of a house in the German quarter at eleven o'clock at night on the twenty-third of September, alone, by a staff which had no means whatever of protecting her, since the German quarter of Warsaw on the twenty-third of September 1944 was a place into which no unit of the Home Army could send anybody at all.

At the end of the hour and eleven minutes, the officer chairing — a lieutenant colonel of about forty-five with a face like a hatchet and a wounded arm in a sling, who had come into the Old Town in the first week and had been brought out through the same canal on the second of September — took off his spectacles and said the sentence that a staff has to say, and he said it to her, at last, looking at her for the first time in the whole hour:

"Comrade Maria. The section's view of the material is that it is the most valuable single piece of paper in the possession of the German quarter and that it will be of use to the Polish state if the Polish state exists. The section's view of the operation is that it is a trap, or a place, or both, and that no operation can be mounted to support it, and that the responsibility for going into that house is yours and cannot be anybody else's, and that no officer in this room has the right to order you to go. The section asks the question. What do you want?"

And Krupa, who had been standing at the window with his back to the room for the whole of the hour, turned round; and Iga Zaleska, who had been brought out of the Old Town on the third of September in a cart and was sitting in a chair in the corner with her leg straight out in front of her and a bandage showing at the ankle, said nothing at all, and looked at her, and the look was the look of a woman who has been holding a ledger for five years and has just found a line in it that she would like to burn.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood up.

"I will go," she said. "On two conditions, and they are both the section's to grant and not mine to ask. The first is that the priest of the Holy Cross carries my answer tonight in the form in which he carried the letter, and that in the answer I am permitted to write one line of my own, and the line is: *I will come at eleven, and I will come in my own name, and I want the file, and you will be told why when I am in the room.*" She looked at the colonel. "The second is that if I am not back on this side of the lines by nine o'clock on the morning of the twenty-fourth of September, the section will do the only thing it can do about the material in that house, and that is to put the whole of this letter and the whole of what I tell them in the next three days into writing and into four separate hands, so that if the file goes to the Szucha there will at least be a copy of it in the Polish lines within a week, and every officer in that villa will know that there is a copy of it in the Polish lines, in the Polish language, with his name in it. That is the whole of what I want. It is not protection and it is not a rescue party. It is the only thing that can be done with a man like that, which is to make him understand that the paper he is holding has two ends."

The colonel looked at her for about four seconds.

"Write the line," he said.

***

She walked into the German quarter at half past ten at night on the twenty-third of September 1944 with a white cloth over her arm and nothing in her pockets.

The route went through the ruins of the northern Śródmieście: down the Długa, past the burned shell of the theatre, across the Plac Teatralny in the dark among the bricks, along the Senatorska with the line of the German posts on the Saxon Garden on her left, and then out of the ruins and into the streets that had never been shelled, which was the strangest thing about that walk and the thing that no description of that September ever conveys: that a person could walk for four minutes through a desert of brick and burned iron in which there was not one window and not one roof, and then step over a line of sandbags and find herself in a street with a working street lamp and a car with its lights on and a sentry-box with a stove in it and the smell of coffee coming out of a doorway.

The three checkpoints were at the Plac Piłsudskiego, at the corner of the Nowy Świat, and at the gate of the villa on the Frascati Street; and at each of them she stood still in the light of a torch with the white cloth over her arm, and said, in German, in the accent of a woman from Vienna, the sentence that the answer had been permitted to contain: *I am the woman from the house on the Frascati Street and the Oberst is expecting me*; and at each of them a man telephoned, and at the last of the three a car was waiting for her and a Gefreiter of the Feldgendarmerie opened the door; and the whole of that walk is the reason the novel can be said to be a war novel at all, because the arithmetic of it is the arithmetic of the whole war in one person: four hundred metres of a city in which there is no law at all, and a woman in it, at night, in a country where her name would have been enough to hang her, going to keep an appointment with the man she had robbed, to buy a paper out of a house that had been his, and she was not frightened; and the reason she was not frightened is the last thing she ever told anybody about that night, and she told it in 1947 in a kitchen in Łódź to the only person still living who had known her brother: *by the twenty-third of September I had left everything I had in the water under the Saxon Garden, and a person who has left everything behind is at last able to do the arithmetic properly, because there is nothing left in the account to lose.*

---

The house on the Frascati Street had been hit once, by a shell from the Polish side of the river or by a bomb from a German aeroplane or by neither, in the third week of August, and the whole of the north-west corner of the upper floor was gone: the roof of the corridor was open to the sky, the wall of the room that had been hers was a ruin with the wallpaper hanging on the remains of it, and the rain of the last three weeks had been in it, and there was a smell in the whole house that she knew from eleven other houses in that city, which is the smell of a building that has been rained on through a broken roof and is full of wet plaster and the leaves of the garden blown in.

The ground floor was untouched.

The hall was swept. There was a lamp in the drawing room and the fire was lit and the curtains were drawn, and the dining room had a table laid for two with a cloth and candles and the requisitioned silver of a family in the Żoliborz, and the piano was gone — burned, she learned later, in the first week of August, when there was no coal — and the place where it had stood was marked by the four dark rings of the casters on the parquet, and the study had a lamp and a fire and the maps, and on the wall of the study, in the place where it had hung for four years, there was the lithograph of the Lilpop works at Burakowska Street, which had been taken down off its hook and put back on it seven times a week for four months in the spring.

And at the door of the drawing room, in a grey tunic and a clean shirt with the collar open and his hair cut and shaved at the cheek, with the duelling scar where it had always been, stood Wolfram von Hagen, fifty-four years old, of the counter-intelligence section of the garrison command of the city of Warsaw, which had eleven days of its existence left, and he looked at her for four seconds.

"You are thinner," he said.

"I have been walking."

"Yes. I heard something of that." He stepped back from the doorway and put his hand out towards the room, in the way of a man who has been brought up in a house where a lady enters a room first, and the whole of the courtesy of that gesture, from a man who had just written his own blackmail in eleven numbered paragraphs, is the reason the rest of the twentieth century was never able to explain that class of German to anybody: "Come in. There is a fire, and there is something to eat, and it is all real, and I did the cooking myself, which is the only thing about this evening you will be able to complain about."

***

They ate at nine o'clock at the table for twelve with two places at one end and four candles and a white cloth, and the food was real food: a soup, and a piece of pork, and a compote of plums from the garden of that house which had grown and been gathered by Janina of the village near Sandomierz, who had gone through the German lines on the second of August to her sister in the Wola and had not been heard of since; and the wine was a bottle of a Burgundy of 1937 out of the cellar of a family in the Żoliborz, and von Hagen poured it himself into two glasses and did not spill any of it, and he talked, and she ate; and the whole of it lasted two hours and eleven minutes, and it was, in the judgment of everyone who ever read the file, and in the judgment of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna for the whole of the rest of her life, the most intelligent and the most terrible conversation ever held in that city.

He began with the war. That was the professional habit and it was also, in the last analysis, the only way into the room.

That the western front had been at the Rhine since the middle of the month; that the eastern front was in Poland, on the Narew and the Vistula, with a Russian army in Praga, on the right bank of the river, looking at the left; that the rising in this city was a military absurdity which had cost the Poles about a hundred and fifty thousand people and had cost the German army a division, and that both of those numbers were on the ledger and the ledger was the only thing the twentieth century understood; that the government of the Reich had decided in August to treat the city as a fortress, and that the order of the twelfth of October, which had not yet been written but which every officer of his rank in that city knew would be written, was that the city was to be destroyed before it was given up, and that the officer who had been assigned the preparation of the demolition plan for the city of Warsaw was himself.

And then he stopped, and put his glass down, and said: "I asked you here for a file and not for a lecture. So. The file is real, and it is in the safe, and you will have it before six in the morning, and there is no version of the next five hours in which I give it to you and you walk out of my house without a conversation, because I have been a serving officer for twenty-nine years and I have never once in my life made a payment without knowing what I was paying for. So: the ordering of the evening is mine. You will answer my questions honestly, or you will lie to me, and it will not make any difference, because I have been reading your answers for a year and a half and I shall read them now. First: how old were you when you went to France?"

"Twenty."

"Your German is not a Frenchwoman's and not a Pole's and not a Viennese's. Where did you learn it?"

"In Warsaw, at the school of the Sisters of the Sacred Heart, from a woman from Graz who taught us the catechism and the language at the same time, and then for two years from a book and from a gramophone, in Lausanne, in the winters of 1937 and 1938, when I was at the school there and could not afford the ski season and sat in the refectory and did the German lessons twice."

"Which is a good answer, and is true in the part that matters." He refilled the glasses, both of them, his own and hers, with a steadiness that she had never seen in him and would remember afterwards as the most frightening thing in the whole of that evening. "Second: the pearls."

"I sold them on the Marszałkowska on the twenty-sixth of April for eighteen thousand zlotys, and I bought a paste strand at the Kercelak the same afternoon for nine hundred, and I did the substitution in the bathroom with adhesive tape and a candle, and the reason I needed the money was a man in the ruins of the ghetto who was asking me for twenty-five thousand a month, and the reason I needed the paste was that you would notice the difference in a necklace on my throat before you noticed anything else in your whole life."

"And the man?"

"He was a szmalcownik and he was shot by the orders of the underground on the twelfth of May on the Chmielna Street, and I did not shoot him and I went to a concert with you that evening and heard a violinist play the Brahms and I have never told anybody in the world about the concert until now."

"Why did you tell me that?"

"Because it is the only thing I have left that costs anything," she said, in the candlelight, with her hands flat on the cloth, in the flat voice with which she had asked a captain for a shopping list in a cellar in the month of July, "and because I have understood, at this table, in the last hour, that you are not going to let me out of this house until I have paid for the file in the currency you want, and the currency you want is not the file and is not the pearls and is not my body, and it is the one thing that neither of us has ever said in this house, which is the truth about the two of us."

And von Hagen sat back in his chair and looked at her for eleven seconds with his hands on the arms of his chair; and the fire went on burning in the grate of a room in a requisitioned villa on the Frascati Street in Warsaw on the night of the twenty-third of September 1944; and then he said, in a completely different voice, which was the voice of a man asking a subordinate about a piece of paper, and which was, in Krystyna Stankiewiczówna's own estimate, the single most dangerous thing that had been said in that house in the whole of the war:

"Then tell me about the two of us."

***

She gave him what he asked for, and the thing she gave him was the masterpiece of her whole career, and it took her forty minutes, and the whole of it was true, and none of it was the truth.

She told him that she was a Polish girl born in Warsaw in 1919, of a family of the impoverished nobility of Mazovia; that she had been in France since 1940; that she had been placed in his path in Paris in 1943 by an intelligence service of the Polish government in London, and that the placing had been deliberate and the whole of her had been in it: the flat, the dress, the Austrian papers, the dinner at the Majestic in December where a general had shaken his hand, the ball, the corridor, the way she had let him find her in a corridor at half past one in the morning; that the pearls had been an investment of that service; that the singing had been taught and the Italian had been taught and the manner with the servants had been taught; that she had gone to his bed on the twelfth of June 1943 on the written instruction of a man in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement, and had reported to that man every eleven days for seven months; and that the whole of the sixteen months she had spent in his house, from the avenue Henri-Martin to the villa on the Frascati Street, had been work, and had been paid for at a rate of a thousand francs a month and expenses, and had been entered in a ledger in a cellar in Paris, and that the ledger had been burned in February 1944, and that this was the whole of the account, and that she was sorry for exactly one thing in the whole of it, and the thing she was sorry for was the afternoon in December when she had sat on a chair in a bedroom in Paris with a pearl at her throat and had told him about the palace on the Nowy Świat and the horses and the winters, because that story had been her own and not the service's, and she had given it to him as part of the work, and it was the only thing that she had ever regretted giving to anybody.

Every sentence of that was true. The dates were true; the places were true; the man in the flat above the laundry was true; the thousand francs a month was a figure she had invented and it was the only untrue thing in forty minutes, and it was invented because a true report with no figure in it is a report that a professional does not believe.

And what made the whole of it the masterpiece was the one thing she did not say and the one thing she made him do, which was to take the true story of a woman who had been sent into his house and to read it as the story of a woman who had never been in it. She gave him the whole of the arrangement and she withheld from it, by not one word and not one hesitation, the only fact that mattered, which was that from the second week of June 1943 she had stopped being a woman who had been sent and had become something else entirely, and that she had gone on sending the reports anyway, and that she had done the work of four years with one hand while the other hand was in his.

She gave it to him because a man of fifty-four in a ruined house in the last September of that war needed, more than he needed the truth, the right to be a man who had been deceived; and because the compact of that evening had been made in the letter, in eleven numbered paragraphs, by a man who had known, when he wrote it, that he was going to ask her this question; and because she had understood, in the fourth minute of the forty, that this was the last kindness she had it in her power to do for anybody in the whole of her life, and that she was going to do it, and that the two of them would have four hours at the end of it, and that she was going to spend those four hours taking his papers, and that the whole of the kindness was the price of the papers, and that she had been trained in a flat above a laundry in 1942 to know exactly what she was doing, and to do it without a tremor, and that she was fifty per cent correct, which is the highest figure that a person in that trade is ever permitted.

***

The bedroom was the room that had been his wife's and then had been his and then had been the room at the top of the stairs with the door that stood open, and it had a bed, a lamp, a chair with a folded tunic on it, and the smell of cool tobacco and saddle soap and, very faintly, since 1931, the lavender water of a woman who had been dead for eleven years and whose packing case had followed him from Paris two postings too late and had never been opened or thrown away.

What happened in that room between midnight and ten past two in the morning of the twenty-fourth of September 1944 is not an incident in this book and cannot be written as one; and the only person who could have written it set down four lines about it in 1947, in a notebook, in Łódź, in a hand that has been preserved, and the four lines are these:

*He was not cruel. He was thorough. He was fifty-four years old and he had lost everything, and he wanted to prove to himself with his own hands that he had owned something, and I lay in the dark of that room and let him do it, and I did it on purpose, and the purpose was in the top left drawer of the desk in the study, four metres away through the wall, and I have never once been able to say whether that was courage or whether that was what four years had made of me.*

*He said one thing at the end of it, in the dark, with his hand on my shoulder, and it was not* I love you *and it was not anything of the kind.*

*He said:* "Tomorrow they will ask me at the office what I did last night, and I shall tell them that I dined with a lady and that the lady is going to be looked for by the police for the next eleven days and will not be found, and that is the last lie I shall tell in this war."

*Then he slept.*

***

She lay still for forty minutes to be certain of the sleeping, and she was certain at ten to three; and then she got up, and put her shirt and her trousers on in the dark without a sound of any kind, and went out of the bedroom and along the corridor and down the stairs of a house in the German quarter of Warsaw in her bare feet, with her boots in her hand.

The study door was open. There was a fire in the grate that had gone to embers, and there was a lamp on the desk that she did not light, and there was the moonlight on the floor from the window with the blackout curtain pulled half across, and there was the lithograph of the Lilpop works at Burakowska Street hanging on the wall in the place where it had hung since 1940, and behind it the safe stood in the wall like the door of a bank in a village.

She moved the picture off its hook and set it on the floor with the glass against the wall.

The whole of the rest of it was a woman's memory doing its job, and it took one minute and forty seconds.

The dial: to the left twice past the twenty-ninth, then right to the third, then left to the seventeenth; the date the twenty-ninth of March 1917, which was the day a boy of fifteen at a school in Potsdam had been told that his school would be closed in June; and the dial gave at the third turn, and the lever came down under her thumb, and the door came open; and she took from the safe, in the following order, in the dark, by touch and by the memory of a night in July:

The file on her family. It was a folder of nine pages in the middle of the safe and it had been made in the first eleven days of August by an officer of the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison, and its first page was a photograph of a man of sixty-nine leaving a building on the Mokotowska Street with a satchel, taken from a window on the opposite side of the street with a lens at a distance of forty metres by a sergeant of the section who had a stolen Leica, and its second page was the addresses, and its third page was a list of eleven names of which four were members of the Stankiewicz family and one was a priest of the parish of the Holy Cross, and its ninth page was the recommendation in his own hand in the margin: *hold in reserve; the woman is worth more living than the family is worth dead.*

The evacuation and demolition schedules. They were in the cardboard cover with the ribbon, in the second part of the safe, and they were not the July plan; they were the September revision of the plan for the destruction of the city of Warsaw, made by the fortress engineers and signed by the chief of staff of the garrison on the eleventh of September 1944, and they contained the following, which the Polish state read for the first time on the twenty-fifth of September 1944, in a room on the fourth floor of a building in the Śródmieście, and thereafter at the trials in Warsaw in 1946 and 1947: the list of the buildings to be destroyed by the engineers of the garrison in the event of the evacuation of the city, which ran to eleven pages and included the Royal Castle, the bridges, the waterworks, the gasworks, the power stations, the telephone exchange, the post office on the Plac Napoleona, the University library, and the archives of the city; the schedule of the demolition charges, with the quantities of explosive at the three depots and the names of the officers responsible at each; the timetable for the evacuation of the remaining German civil administration, in four columns with the dates; and, in the third appendix, the order for the removal of the city's movable property, which had been issued by the Reich's authorities in Berlin on the eighteenth of September and which had a list of eleven categories, of which the second was: *the archives, the paintings, the libraries arid the works of art of the city of Warsaw and of its institutions, to be removed to the Reich without regard to the state of the front.*

And the last thing she took out of that safe was a small thing and it was the reason she had come: an envelope of the file of the Sicherheitspolizei's office in Warsaw, stamped inwards on the eleventh of August 1944, containing two pages that Kalbach had sent to von Hagen in July in the ordinary course of the rivalry of two offices, which were the beginning of the investigation of the woman of the villa on the Frascati Street, and which contained, in a German hand, the specification of the pearls and the paste, the description of the woman's handwriting specimen, and a single line of conclusion: *the office requests the assistance of the counter-intelligence section in the identification of the woman of the household; the case is open; the case is not closed; in time.*

She put all of it inside her shirt, against her skin, in a bundle, and closed the safe, and turned the dial with the back of her hand, and hung the lithograph back on its hook by touch, and then she went to the desk and opened the top left drawer.

It opened with a small, dry, wooden sound, and there is no way to describe what was in that drawer except to describe it in the order in which her hands found it in the dark; and what her hands found was: a kid glove of hers, of the winter of 1944, with a small tear at the thumb; a letter from Odile Boin of the rue Saint-Honoré in Paris, dated the nineteenth of December 1943, about a fitting, in French; a hairpin; a photograph of a woman of about thirty-five in a garden with two children, which was the wife; a Walther pistol of the model of 1938, loaded, with the safety catch on; and a silver compact with a false floor and one tablet in it, which had cost nine hundred francs in the rue de la Paix and had been given to her by a man with a stick in a church in Paris, with the instruction that she was not to be taken alive and talking.

She took the pistol and the compact out of the drawer and put the other things back in the drawer, in the order in which they had been: the glove, the letter, the hairpin, the photograph of the wife and the children; and then she stood in the middle of the study in the dark with the pistol in her right hand and the papers against her skin, and looked at the embers of the fire, and did the arithmetic of the next four minutes with the whole of her mind, in the way in which she had been trained to do arithmetic, which is without any emotion at all:

***

He was awake when she came back into the bedroom, and he was standing at the foot of the bed.

The moonlight came in through the gap in the blackout, thirty centimetres of it, and in it she could see the following: a man of fifty-four in a dressing gown with his hair down on his forehead and his feet bare on a floor of a house whose ground floor was his and whose roof was gone, with his hands at his sides, and no weapon, and no way to ring a bell in a house whose telephone had been dead since the second of August; and she could see, in the grey light, that he had been awake for some time, and that he had decided something, and that the thing he had decided was in his face and was the one thing in the whole of the eighteen months that she had never once seen there, which was comprehension.

"You took the picture off the hook," he said.

"Yes."

"How long have you known the combination?"

"Since the thirty-first of July. You said it out loud in this house, in a room with me in it, at eleven o'clock at night, with the door of the study open, and I was standing at the corner of the desk with a cup of coffee in my hand."

"That is very good," said Wolfram von Hagen, in German, in the voice in which he had said *precisely* eleven hundred times, and took one step forward; and the whole of what followed lasted about nine seconds.

He was faster than a man of fifty-four has any right to be; the whole of his youth had been spent in the cavalry school and the whole of his middle age in a saddle, and the arm that came up in the grey light was an arm that had been trained to take a man off a horse; and the pistol came up in her right hand, at a distance of about two metres, and the safety catch was on.

He got his left hand on her right wrist and his right hand on her throat and drove her back into the wall of the corridor doorway with the whole of his weight; and the pistol, in that grip, with her wrist turned, went off into the floorboards four centimetres from his left boot; and the second shot, which is the one that matters in every account of every killing of that kind, was fired by a woman of twenty-four with a man's hand on her throat, a pistol in her right hand at an angle of about forty degrees, and a training in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement in Paris in the autumn of 1942 in which a man with a stick had made her fire eleven hundred rounds at a hanging sack and had told her, every single time, in the same dry voice, the same sentence: *the woman who fires twice is the woman who lives.*

The round entered under the left collarbone and went down and to the right, through the upper lobe of the left lung and the aorta, and the whole of the medical content of it is that; and Wolfram von Hagen came down on to his left knee in the corridor doorway of his own bedroom with his hand still on her wrist, and then on to the floor, and then lay on his right side on the boards with his left hand up against the frame of the door, in a gesture that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna looked at for about four seconds and never once, in the whole of the rest of her life, in any of the eleven countries in which she lived afterwards, was able to describe to anybody; and he looked up at her, and his mouth moved once.

She knelt down on the floor beside him with the pistol in her hand and her shirt open and the bundle of papers from the safe of that house against her skin, and she said, in Polish, into the ear of a dying Prussian officer of the counter-intelligence section of the garrison of Warsaw, at four o'clock in the morning of the twenty-fourth of September 1944, the last words of that whole arrangement:

"My name is Krystyna."

"Krystyna," said von Hagen, in Polish, with the whole of the vowel of his accent at the wrong end of the word, because he had learned the word eleven minutes ago; and then he said one more thing in German, which was not a name and was not a question and was not a curse, and which is not in anybody's account of the matter, because the only person who ever heard it died before she ever told it to anybody.

***

She stood in the study for twenty minutes doing the practical things, and the practical things were these: she wiped the pistol with the hem of her shirt and left it in his right hand, in the corridor doorway, and closed his fingers on it with her own; she took the paybook and the identity card and the wallet out of the tunic on the chair in the bedroom and put them in the pocket of her trousers, because a German officer's identity papers on a woman crossing a line are one kind of paper and no papers at all are another; and out of the wallet she took the money and the card of the hospital of the garrison and left, on the desk in the study, where a woman making an inventory of the room would find it and would understand nothing at all, the letter of the seventeenth of January 1943 out of a cellar at Gumrak, on the sixth page of which a boy of nineteen had written two lines about a dog.

And at twenty past four in the morning of the twenty-fourth of September 1944, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, the woman who had come into that house in the first week of March at half past eight in the evening in a grey coat with her hand on the banister rail, went out of it through the kitchen, and across the garden, and out through the hole that a shell had made in the wall at the end of it, with the destruction of the city of Warsaw against her skin under her shirt, and 1,200 rounds of nothing much left in the morning's arithmetic, and one tablet in a silver compact in her trouser pocket.

And she was four metres from the wall, in the garden of the neighbouring house, in the dark, when the light of a fire two streets away came up over the roofs and showed her the following three things at once: that the gate of the villa was open; that the sentry box at the gate was empty; and that there was a car standing at the corner of the street with its lights off, and that a man was sitting in the front passenger seat of that car with the door open and his feet on the running board, reading a small notebook by the light of a burning city four hundred metres away, and that he was a man of about thirty-eight years of age in a civilian overcoat with a hat that was two years out of fashion and spectacles with a repaired frame.

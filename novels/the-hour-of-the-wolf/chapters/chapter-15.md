# Chapter 15: The Hunter's Notebook

Sturmbannführer Bruno Kalbach came to Warsaw on the eighth of May 1944 on the night train from Kraków, in a second-class carriage with two suitcases, a box of files and a hatbox, and stood on the platform at the Ostbahnhof for a moment in the fog with the collar of his civilian overcoat turned up and the whole of the station shouting at him in two languages, and thought, with a satisfaction he would not have admitted to anybody, that he had waited eighteen months for this city.

He was thirty-eight years old and he looked like a man of fifty in a way that was not ageing but a kind of grey efficiency: small, neat, with a soft face, spectacles with a thread-repaired frame, and hands that were always occupied, if not with paper then with a handkerchief or a spectacle lens or the rim of a hat. He had been in the Sicherheitspolizei since 1935 and in its counter-intelligence sections since 1939, and before the war he had been a clerk in a notary's office in Vienna, and in 1938, in March and April and May of 1938, he had been the officer who catalogued what was taken out of the flats of the Jewish families of that city, in the presence of their own caretakers, in writing, in triplicate, in the correct order, and it was the only work of his life that he remembered in detail: the flat of the professor in the Berggasse with the piano and eleven thousand books, the flat of the dentist on the Wollzeile with the silver and the photographs, the flat of a widow of eighty on the Schottenring with the linen in a cupboard that had been her mother's, item by item, the list in his own hand, the caretaker's signature, the van.

That work had taught him two things which he had never once found to be wrong in six years: that a person's life can be read off the objects in a room, and that the reading is not cleverness, it is patience.

The office was on the second floor of the building on the aleja Szucha with the window on the lindens, and it had been a room with a view since 1940 and had been occupied in that time by three different officers of the section, each of whom had left something: a map, a chair, a smell of eau de cologne. The lindens outside were in leaf in May and they were full of birds at six in the morning and the birds were the reason he asked, on the first day, for the window to be closed and the latch wired, which the clerk noted in the day book of the office as an eccentricity and which the typists discussed at breakfast, in the canteen, over the ersatz coffee, where Kalbach ate his meals for the first week because he had no establishment of his own and nobody to eat with.

He had eleven people under him, of whom four were watching groups and six were clerks, and he had the whole of a box from Paris.

The box was the material of the case that had been opened on the avenue Foch on the fourteenth of January 1944 by a man named Kalbach, who had had a hypothesis, and had had it confirmed three times since in ways that would not stand in any court, and who had then had the whole case transferred to Warsaw for the reason that he had stated in the memorandum himself, in writing, to his own superiors, in the following words: *the material is not in the west; the material is where the woman comes from.*

He did not believe in the woman.

That is the thing to understand about him, and it is the thing that made him dangerous in a way that no policeman in Paris had been: he had no theory of a person called Fräulein. He had a set of anomalies in a set of documents. He had a chart of access, and a page of linguistic evidence a professional would have thrown away, and a question mark, and eleven interviews, and a day book, and no name at all; and what he did in his first three weeks in Warsaw was not to hunt. It was to write the whole of it out again, in his own hand, in nine pages, in a hotel room on the Krucza Street by a lamp, because he had found in 1938 that material which has been arranged by other people is material which has been ruined by other people's opinions.

He walked the city for four days with a notebook, in the manner of a man taking an inventory of a room he intends to live in, and what he saw was this.

He saw that the street names were German on the tram stops and Polish on the corners, in chalk, re-written at night by somebody; that the walls of the Śródmieście carried four kinds of writing — the German proclamations, the posters of the executions, the small hand-chalked marks of the underground, and, around the food offices, the pencil marks of a hundred private arrangements about bread — and that a Pole walking down a street looked at the walls the way a farmer looks at the sky; and he understood, standing on the corner of the Marszałkowska on his second evening with his hat in his hand, that he had come to a city which had been reading itself for four years, and that the whole of his method, which was reading, would have to be done here in a language he could not read.

He saw the ruins of the ghetto from the tram on the Bonifraterska, and looked at them for the length of a city block, and wrote in his notebook: *brick. guards on the north. the market uses the cellars. no information of value can be obtained here; the population that knew the district is destroyed.* It was the most nearly human sentence he wrote in his first month, and he did not know that it was.

He saw the queues: for bread, for water, for the registration of documents, for the tram; the queue at the corner of the Żelazna for the chemist; and the queue at the window of the Pawiak on the Dzielna Street, at which women stood from six in the morning with parcels and a name, and which told him more about the city than any file, because a queue of women outside a prison is a queue of people who believe there is a point in it.

He saw an execution notice go up: a boy of about sixteen pasting a sheet on the kiosk at the corner, and four people reading it, and a woman going past it without turning her head, which is a discipline, and an old man stopping in front of it and reading the whole of it with his lips moving, which is not.

And on the fourth day he noticed the tail: a woman in a dark suit with a canvas satchel, on the tram behind his, twice in one week, in two different coats, with her hair cut short and badly and a face like a locked door. He noted her in the notebook, and did nothing, because a man who is being watched is a man who is being watched by somebody worth watching, and because in that city a police officer with a tail on his tram was, in his own estimate, six weeks of quiet work away from meeting the one adversary in Warsaw who could be relied upon to have a method.

***

The nine pages were a chart, and the chart was the whole of the case.

The first page was a list of everything that had leaked from the German administration in France between June 1943 and February 1944, in the order in which it had leaked: the coastal sector's inspection schedule; the convoy route on the eleventh of December; the two dates at Saint-Nazaire; the fuel allocations of the Fifteenth Army in the second half of January; and, at the bottom, in the margin, in Kalbach's hand, the note that none of it had ever appeared in the British or American press, which meant that the receiving end was a service and not a newspaper, and that the service was organised, which meant that it had a country.

The second page was the list of the rooms in which those particular documents had passed. There were forty-one names on that list, and thirty-nine of them were German, and the two that were not were a French typist in the administration's supply office who had been dismissed in January 1944 for theft, and a Polish woman in the office of the Hôtel Majestic's administration, and beside those two names Kalbach had written, in pencil: *check, in time*.

The third page was the traffic: the hours at which the material had left Paris, inferred from the dates of the London signals that the wireless section had intercepted in the spring of 1943 and had not understood until now; and the inference was that the material left the city, in general, between nine in the evening and six in the morning, and that the source therefore had a house to go to at night.

The fourth page was language.

There were three items on it, and the third was the waiter's memory of an accent with a Warsaw consonant in it, and above that item Kalbach had written a single question in his small hand: *where is the woman from?*

The fifth page was the day book.

It was not Rutz's day book — he had not seen that yet — but the concierge's ledger from the house on the avenue Henri-Martin in Paris, which had been obtained in January by a man in a grey overcoat who had asked for it and had been given it, and which was a French concierge's book of the arrivals and departures of a household, kept by a woman of sixty-two in the ordinary way, with one peculiarity: that a column had been added to it in pencil in January 1943, headed *heures des sorties*, and that the entries in that column were in the same hand as the rest of the ledger.

The concierge had explained the column in two sentences in her interview. She had said that a gentleman of the administration had told her, in the January of the second year, that the household was under an enquiry into the petrol and that the hours at which the young lady of the house went out and came in were to be written down; and that the gentleman had been a German in a grey overcoat, and that he had been very polite, and that she had never seen him again; and Kalbach, reading the interview in the file in his room in the Krucza Street, had underlined the two sentences twice, because they were the whole of the case in miniature: somebody in that city had already been running a day book on the woman, in 1943, from inside the German administration, and the somebody had never been identified by any office.

The sixth, seventh and eighth pages were the interviews he had ordered in his first two weeks in Warsaw, which were three.

The first was with the Polish maid at the villa on the Frascati Street, a woman of forty-five called Janina Prus, who was brought to the Szucha in the ordinary way, in a car, at nine in the morning, having been met at the corner of the Bracka by two men with a paper, and who was questioned for two hours and forty minutes without being touched and without being made to stand, in a room on the second floor, in the presence of a typist, and who said, in the end, having been asked eleven different ways about the young lady of the house: that the young lady was quiet, that the young lady did not go out in the evenings, that the young lady never asked her questions about the household, that the young lady had asked her once about her brother in the Reich and had not written anything down, and that the young lady had never once, in four months, been in a room with a Pole for longer than it takes to give an instruction.

Kalbach wrote down the last answer in his notebook, twice, and did not believe one word of it; and he was right not to believe it, and he was wrong, which was the shape of the whole of his war: the woman was telling the truth she had decided on in the corridor on the way in, and it was a good truth, and it had been made in the four minutes that the car had waited at the corner while the driver finished his cigarette, and Kalbach had no instrument in the world for the difference between a lie and a decision.

The second interview was with a pawnbroker of the Marszałkowska Street, conducted by his own men in the shop, in the ordinary way, with a receipt: the man was seventy, had a bad eye, and was frightened of nothing and nobody, and he told them, when they showed him a photograph of a necklace of the same pattern as the one recorded in the inventory of the household property of the villa on the Frascati Street, that he had never seen such a necklace in his life, and that his records were in the book, and that they were welcome to the book; and after the two men had gone he thought about it for four days, because a jeweller's conscience is a particular instrument, and on the fifth day he went to a priest, and the priest told him to say nothing and to keep the money in the tin, and he said nothing, and he kept the money in the tin, for eleven months, until the end.

The third interview was with the assistant of a jeweller on the Kercelak, who had been a seller of imitations for eleven years, and who said, in the way of a man in that trade talking to two men in coats, that he sold *a great many* strands of imitations, and that in the last week of April he had sold a strand of forty-one pearls, of the colour of skimmed milk, with a clasp of silver plate, to a lady in a grey coat and good boots, and that she had paid nine hundred zlotys and had not argued about the price, and that he had been told to put the strand in a box with a silk cord, and that she had asked him whether the clasp could be made to take *a better stone*, and that he had said no, because it was plate.

And Kalbach, sitting in a hotel room in Warsaw in May 1944, with the two reports on the table in front of him, wrote in his own hand on the eighth page a single sentence, in German, which is the sentence that closes the middle of this book:

*Item: pearls sold. Item: paste bought. Note: a woman who pawns a gift from a rich man in April and buys paste in the same week is a woman with a reason, and the reason is not jewellery.*

***

The interview with the orderly was on the nineteenth of May, in a room on the ground floor of the Szucha, and it took an hour and ten minutes, and it was conducted with the entire courtesy that Kalbach kept for soldiers of the old army, which is a courtesy of a kind that no civilian in Europe could have resisted, and Rutz did not resist it for a second.

He was fifty-one years old, from a village near Bamberg, and he had been twelve years in the army and four years with the Oberst, and he sat on the chair with his cap on his knees and answered every question in the way in which a Bavarian soldier answers a policeman, which is generously, and with the assumption, entirely correct, that he had done nothing wrong and that the answers could not possibly be interesting to anybody.

The car's mileage, the hours, the wine, the silver, the laundry, the Colonel's health, the Colonel's temper, the Fräulein's shopping, the Fräulein's hat. Did the Fräulein go out alone; when; how often; did she come back with anything; did she ever meet anybody; did the orderly know any of the Fräulein's friends in the city; had the Fräulein ever asked him to take her anywhere that had surprised him.

And then, at the end of the hour and ten minutes, with the whole of it written down and the typist's carbon in the tray, Kalbach asked the last question, which was not a question about the case at all, and which he had put at the end of every interview in his career since 1938, because the answer to it is always of the same kind:

"Is there anything about the Fräulein that has ever struck you as odd, Herr Rutz? Anything at all — a habit, a word, an accent. I ask everybody this. It is a formality."

And Rutz, who had been six months of his life in a car with the same woman, who liked her, who wrote her hours in a notebook, who had a wife in Bavaria and a photograph of a niece in the windscreen of the Mercedes, and who had nothing whatever in his soul that could have imagined why the question was being asked, laughed slightly, and said: "Only that she says a Polish word sometimes. Odd, that. She is Viennese — you can hear it when she is tired — and when she is tired she says *bułka* for a bread roll, which is Polish, if you say it my way; and one evening at the table in Paris last year she said it to the cook. It gave me a turn. My grandmother was from Posen and she said it like that, and I have not heard it since 1931."

Kalbach wrote it down.

He wrote it down in the small notebook, in the small hand, and said thank you, and stood up, and shook the orderly's hand, and had the door opened for him, and stood in the corridor of the second floor of the building on the aleja Szucha for a moment with the notebook in his hand, and read back the sentence, and thought about the fifth page of the nine pages in the room on the Krucza Street, on which there was a waiter's memory of an accent with a Warsaw consonant in it.

The word did not prove anything. That was the point about it, and it was the reason it was worth so much: a woman of Vienna who says a Polish word for a bread roll when she is tired is a woman with a Polish grandmother, or a Polish nanny, or a Polish year somewhere, and there were two hundred thousand such women in the Reich and half a million in the part of the world that was called Austria and had been called something else before it. It would not stand. It would be laughed out of a room by any lawyer in the Reich. But a man who is building a chart of anomalies does not want a proof: he wants a nail, and the word was a nail, and it went into the fifth page beside the waiter's accent and beside the file's question mark, and there it joined the rest of the ironwork, and afterward it never came out again.

***

The interview with the Oberst was on the twenty-second of May in the study of the villa on the Frascati Street, and it lasted fifty minutes, and the whole of it was a chess game in which one of the players had a notebook and no authority at all, and the other had an army and no evidence at all.

Kalbach came with three things: the pawnbroker's report as his men had written it up, the Kercelak seller's report, and a page of the inventory of the villa's effects as it had been made on the requisition in June 1940, and he put the three of them on the desk in an order, and sat with his hat on his knees, and began, in the manner of a man who is being deferential about the fact that his business is small.

"Oberst, with your permission: the household of the Fräulein. We are tidying a file that came from Paris. There are two pieces of paper. The first is a jeweller's statement that a strand of pearls matching a necklace said to be in the possession of the young lady of this house was sold on the Marszałkowska in April. The second is a statement of the same week that a strand of imitations of the same pattern was bought on the Kercelak." He looked at his own hands, and then at the Oberst, with the most modest expression available to a man with spectacles. "There is nothing in either piece of paper. Either of them alone is worth nothing at all. Together they are a question, and the question is what a lady in this household was doing with two strands."

Von Hagen listened to the whole of it with his hands flat on the desk, and did not look at the papers, and did not pick them up, and when Kalbach had finished he leaned back in his chair and said, in the tone of a man who has been asked about the price of a horse by a man who cannot ride:

"Sturmbannführer. In 1942 I was in Russia with the Sixth Army's supply, and in 1943 I was in Paris, and I have been in this city since March. In each of those places I have had, at one time or another, a woman in my household, and in each of those places a man with a card index has come to me with two pieces of paper and a question. In Paris it was a set of railway tickets; in Russia it was a doctor's certificate. I did not answer either of them, and I shall not answer this one, and I am going to explain to you why, and you are going to write it down, because I have been told that you write everything down.

"The woman in question is an Austrian national of no political interest whatever, certified by the Auswärtiges Amt in Berlin in February by a procedure that took three days and which costs a favour; she is employed as a clerk in my section and is paid from the salary book of the garrison command; she is in my house and in my bed, and that is a matter between me and my conscience and my tailor. Her hours are known to my orderly, who writes them down; her movements are in the day book of this house so that anybody who wants to know them may know them. In the last three months she has been in this house, my office, the homes of four officers of the garrison, the cathedral on the Świętokrzyska, a café on the Nowy Świat, and the dress shop of a woman on the Bracka; every one of those places is a German place, every one of them can be checked, and every one of them has a German in it who will remember her hat." He stood up. "There is no case. There is a clerk with two pieces of paper and a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is welcome in my section as an intellectual exercise and is not welcome in my house as an insult. Do you understand me, Sturmbannführer?"

"I understand you, Oberst," said Kalbach, and stood up, and put on his hat, and said, in the same modest voice: "May I ask one thing, for the file, and then I shall go? It is not about the young lady. It is about this house. Is there, in this house, an orderly's book, of the kind that Furiers keep?"

And von Hagen, who had won, and who was a Prussian and knew what a day book was and had never in his life considered that his own orderly's notebook could be anything but a piece of paper about petrol, said: "There is. Rutz keeps one. He will show it to you on your way out, and you will read it in the hall, and you will give it back to him, and you will not take it away with you, and you will report to me in writing what you found in it, which will be nothing."

"Of course," said Kalbach, and did exactly that; and read the day book standing in the hall of the villa for eleven minutes with his hat under his arm, and wrote down, in his small notebook, in the small hand, with the pencil moving along the page like a man reading a timetable: the hours, the days, the eleven occasions in April and May, the eleven occasions on which the lady of the house had gone out on foot and come back within the hour from the direction of the Hoża Street, where there is no shop that a lady in a German household has any reason whatever to visit.

***

The ninth page was written on the Krucza Street on the night of the twenty-sixth of May 1944, by a lamp, with the window open because the lindens in the Szucha had given him a headache, and it was not a page of a report. It was a page of a letter to himself, of the kind he had written every month since 1939 and had never sent to anybody, in which he put down what he thought, as opposed to what he proposed, and kept it in the drawer of whatever desk he had, and burned it when he moved.

*The case in France was a woman. The case in France is now a woman in Warsaw, and the two are the same woman, and I am the only person in this city who believes it. I have a word, and an accent, and two strands, and eleven walks in the direction of a street with nothing in it. I have nothing that would survive an hour in front of a man like von Hagen, and I have made an enemy of him by looking at his house, which is a thing that I shall pay for in due course and shall not avoid paying for, because the alternative is to know the answer and to leave the file in a drawer. In time.

*My method has always been the same and it does not fail except when a man loves. In Vienna I read the flats: three hundred and eleven of them in four months and I was never once wrong. What is in a room is in the room because a person put it there, and a person puts a thing in a room for one of a hundred reasons and all of them are legible. But a man in love is a man who will lie for a woman in a letter, in a report, in a court, in a war, and he will do it not because he has been bribed but because the account in his head has been rewritten, and there is no instrument for the reading of a rewritten account.

*Therefore the woman: the pearls, the paste, the word, the accent, the walks. Nothing.

*Therefore not the woman. Therefore the account.

*Therefore: the surveillance is routine and shall not be theatrical: the house, the car's routine, the woman's routine in writing, by two men on bicycles, and the day book once a month from the orderly, with the Colonel's approval in writing, which I shall obtain by going over his head to the garrison's Ia with an argument about petrol. And one thing more, which no office in this city has ever done and which is the whole of the idea, and I have not seen it in any file since Paris: I shall require the identification of every Pole who has been in the same room with this woman for longer than ten minutes without a German present. Every one. Shop assistants, dressmakers, hairdressers, clerks, the staff of the offices she has been in, the servants of the houses she has visited. In the last four months: eleven such rooms. In the next four months: there will be more, and there will be a pattern, and in a pattern of Poles there is always one name that appears twice in two places that do not touch; and when I have it I shall not need pearls and I shall not need paste and I shall not need to argue with a Prussian about his house.*

He wrote it out, and read it, and put it in the drawer, and then wrote a second sheet, which was the order, in the correct form, to the officer commanding the second section: *Surveillance, routine, minimum two watchers, in writing, twice weekly. One additional name on the watch list, approved by me on the ground of the day book of the household of the Oberst: any German officer of the garrison who is seen on foot in the vicinity of Hoża Street on two or more occasions within thirty days is to be noted, with the hour and the date, and no action taken. Report fortnightly. In time.*

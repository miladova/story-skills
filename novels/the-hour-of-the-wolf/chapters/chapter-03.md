# Chapter 3: The Ledger of Small Hours

There were four chairs in the corridor outside the office of the Chef der Militärverwaltung on the third floor of the Hôtel Majestic, and three of them were occupied by Frenchmen with briefcases, and the fourth, for three hours on the second of December 1943, was occupied by a woman in a grey dress who had been told to wait and who had discovered, in the course of a great many hours of waiting in a great many buildings, that waiting was the only form of intelligence work in which a woman was never suspected of being engaged.

Nobody looks at a woman who is waiting. She is furniture with a handbag. In three hours she learned: that the corridor's second door on the left was used by the officers of the transport section, because the man who came out of it had a timetable under his arm and a pencil behind his ear; that the third door on the right was the office of the administration's legal adviser, because two lawyers and a priest went in and none came out smiling; that the courier who arrived at ten past eleven with a leather satchel and left at twenty past, twenty-four, and forty-one past, was a despatch rider from the Kommandantur, and that he was sleeping with one of the two secretaries in the typing pool, because he looked at the blonde one and the blonde one did not look at him and both of them were very careful about the door.

That the SD's man had been in the building that morning, because he had left his card on the table for the officer of the day, and the card was picked up by a clerk who read it, looked at no one, and put it in his pocket, which is what every clerk in France did with a card from the avenue Foch.

And that at eleven o'clock, an hour and a half into her wait, an officer came down the corridor in a civilian overcoat with a hat in his hand, and glanced at the four chairs with the glancing of a man who inventories everything, and looked at her for perhaps two seconds longer than a German officer looks at a waiting woman, and went out through the main door, and she sat with her hands in her lap and her face empty and did not move until he had been gone half a minute.

At half past twelve a Hauptmann came out and said, "Fräulein Herschlag? The Colonel will see you now," in the voice of a man interrupting a holiday, and she stood up, and gathered her bag, and went in.

The office was too large for the desk. That was the first thing about it and the thing she came to understand as the man himself: it had been the sitting room of a suite built for a family that had employed nine servants, and the German administration had put a desk in the middle of it and a map on the wall and a table with four telephones, and left everything else exactly as it was, so that an Oberst in the service of the Reich sat with his back to a marble fireplace that had been carved with a hunting scene, doing his paperwork under a plaster ceiling with a naked woman in it.

He had been in there since half past ten. He looked up when she came in and did the one thing she had learned to dread in him, which was to look at her for a moment as an object of value before he remembered that she was a person.

"My dear. Sit down. Have you eaten?"

"I have eaten."

"You have not eaten, you never eat, everyone in this building tells me you never eat, they watch you at the receptions like a laboratory experiment." He pushed a plate across the desk: sandwiches of real ham on real bread, cut into triangles, with a sprig of parsley on them, in a building in a city where the ration for an adult Frenchman in that week was two hundred and fifty grams of bread a day, and children in the twentieth arrondissement were being sent to the country to drink milk that the Germans had not requisitioned because there were not enough lorries. "Sit down. I am going to dictate and you are going to be useful, which you have not been for eleven days."

She sat down, and got out her notebook, and was useful.

He dictated four letters. He dictated like a man reading a poem he did not admire: flat, accurate, with the verbs at the end, and pauses at the end of paragraphs in which he looked at the map or out of the window or at the corner of the room where the photograph was not. One letter to a quartermaster in Nancy about a requisitioned hotel; one to his brother about the Pomeranian timber, in which he wrote a fair price and an unfair sentence and crossed out the sentence; one to the wife of a colleague about the death of the colleague's brother, which was four paragraphs long and contained no sentiment whatever, and which was, she thought, the most German document she had ever handled, because every one of the four paragraphs said in a different way that the man would be replaced; and one, the last, dictated more slowly than the others, to a schoolmaster in a village near Magdeburg.

*Dear Herr Direktor, You will recall my son Klaus, who was in your school from 1931 to 1936 and who was an indifferent scholar in mathematics and a first-rate scholar on the subject of horses. I am informed that the school intends a memorial for the former pupils, and I have been asked for a photograph and a short paragraph. I send the photograph. For the paragraph I find that I have nothing to say that would not embarrass the school, and I would be grateful if you would write it yourself. If it would assist you I can tell you that he was not afraid of anything except failure, and that he was frightened of failure to the last day of his life, in January, at Stalingrad, at the age of nineteen, and that this was not a fault of the school.*

He stopped. The room was quiet in the way of a very large room with a marble fireplace and two telephones and a man in it who has stopped talking.

"Read that back," he said.

She read it back, twice, and he changed one word — *first-rate* to *good* — and told her to add a sentence of condolence from himself to the Direktor's family, and she wrote it in her notebook, in her own hand, which she permitted to be a little clumsy at the ends of words because a woman who writes too well is remembered.

"Now the file," he said, and pushed a folder across the desk with his two fingers, and she took it and opened it, because this was what she was for: an officer's mistress who could type in German was worth the taking; an officer's mistress who could type in German and file was worth keeping.

Inside were the papers of an argument. She did not read them — she never read anything in his presence except what he asked her to read — but she turned them, and sorted them, and put them into the order of their dates, and in the doing she took in, through her fingers, four sheets that mattered: two memoranda from the Militärbefehlshaber's office in Warsaw about the state of the bridges on the Vistula; a copy of a report on the security situation in the Warsaw district, in the language of the office, in which the phrase *these persons are to be reckoned with* occurred three times; and, on the last sheet, a list of three names in a hand she did not know, in which the second was the name of a man appointed to the Warsaw garrison command, and the first was a name in the fourth line of a paragraph about a police commander who had been shot in the street in Warsaw in the winter and about the reprisals that had followed.

She had it all inside a quarter of an hour, through her fingertips, in a room with a marble fireplace, while the man who had dictated the letter about his son sat and looked at the map and drank ersatz coffee and told her, once, without looking round, that the heating in the building was a scandal.

At one o'clock the telephone rang, and he answered it, and she got out of the chair because the tone told her to.

"An hour," he said. "At four. Bring a hat. There is tea."

"But the fit—"

"The fit will wait. There is a gentleman in the building from the Sicherheitspolizei who has asked to be introduced to you, and I have agreed, because agreeing costs nothing and disagreeing costs a file." He turned back to the map, and then he stopped, and he said the last thing that happened in that room before she left it, and it was not to her, and it was as flat as everything he said. "It is the third time he has asked. First the office, then the street, and now my house, in three weeks. A man who asks three times is not curious, he is methodical, and I do not like methodical men in other people's departments. Go and be charming. Do not be interesting."

***

The tea was in a room on the second floor with a portrait of a French banker over the fireplace, and there were eleven people at it, and by the time Krystyna sat down she had already made her assessment of the two men standing at the window, and the assessment was not comfortable, because the one she had been brought to meet was not the one who told her so.

He was perhaps thirty-seven, perhaps forty, in a grey civilian suit, medium, ordinary, with brown hair that had no grey in it and a face that had been designed, she thought, by a committee whose subject had been a bank clerk, and he was cleaning his spectacles with the corner of a handkerchief when she came in, and he put them on and stood up and bowed his head a fraction of an inch, and said, "Fräulein Herschlag. Bruno Kalbach."

"How do you do."

"I do very well. May I get you something? The tea here is not tea. There is a Frenchman at the end of the corridor who makes something he calls coffee, and which is not coffee, but which is hot."

"I should like the thing that is hot and called coffee."

"So should we all," Kalbach said, and went and got it, and brought it back himself rather than letting the orderly do it, which was the first of what she was to number, over the next ten months, as twenty-three acts of consideration.

The conversation lasted thirty-one minutes and she could have reproduced every word of it for the rest of her life from memory, and did, in other rooms, on other evenings, in the bath at the flat in the ninth, turning it over and over the way a woman turns over a stone in a stream to see what is on the underside.

He began with the weather and the price of coal, and went on to the ballet, and told her about a performance of *Giselle* he had seen in Vienna in 1938, in a way that made her understand that he had been in Vienna in March 1938 and that he had seen everything else that was there that month, and then he asked her the four questions he had been waiting to ask, in the right order, in the way of a man who had spent the morning arranging them:

Where had she been at school?

Vienna, at the Institute of the Sacred Heart on the Rennweg, until sixteen, and then, because her father's health had gone and the money with it, in a lycée in Lausanne for two years, where she had been very badly taught French by a woman from Bern and learned to swim.

Which of those two places did she think of as home?

Lausanne. Vienna was the city in which she had been a child, and she was not a child any longer, and to be in Vienna in 1943 was to be walked through a museum of her own mistakes.

Did she play the piano? He had heard she sang.

She sang a little, and played only well enough to accompany herself, and had been taught, oddly enough, by a Galician woman who had been in service in her mother's house and who had taught her the songs of a country she had never seen.

And her father's health?

He had a heart, she said, that was older than the rest of him, and he had died at Herschlag in 1939, and her mother had gone in 1941, and she was an only child, which was why she found herself, in Paris, in 1943, with no one to whom she had any obligation at all.

"Which is an enviable condition," Kalbach said.

"It is a lonely one."

"The two are usually the same," he said, and looked at her with his spectacles on and his hands folded round his cup, and there was nothing whatever in his face; and then he said the thing she carried home and turned over in the bath, because it was not a question, it was an observation, and observations are what hunters leave in the forest to see which animals will step over them. "We have a very peculiar sort of thief in this building, Fräulein Herschlag. I have been reading his file all afternoon. He has been at it for two years and he has never once taken a thing that could be sold, and he has never once been late, and he has never once made a mistake that we can see. In Vienna, in 1938, I catalogued the contents of eleven hundred flats, and every single person in them believed that they had made no mistakes either. What do you think of that?"

"I think," Krystyna said, "that you must be very good at cards."

Kalbach did not laugh. He said, "I am very bad at cards," and finished his not-coffee, and stood up, and thanked her for the conversation, and bowed again, that fraction of an inch, and left the room.

And the second man, who had been standing at the window the whole time with his back to the room and his hands behind him, turned round when the door had shut behind Kalbach, and looked at the place where Kalbach had been sitting, and said, "Well. What did the little clerk want?"

"To know where I was at school," Krystyna said.

"Everyone wants to know where you were at school," said Hauptmann Kurt Lindemann, of the counter-intelligence section, thirty-three, handsome in an over-designed way, with his hair done with a hot finger, and a laugh he use to cut things. "It is the curse of a beautiful woman in an occupied country. He has been at the building for three weeks and he has spoken to eleven people, and you are the first woman, which either means that he has eliminated the other women or that you are the first woman he has any reason to speak to. May I be candid?"

"You are always candid."

"Naturally. Then: that man is the most dangerous person in the German administration in France, not because he is clever, though he is, and not because he is cruel, though he is that too, but because he does not enjoy any of it. Our people enjoy it. Reinhardt enjoys it, he had his office redecorated twice; I enjoy it; even the Colonel enjoys it, in the way of a man playing a long game of chess. That man in there does not enjoy anything at all, and he has no wife, no mistress, no dog, no debts, and no vices that anyone has been able to find, and he has been in the service since 1934, and when he goes home at night, Fräulein Herschlag, I believe he goes home and reads. Do you understand what that means?"

"That he is boring."

"It means that he is the only man in this building who is exactly what he appears to be," Lindemann said. "Which means that when he looks at you, he is looking at you. Be dull, madame. Be lovely and dull. You have been given a great gift: the entire German officer corps believes that you have no head for anything, and God knows they need to believe it. Do not disabuse them."

***

The villa on the Frascati would not be requisitioned until the spring, and the villa on the avenue Henri-Martin was the house of the winter, and by eleven o'clock that night she was in it, and by half past eleven she was in the bed in it, and at midnight the Colonel came up with the smell of the office on him and the tiredness of a man of fifty-four who has been shouted at by a general on the telephone.

He lay down and put his hands behind his head and told her about Warsaw.

Not because she asked. Because there was a woman in the bed and the light was off and a man who has been shouted at by a general needs to fill a room with his own voice, and because, in the six months she had lived in his house, he had told her more about the German administration in the east than her network had been able to assemble in three years of couriers.

"There will be a new arrangement in the east," he said. "The Generalgouvernement is not an administration any longer, it is a corridor, and everything that moved into it in 1939 has to be reorganised because of what is happening on the other side of it." He said the word *reorganised* the way he said *requisitioned*: as a fact of nature. "There is a man in Warsaw who has been running the police there since the autumn, a Kutschera, an Austrian, from the SS, with the manners of a border guard and a very good record, and he has been asked to do in Warsaw what we have not done in Paris, which is to break the Underground's intelligence. Do you know what the difference is between the Underground in Poland and the Underground in France?"

"I have never been to Poland."

"No," he said. "Nobody has been to Poland. It is a country that consists of a railway station and a graveyard and a great deal of argument." He laughed, once, and it was not a laugh she had heard him use before, and she filed it. "The Poles have an army in the forest. They have a government in London with three parties in it and a division of opinion, and they have courts, and schools, and newspapers. In this country, the resistance is a hobby that the middle classes have taken up instead of the theatre. In Poland it is a state. And the state's weapon is not the bomb, it is the file. They know, my dear, the name of every policeman in Warsaw who has ever taken a bribe. So they send an Austrian with a good record, and they give him counter-intelligence and a free hand, and the polite version of his instructions, which I have read, is that the intelligence apparatus is to be dismantled and its personnel put in the ground. That is the polite version. I have seen the impolite one, and I do not intend to repeat it in a bedroom."

She lay still in the dark with her cheek on his shoulder, which was the position she slept in when she was told to.

"In the spring," he said, "the whole Abwehr in this country will be dismantled, and I have it from a man who knows, because the Admiral has lost his argument in Berlin, and because there is a document, and because this is how the Reich does everything, by document. The sections will be divided, and the officers will be distributed. When a thing is distributed, my dear, people like me are given a new address. I have been told to expect the east."

She kept her breathing exactly as it had been.

"Warsaw," he said, and the word came out of the dark at her like a hand. "I am fifty-four years old, I am a colonel of counter-intelligence, and I have broken four networks in three years, and they are sending me to Warsaw, which is a place where I shall be given a great deal of work and no thanks, because that is what the Reich does with men like me at the end of the fourth year of a war it is not winning."

"Will you go?"

"I will go where I am sent." A pause. "There is a house there, on a street called Frascati, that was requisitioned from a Jewish lawyer and is currently occupied by a man from the administration of the ghetto who is being transferred to Lublin, which is a German euphemism, my dear, and you need not look it up. It has four rooms and a garden and a safe in the study. I have been offered it, and I have accepted it, and the requisition papers arrived at the office today."

She said, because it was a question and a question can be answered without lying, "And what am I to do with a house in Warsaw?"

"You will come with me," von Hagen said.

In the dark, with her cheek on his shoulder, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna counted to eleven, and then said, in the warm, slow, foolish voice that had taken her two years to build, "Do they have a good dressmaker in Warsaw?"

He laughed — the ordinary laugh, the man-at-home laugh, the one she could steer — and put his hand on her hair.

"They have nothing in Warsaw, my dear. That is the point of it."

***

She was at the Mission on the rue Saint-Honoré at seven the next morning, because the Mission opened at six and the priest who mattered came to the early mass with his hands blue.

She gave him what she had in the order she had learned: the two memoranda about the bridges, the phrase *these persons are to be reckoned with*, the name in the second line of the list, and the fact that the German administration in the west expected its counter-intelligence apparatus to be broken up in the spring and its officers distributed — a piece of news which was, she knew, worth more to the Polish government in London than a battery or a bridge, because a government that has been in exile for four years lives on the knowledge of who is going to be standing where.

And then she said, in Polish, without any change in her voice at all: "The Colonel has been given a house in Warsaw. He is to be posted there in the spring. He has told me I will go with him."

The priest was silent for the length of three Hail Marys.

"You will go," he said at last. "That is not a question for me. It is a question for the people in London and for the people under the ground in Warsaw, and I shall send it tonight, and you will have your answer in three weeks. Until then you will do nothing whatever, and you will do it very well."

"I know what the answer will be."

"Of course you know what the answer will be. Knowing is nothing. Doing it without sentiment is everything, and you were trained by a man who was very good at that." He crossed himself, and looked at the altar, and said, in a voice that had something in it which she had not heard from him before: "There was a girl from Warsaw in this parish in 1941. She had been at a school with a cousin of mine. She had a head for languages and a mother in a hospital at Ivry, and she worked with a group of Poles in the twentieth arrondissement, and in June the Germans took eleven of them and shot six, and the six were the ones who had been betrayed by the eleventh. She was not one of the six. She was one of the eleven who were questioned and released, and she came to me afterwards, in this room, in that chair, and asked me what she was supposed to do with the rest of her life. And do you know what I told her?"

"What?"

"That God had not made her for this. It was the worst thing I have ever said to a living soul, and she went home that night and took the gas, and I have never once told anybody, except a woman I have never seen before and shall probably never see again, who is off to Warsaw in the spring with a German's papers in her bag." He stood up. "You will not be told that God did not make you for it. You will be told nothing at all. You will simply be told where to stand, and when, and what to bring, and you will not be thanked, and if you are lucky you will be old one day, in a country that exists, and you will have a great many bad nights, and that is the whole of the reward. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Then go and have your breakfast with the enemy," the priest said, "and do not come to me on Thursdays any more. The man in the hat was at the door of the building on Wednesday and again this morning. Sundays, after the eleven o'clock, in the chapel of Our Lady, where the bell cannot be heard from the street."

***

At eight o'clock, in the café on the corner of the rue de Rivoli, she ate two eggs and a piece of bread and drank a bowl of something the menu called coffee, and read the morning paper, which was full of the war in Italy and of a speech by a German field marshal about the invincibility of the Atlantic defences, and which printed, on the second page, in a box, the names of eleven men who had been executed at Fresnes in the previous week for acts of sabotage against the military authority of the Reich.

She read the names the way she read everything now: once, all the way through, and then a second time in her head, in case any of them was a name she had ever heard in any room, because if it was, there was work to be done before noon.

None of them was.

Then she put the paper down, and lit a cigarette — she had four a day and the fourth was in the evening, and she smoked them like a soldier on leave — and looked out of the window at the rue de Rivoli, at the German soldiers filing past with their rifles slung and their eyes ahead, at the woman with the pram and the coal, at the two girls on bicycles, at the gendarme who was thirty and tired and standing in the doorway of the préfecture with his hands behind his back, and she let the thing she had been holding since half past midnight sit in front of her on the table for exactly as long as a cigarette lasts.

Warsaw.

She had not let herself think the word, properly, since the platform in September 1939, and now it was on the table with a German colonel's requisitioning papers behind it and a hunter's file on the avenue Foch behind that, and she understood, with a clarity she had not asked for and did not want, that her own life had just been divided into two parts: the part in which she was a woman with a face nobody in a city knew, and the part in which she would be walking through the streets of her own childhood, in a fur coat, on the arm of the enemy, and every doorway would contain the possibility of a woman who had known her mother.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna of Warsaw had, in the four years since she left it, done the following things: crossed three countries on papers she had paid for; learned to be another woman twice, once badly and once well; memorised the coast of France; and lain in the bed of an Oberst of the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich and listened to him talk about the east.

She had also, in those four years, developed a rule for the moment when the fear arrived, which was this: the fear arrived, and she gave it one cigarette, and then she took it out of the room by the scruff of the neck and put it to work.

She finished her cigarette, and paid, and put on her gloves, and went out into the cold to the fitting on the rue de Miromesnil, which had been moved to nine because the dress was to be worn on the twenty-third and it was very far from finished, and to which she brought, folded in her bag and printed in her memory in its entirety, everything she had learned in the Hôtel Majestic the previous day: the bridges, the phrase, the names, and the fact that, in the spring, the whole apparatus of German counter-intelligence in the west was going to be taken to pieces like a watch and put back together in the east, and that among the parts being moved was a fifty-four-year-old Oberst with cracked knuckles and a dead son and a photograph with its back turned to the room, and a hunter with clean spectacles and no vices who read her the way a man reads a page of sums, and one man in the Polish desk of the Hôtel Lutetia whose name she did not know and whom, in twenty-nine days of absolute obedience to her own instructions, she had managed not to meet.

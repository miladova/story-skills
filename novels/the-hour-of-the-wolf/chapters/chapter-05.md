# Chapter 5: The Boy Who Kept Her Name

The corridor was eleven metres long and had four doors in it and a mirror at the far end, and in the mirror she could see the two of them: a captain of the Abwehr on the top step with his cap in his hands, and a woman with a pearl necklace standing in front of a cloakroom counter with a brass ticket in her fingers, and between them, on the wall, a French clock of the sort that the German administration did not bother to wind.

She had exactly one second, and she used it the way she had been taught to use a second: she took in his hands, his boots, the absence of a ring, the way he stood, the way he had said the word, and she arrived at three possible answers and rejected two of them, and then she heard her own voice come out of her, warm, unhurried, with a lift at the end of it, in the accent of a woman who had been brought up in Vienna by a notary and badly educated in Lausanne by a woman from Bern.

"I beg your pardon?"

The captain did not move.

"You have the advantage of me," she said. "I am afraid I do not—" and she let the sentence trail, and smiled the specific smile that had been built for exactly this, in a room above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement, over four days, in front of a mirror, while a girl from Lille watched her and told her when the eyes went wrong.

The man came down the two steps and stopped three metres from her, and the old woman in the grey overall, who had been listening with the total attention of the nearly blind, pushed the counter flap down and shuffled out to the far end of the corridor to be nearer to whatever was going to happen.

"My apologies," he said, in French, with an accent that had been formed in a classroom in Warsaw and corrected in Hamburg, and which she had last heard in an orchard with the flies going up off a pond in the summer of 1939. "You are extremely like someone I knew. It is a great impertinence."

"It is not the first time," she said. "I have one of those faces."

"You have not got one of those faces."

"Then I am sorry to be a disappointment."

"You are not," he said, and he looked at her for one more second, and then he did the thing that took the whole of her professional armour off her, the way a man takes a coat off a chair: he stopped. He stopped looking, and he bowed his head an inch, and he stepped back, and he said, "I hope you will forgive me. I have just come off a train."

And she stood there with her pearls and her ticket and her training, and she understood that she had walked out of the room in which she was safe. Because a man who insists is a fool, and a fool can be managed; and a man who is embarrassed is a gentleman, and a gentleman can be lied to; and this man had stopped, not because he believed her, but because he had decided — she watched him decide it, in a corridor, at half past one in the morning — that being mistaken was a thing he could afford and being certain was not.

"Fräulein Herschlag," said a voice behind her, and there was the Colonel, come out of the ballroom with his coat over his arm and his hat in his hand, in a good humour, which was the most dangerous of his four moods, and he took in the scene in the way he took in everything, in a single pass, and drew the conclusion that any other man in his position would have drawn. "Brandt. So it is you that the whole building has been waiting for. My dear, this is Hauptmann Brandt, of the intelligence section; he has come back from Paris with the Lutetia's paperwork and a reputation, and he has been at my table three times this winter without managing to be introduced to you, which I am told is a symptom."

"Colonel."

"Herr Brandt." Von Hagen put his hat on the cloakroom counter and looked from one of them to the other with the mild, proprietary amusement of a man who has brought two things he owns into the same room. "You are the Polish speaker. My mistress has just been saying something to you in French, which means you have made a mistake already. She is Austrian and her French is a scandal and she refuses to be corrected."

"It is not the French," said Phillip Brandt. "It is the impertinence. I thought Fräulein Herschlag was someone I had known in Warsaw before the war, and I said so, and I have been apologising for it."

"You have been in Warsaw?"

"I was born in Warsaw," Phillip said. "My father is a German engineer. We left in October 1939, when the city was still burning, which is a thing one does not forget, and I have been correcting the French on their Polish place names ever since, for which they are grateful about once a year."

And von Hagen laughed, and put his hand on her back — the hand, the whole hand, the whole of the deed — and said, "There. My dear, it is the same old story. Everybody in this building came out of some city in flames, and on a good evening you can hear four different nationalities lying about their childhoods in one room. You shall dance with him, and he shall tell you about the Poles, and you shall tell him about the confectioner on the Rennweg. Come."

The band played. Three of them stood by the tall window at the end of the ballroom, where the rain had stopped and the black glass carried a yellow smear of the city on it: the Colonel with his hand on the small of her back, the captain with his cap under his arm, and herself between them; and they talked for four minutes, and she counted every one of them.

"You have been to Warsaw," von Hagen said. "Who is at the top of the building now? I have a file in the office that says Frank has too many cooks."

"Fischer has the labour section and Koppe has the police, and neither can be told anything by the other, and the two of them sit on the same telephone exchange, which is a fact the office finds interesting." Phillip said it flatly, as an inventory. "The civil administration considers Warsaw a colonisation. The security people consider it a problem of order. The two positions are incompatible and the incompatibility has been allowed to run since 1940, and the reason is that both men have an ear of the Führer, in different ears."

"You see," said von Hagen, to her, with the satisfaction of a man showing off a horse. "This is what I tell you. There is no plan. There is a collection of ambitions wearing a plan."

"There is a plan," said Phillip. "It is the plan of a man who does not write it down. The Warsaw district reports every three weeks, and every report says the city is quiet. It was quiet in February and it was quiet in April and it is quiet now. That does not mean anything except that the people who write our reports are told what to see."

"Ugh," said von Hagen. "You are a pessimist. My dear, we have with us the most reliable pessimist in the Militärbefehlshaber's service, and he is on loan from the east because nobody in this building can tell a Polish address from a Czech one." He took a glass from a tray and put it into Phillip's hand without asking. "But explain something to me, Brandt, since I have never had it explained. Why is it that a Pole will salute you one day and shoot at you the next? The Czechs, the Danes, the Dutch — they are all in the business of surviving. A Pole is in the business of Poland. Where does that come from?"

"It comes from the fact that Poland has been occupied for a hundred and forty years by three countries and never once stopped being Poland," Phillip said. "A man in Kraków has been in a conspiracy since he was fourteen. His father was in a conspiracy. His mother has a hiding place behind the stove that was built in 1863." He drank. "Your question is why he does not behave like a Dutchman. The answer is that the Dutchman's history is a different history. It is not a compliment to anybody. It is the shape of the place."

"And you have a Polish mother," said von Hagen, pleasantly.

"I have a Polish mother and a German father, and my father's firm laid the tram rails in Warsaw, and I have spent my life with one foot in each of them, and at present I am standing in a ballroom in Paris being asked by an Oberst to explain a country he has no intention of entering." Phillip said all of it with a deadpan, agreeable courtesy, and drank, and looked at her for exactly as long as the last clause took. "You will understand, Fräulein, that I am often not invited twice."

She laughed, because that was the correct response and because laughing was a labour she had performed four hundred times; and inside the laugh, behind it, in the load-bearing room of her, she was doing something else entirely.

He had said, in the course of those four minutes, three things that were checks and one thing that was not.

The checks: a street by the Vistula, an aunt who grew a lemon tree in a tub, the courtyard of the Institute of the Sacred Heart. He had put each of them into the soft places of his own sentences, where a woman who had been at school in Vienna could answer them and a woman who had been born in Warsaw could recognise them, and he had watched her face while the questions went past without changing his own expression at all. And she had answered Vienna, three times, in her Austrian voice, with charming, vague, entirely false details.

Not one of the three satisfied him. She saw that; she saw him file the fact; she understood that she had just been read, thoroughly, by a man wearing a captain's collar who was asking a colonel about the labour section, and she understood something else, which was that he had done it kindly. There had been no trap in it. He had wanted to know and had asked the only way he could ask in a room with a chandelier in it.

But the fourth thing was not a check.

Somewhere in the middle, while von Hagen was talking about the Polish character, Phillip had said half a sentence in German about his mother, and had stopped, and had finished his glass rather than finish the sentence; and she had seen, in that half-second, that he did not know whether his mother was alive or dead, and that he had been carrying the not-knowing for four years in the same way she carried hers. Then the orchestra came back up, and von Hagen put his glass on the tray of a passing waiter, and said, "There. Now. You shall dance with my Marion, Brandt, so that the whole building can watch you do it and be envious," and Phillip said, "If Fräulein Herschlag permits," and she said, "I permit everything that is permitted," which was a sentence she had used before and which nobody had ever found odd, because it sounded like coquetry and was in fact a very exact statement of policy.

***

They danced.

In the ballroom, at ten past two in the morning, to a slow thing from *The Merry Widow* played by eleven Frenchmen who had been playing it since September, a captain of the Wehrmacht and the mistress of an Oberst of the Militärbefehlshaber took one turn round a floor with two hundred and forty people on the edges of it, and the floor was crowded for the first twenty seconds and empty for the rest, because it was very late and people were leaving and because there is nothing in the world so interesting to a room full of Germans as a woman they have all assessed dancing with a man they have not.

He held her correctly: his right hand flat on her shoulder-blade, his left closed round hers, no pressure, the whole distance of four centimetres between them that is the correct distance, and he danced in the German way, which is to say extremely correctly, with the counting visible.

For eleven seconds neither of them said anything.

"You are not," he said at last, in German, quietly, with his eyes above her shoulder.

"Not what?"

"Not saying anything," Phillip Brandt said. "The Krystyna Stankiewiczówna I used to know would have had four sentences by now, and at least one of them would have been insulting."

She looked up at him. He was twenty-six, and he had a fine line at the left eye that had not been there at twenty-two, and he had shaved closely and inexpertly at the jaw, and he was holding her hand the way a man holds a document he intends to give back.

"Nine of them," she said, in Polish. "I have nine sentences. I have been keeping them for four years and they have all gone stale."

She heard his breath go, once, on the word *years*.

"That is not them."

"No. That is not them."

They made the turn at the end of the room and came back, past the general of artillery, who was drinking champagne and telling a Frenchman something about 1914, past the wife of the man who had ordered the deportations of August, who was eating a last marron glacé with the neat, terrible appetite of a small bird, past two majors of the Luftwaffe standing in the middle of the floor discussing a film, past the door where Emil Rutz was waiting with a coat.

"How long?" he said.

"Since when?"

"It is half past two in the morning in a German building," Phillip said, "and in four minutes the orchestra will stop. The well at Wierzbów. The one by the orchard, with the rope, where the bucket sticks at eleven metres and you have to let it down twice. Do you remember it?"

She did not answer him. They finished the dance. He let go of her, three centimetres, four, and stepped back and bowed, and she stood with her hands at her sides in a ballroom in Paris in December 1943, and he turned and walked to the door without looking back, and she went to the chair where the Colonel was putting on his coat, and stood there and said something amusing about the width of the bridge at Orléans, and got into the car, and rode home along the avenue Henri-Martin with her cheek six inches from a shoulder that she could have bitten through.

***

At the villa the Colonel was in a good humour and wanted her, in the undemanding, proprietorial way of a man who has had a great deal of wine and a great deal of applause and considers that the evening belongs to him; and afterwards he lay on his back with his forearm over his eyes and returned to the subject of Brandt.

"A clever fellow," he said. "The kind of clever that never gets promoted. He has a Polish mother and a Polish accent in German and no party, and he will be forty-five and a Hauptmann, and he will be a very useful analyst for somebody."

"Then why is he an officer?"

"Because his father signed a paper." He yawned enormously. "The whole trick of the east, my dear, is that there is always a paper. Give a man a paper and he will sign it and then he will serve you for four years out of shame, and it costs nothing, and it is the most economical invention of the century."

She lay beside him in the dark of the avenue Henri-Martin and looked at the cornicing with the hundred leaves and the last four botched, and she thought, with the exact, hard, professional attention she had brought to a sheet of blotting paper at three o'clock in the morning three weeks earlier, about a boy in an orchard with a bucket and a rope.

The orchard had been her father's, at Wierzbów, the estate that had been in the family since 1796 and had gone to the bank in the summer of 1939, the year the mortgage ate it, and the well had been on the south side, eleven metres of it, with a wooden cover and a rope that had been renewed in 1936 and a bucket that stuck at eleven metres where the shaft narrowed, and the reason she knew the bucket stuck at eleven metres was that she and her brother and a boy called Phillip Brandt, who was the son of the German engineer at the Lilpop works in Warsaw and who had been brought to the estate in a motorcar for the shooting, had spent the whole of a September afternoon in 1938 letting it up and down to watch it stick.

If you have ever stood at a well and watched a person you are twenty and in love with lean over it, you will know that there is nothing in the world you can be told about the well afterwards. She had never told anybody about the well. She had never told Wiktor, who had asked her, in 1942, in a room above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement, in the course of nine weeks of questions designed to find out what she would say under pressure, to describe her childhood, and she had described the palace on Nowy Świat and the school and the winters, and she had left out the well, and had noticed herself leaving it out, and had put the noticing away with the other things.

At four o'clock in the morning she got up and used the bathroom, and washed her hands, and stood looking at her face in the glass.

Then she went back to the bedroom and sat on the edge of the bed with her feet on the cold floor, and did what she always did when there was a thing she could not put away.

She made the plan.

The plan took eleven minutes and had four parts: where he lived; when he was at work; how to be in a room with him without being seen to be in a room with him; and how, on the day the fourth part became necessary, it would be done. She knew a man in the twentieth arrondissement — a Polish deserter from the Wehrmacht who had been in the network's hands and gone rotten in the way of some of them, and who was paid in francs and silence — and a Hauptmann of the intelligence section who was found dead in a stairwell in the sixteenth arrondissement in December 1943 would be a page of German paper, an investigation, an SD file with clean spectacles reading it, and eleven arrests among the porters and charwomen of a building that had nothing to do with any of it.

She sat there on the edge of the bed in the dark and ran the whole of it, four times, and at the end of the fourth run she lay down, because there is no decision to make at four in the morning that cannot be made worse by making it at four in the morning, and because she had learned in 1940 that the finest of all the network's rules was the last one on the list, the one they had printed in tiny type at the bottom of the last page of the manual: *sleep*.

***

What she remembered, when she let herself, was not a scene. It was four of them, and she kept them in order, the way you keep four cards you have decided not to look at.

The horse: a chestnut mare called Bella with a white sock on the near fore and a habit of leaning her shoulder into a gate, which she had ridden every summer from the age of eight to the age of eighteen on the estate at Wierzbów, and which had been sold in the autumn of 1937, when the beet money failed for the second year, to a stud farm near Sandomierz; and the estate itself went to the bank in the summer of 1939, and the buyer from Poznań pulled down the north wing in 1941, and none of that matters now, which was always the point of the horse. It was the least important of the four and it was the one that came first, always, because it was the one that did not hurt.

The birch wood: September 1938, the second week, the light coming down through the leaves in long yellow slats onto the path, and the smell of leaf mould after a week of rain, and the two of them walking the horses because they had been out an hour and the flies were bad. He had been twenty-one and had arrived the evening before with his father, who had come about the mortgage, and had spent the whole of the first evening at the end of the table being courteous in the way of a boy who has been raised to be courteous and who would rather be somewhere else. And in the wood, on the second day, he had stopped in front of her in the middle of a sentence about her brother's terrible French and had not kissed her, and had stood there with the mare's reins in his hand and his ears entirely red, and she had said, "Well?" — and he had laughed, and it had happened.

The wrist: the same afternoon, in the same wood. He had put his hand on the back of her neck while she was standing at a tree with her forehead against the bark, and had asked her something in a voice that had gone low, and she had taken hold of his wrist, above the joint, with her thumb over the tendon, and had turned it and pushed, the way her father's riding master had once shown her when she was twelve and told her never to do it to a man in jest, and Phillip had gone down onto one knee in the leaves with his mouth open and his breath gone out of him, and had looked up at her from the ground with an expression of something that she had not understood properly for two years and that she understood now, in a bedroom on the avenue Henri-Martin, at the age of twenty-four, entirely.

And the fourth card, which she did not turn over.

It was the reason she had spent the last hour of the night with paper and pencil, working out the address of a hotel and the hours of a man, instead of the reason a person normally has for working out such things; and it was the reason that at some point in the next four months — she put the point at some time in the spring, and was wrong about it by eleven days — the two of them were going to have to have a conversation that would end either with him dead or with the two of them in a canal.

She held the four cards for a while, in order, and then put them away. Outside, the blacked-out city with its slit headlamps went on making the small, ordinary, dead noises of a city in its fourth winter, and beside her, in the bed, a German colonel turned over and put his hand out and found nothing, and slept again.

She slept for forty minutes and woke to the bell at the service door, and lay still while the maid's feet went along the corridor and stopped, and listened to the murmur and the door and the feet going back, one set of them, and a second set that was the cook's, and the low laugh that servants make at half past four in the morning at the expense of whoever is at the door.

She got up at five, because there was no more sleeping to be had, and went down the back stairs into the servants' hall in her dressing gown, and the cook was at the range with her back to the room and the kitchen maid was shelling something grey into a bowl, and the cook said, without turning round, "Fräulein. There is a letter for you on the tray."

"Who brought it?"

"A young man. He came to the door at twenty to four, and he would not come in, and he would not take anything, and he gave me this and he said, 'For Fräulein Herschlag, from the florist's,' and there is no florist open at twenty to four in this city, Fräulein, and then he said, 'There is nothing to pay,' which was also not necessary." The cook turned round, and looked at her with the long, level, unshockable scrutiny of French servants in German houses, who have been asked to understand a great deal and who have understood all of it. "He was very polite. He had a German coat on. He was not French. He went up the street on foot in the dark, and my niece's husband is a policeman in Neuilly, Fräulein, and I could have had him followed out of pure professional curiosity, and I did not, and that is a favour, and you may consider what it is worth."

"Thank you, Madame Berthaud."

"Then take it up before Rutz comes down. He is tidier than the Colonel and he notices the tray."

The envelope was cheap, the wartime kind, grey, of paper that had been made of straw and would not take ink; and somebody had written on it in pencil, at a café table or a bar or the top of a wall, in a hand she had last seen on a school exercise book that had been left in the hall of the house at Wierzbów with a silver pencil on top of it in the summer of 1939, two words and an initial.

*M. Herschlag.*

She did not open it in the servants' hall. She took it up to the bathroom, at the top of the house, where there was a small window with black paint on the glass and one bulb and a lock, and she sat on the edge of the bath in her dressing gown and opened it, and the inside was one sentence in Polish, in the same hand, written with a nib that had been pressed too hard.

*I have kept it for four years. I can keep it longer.*

It was not signed with a name. It did not need to be.

She sat on the edge of the bath with the paper in both hands and understood, in the way in which one understands things in the fifth year of a war, that the envelope was the second sentence, and that it was the more dangerous of the two. There was no postmark on it, no stamp, no boy from a telegraph office and no man from a courier's van: it had been carried to the service door of a requisitioned German house on the avenue Henri-Martin, at twenty minutes to four in the morning, by hand, by a captain of the Militärbefehlshaber's intelligence section, on foot, in a German coat, and handed to a French cook with two lies about a florist, with two sentries forty metres away at the gate, at the hour when the sentry on the gravel makes his eleventh-minute round past the statue of the stag and stops to work his feet in his boots.

***

At twenty to seven she was in the side chapel on the rue Saint-Honoré, in the fourth row, with her hat on and her hands in her lap, and the priest came out to the altar and did what he did at the altar, and then he came down the side and sat in the chair in front of her, and put his hands on his knees, and waited.

She gave him the memorandum. The coastal-sector number, the inspection schedule, the coastal battery’s firing table, the two dates, the name of the officer who was coming from Berlin to do the inspecting and the fact that he had been in Warsaw in October. She gave it in order, in Polish, in a low voice, without notes, twice, because that was the rule; and she gave him, after it, the small change of the week: the eleventh department, the contractor's cement prices, the two of them lying about their childhoods at the Christmas party, the carol, the pearls.

"And the ball?" said the priest.

"There were two hundred and forty people. There will be an illustrated paper."

"Good. That is a good week." He looked at her. He had a big, calm, ugly face and the hands of a man who had done manual work before the seminary, and he had been a chaplain in the Polish army in 1920, and there was nothing in his face that could be hurried. "And?"

"And nothing."

"You have come in from a night that you have not described to me, and you have given me a very good memorandum, and you have told me four things about a party, and there is something standing behind you at this moment that I can see from here, and you are not going to tell me what it is." He said it without any weight at all, in the tone of a man reading the weather. "That is your right. But I will tell you the rule, Marion, because I am old and because you have been in the trade for three years and I have been in it for twenty-five. The rule is that nobody is ever caught by the thing they decided to tell. They are caught by the thing they decided not to."

"I know."

"Then you know. And I will not ask again, and if you come to me in a week and tell me, I will listen, and if you never tell me, I will still be here at this hour, and if you tell me a lie I will know it, and that is the end of the conversation." He took the folded devotional leaflet out of his sleeve and put it in her hands, the same grey leaflet against the same grey paper, with the same instruction inside it: the drop on Thursday, the man with the striped scarf, the hour, the second hour if he did not come.

She got up to go.

"Marion."

"Holy Father?"

"Say the name to yourself," the priest said. "Whatever it is. Say it in the street, once, quietly, and then put it away. A name that has been said out loud once is a name you can carry; a name you have never said out loud is a hook in the mouth, and it will pull you wherever it likes." He turned his hands over on his knees. "I do not want to know it. Go and be foolish somewhere else, and come back on Thursday."

She went out into the rue Saint-Honoré at seven in the morning of the twenty-fourth of December 1943, into a cold grey light and the smell of a bakery and the sound of a nun's bicycle bell, with a bouquet of evergreen and white ribbon under the arm of a market porter she passed, and the whole city getting up in a language that was not its own; and she put her hand in her pocket, and touched the corner of the envelope, and did not take it out; and she said nothing to anybody; and she did not go to Wiktor, and she did not go to the woman in the twentieth arrondissement, and she did not write a line of it down.

She walked east along the rue de Rivoli with her hat down against the wind, and by the time she reached the Place des Pyramides she had already begun to be late for a fitting, and she had not said the name once, not once, not even inside her own skull, and she was twenty-four years old and carrying it the way a person carries a small, warm, illegal thing: carefully, and in the left hand, and against the skin — and there is no name for that hour of the morning in a war, when you have decided a thing without deciding it, except that in the Polish it is *ta godzina*, the hour, and in French there is no word at all, and the boy who had said her name in a corridor at half past one in the morning was the only other person alive who knew which one she meant.

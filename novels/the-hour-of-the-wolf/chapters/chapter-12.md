# Chapter 12: The Cold Flat

The work she was given was real work, and that was the point of it: the Oberst of the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison command had a large amount of paper in his life and no patience with any of it, and a man in that position wants three things — someone to sort the paper, someone to write the draft, and someone to be in a room with at the end of the day who is not a soldier — and she had been hired for the third of these and had spent four months making herself necessary for the first two.

So she typed.

The typewriter was a German make of the nineteen-thirties that stood on a small table in the corner of the study, by the lithograph of the Lilpop works, with the safe behind it; and she typed the Colonel's private correspondence and his memoranda to the garrison command and the fair copies of the reports that came out of the section to be signed by officers who could not spell; and she did it well enough to be asked to do more of it, and badly enough that whoever read her work first was not troubled by it.

And here is the trade she had made with herself, in the first week of March, in the bathroom of a requisitioned villa on the Frascati Street, with the tap running:

One paragraph a day.

Not more. Never a document, never a photograph, never a carbon, never a paper out of the house; one paragraph, memorised, once, in the minute and a half it takes to type it — a paragraph of a memorandum to the garrison's department Ia about the disposition of the counter-intelligence section's field offices; a paragraph of the quarterly report on the Polish police battalions; a paragraph on the interception of wireless traffic in the district, which had in it the location of two sets; a sentence about a man in Siedlce who was delivering reports to a captain of the Abwehr and had been given a cover name. One paragraph a day, delivered to Iga Zaleska at intervals of eleven or twelve days, memorised and spoken aloud in a cellar, never carried, never written, never in the house.

That is what a person who is good at that trade actually does, and it is why the trade is not glamorous: you take one paragraph, and you leave three paragraphs, and if you do that every day for a year you get an extraordinary amount of paper out of a building, and if you try to take four paragraphs you are discovered in a fortnight by a man with a chart.

She had the house to herself two afternoons a week, Tuesdays and Fridays, between two and five, when the Colonel was at the Saski Palace and Janina went to her sister in the Wola and Rutz drove the Colonel; and she had a German sentry at the gate who was nineteen years old, from a village near Kassel, four months out of his parents' house, bored into the marrow of his bones, and who could be made a fool of by a lady in a fur coat who brought him cigarettes of a French make out of a private supply and asked him about his mother.

By the second week of April, the sentry at the gate of the villa on the Frascati Street would have given a month of his pay to be useful to the gnädige Frau, and would also have said, if asked under oath, that she never left the house on Tuesdays and Fridays, which was true of the car, and which was what the day book recorded, and which was all that would ever be looked at.

***

Janina she dealt with as one deals with a person who has been told not to like you: by being exact.

The woman cooked, kept the two floors, did the marketing with the household's money and a German ration card for a German household and a Polish card for herself, and had a sister in Wola and a brother who had been taken to the Reich for labour in 1942 and who wrote twice a year; and she never once asked Krystyna a question about anything, and never once met her eyes for longer than a second, and every evening at half past nine she stood in the door of the dining room and said, in German, out of a mouth that had been taught the words the way a bird is taught them, "Gnädige Frau, is there anything?"

The first week of April there was.

They were in the kitchen, at seven in the morning, and Janina was at the table with a newspaper spread on it and a chicken on the newspaper, and she was not looking at the door, and Krystyna stood in it and said, in Polish: "The chicken is from the black market and it is not for the Oberst. How much did you pay?"

Janina's hands went still on the bird. "Two hundred and forty zlotys, and a quarter of tea."

"I shall put four hundred zlotys in the tin in the dresser every Thursday, in the blue tin, and you will spend it on the house and on whatever your sister needs, and you will not account for it to me and you will not tell the Oberst, because he does not know what a chicken costs and if he ever asks I shall tell him it costs what you paid and no more." She took a piece of paper out of her sleeve. "This is a list. On the list there are four things: a woman's coat, grey, wool, for a girl of seventeen; two kilos of lard; a hundred cigarettes; and a bottle of the spirit that the chemist on the Żelazna keeps behind the counter. The coat is the first thing. It is for a person whose name you will not be told."

And Janina looked up at her for the first time in four weeks, for about four seconds, with the face of a woman of forty-five who has been in one house for eleven years and who has understood, in one sentence, exactly what sort of a person has been living in the room at the end of the corridor; and then she looked down at the chicken and turned it over and began to draw the knife along the breastbone.

"The chemist on the Żelazna is my cousin's husband," she said. "The coat will be difficult, but there is a woman on the Ogrodowa who keeps coats."

"I know," said Krystyna. "I know there is." She put the paper on the table, and the money under the newspaper, and went out; and neither of them ever referred to the conversation again, and neither of them ever gave the other away, in four months of a city where that was the whole of the currency.

The coat left the house on the last day of April in a market basket with the lard and the cigarettes on top of it and went to the Ogrodowa to be kept, and Krystyna did not ask anybody in the movement to carry it and did not tell the cellar on the Śliska that she had bought it, and there was no note in the pocket, and she never learned whether it was worn. What she knew, on the eighth of May, standing at the window of the study behind the lithograph of the Lipop works, was that a girl in a grey wool coat had gone past the end of the street on the far pavement at four in the afternoon at the pace of a person who had somewhere to be, and that the girl had not looked up, and that she had not looked twice, and that this was the entire return on the four hundred zlotys and the conversation in the kitchen, and that she would take it.

***

The flat above the cobbler's shop on the Zielna was four rooms on the first floor of a building of 1898 and belonged to a family called Wolski who had gone to the countryside in 1940 and had left the clock and the hat behind; and the room at the back, over the courtyard, had a table, three chairs, a bed, a stove that had not been lit since 1943, and a window with a curtain that could be hooked back halfway up, which was the arrangement.

The cobbler was real. He was seventy-five and he had been at the front of the Ruska Street for eleven years and had a wife and a certificate for a bad leg, and he was one of perhaps four thousand people in Warsaw in 1944 who knew one thing about one room, and nothing else about anything in the world, and who had kept it for two years because nobody had ever asked him anything except about shoes.

The parcel was on the table.

She had come to collect, and he had come to hand over, and that is all that either of them had been told, and they sat down at the table with a coat on, in the cold, at four in the afternoon of the sixth of April 1944, and did forty minutes of entirely professional work.

The parcel was eleven days of Phillip Brandt's access, and this is what it contained.

A page of a movement order of the Feldgendarmerie's district command, which she copied? No — which she read, because nothing in that room was ever written down: a summary of the sweeps in the district of the next ten days; the times and the streets; the eighteen-hundred curfew on the ninth, which would be an action.

A list of the eleven vehicles of the section's motor pool with their registration numbers, because the numbers were the way to follow a car.

The establishment of the counter-intelligence section as of the first of April, in the form in which it had been circulated to the garrison command: the names, the ranks, the rooms, the telephones, including his own name, Hauptmann P. Brandt, Room 14, telephone 42 118, and the name of the Colonel, and the name of a man called Kalbach with an asterisk against it, which in the staff language of that army meant seconded from another authority.

And a single sheet of paper, six lines long, in German, of the sort that comes off a stencil: an order of the 5th of April to all offices on the registration of the *Kennkarten* of the civil population, and a paragraph on the control of the roads east of the city, which was about the work battalions and was, in fact, about the evacuation.

She memorised the whole of it in the forty minutes, in the way she had been trained, which is by taking the paper apart and putting it back together in a different order: the numbers by pairs, the names by sound, the streets by their shape on a map she had carried in her head since she was seven years old.

He sat and watched her do it, and said nothing, and at the end of it he said: "There is a man in the section called Schmidt. Do not memorise him; he is a clerk and he has no access to anything. I am telling you because his name is on the establishment and you will see it in a paper one day and think it is a person of interest. He is nothing."

"That is the eleventh time you have told me what a paper is not," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you are going to have to read a great deal of my summarising, and my summarising is a profession, and the first thing a profession teaches you is the shape of its own limits." He rubbed his hands, which were cold. "You asked me once in a church in Paris for four sentences. This is the fifth: everything I give you will be true and incomplete, and the incompleteness will not be a lie."

***

She tested him on the ninth.

She had prepared the test for three days, out of Wiktor's manual and out of her own four years, and it was in the manual under a heading that she had once thought excessive: *the provocation of a colleague*, and the object of it is not to catch a liar but to watch a man being asked for something he cannot produce.

"Police battalions," she said. "The disposition of the German police battalions in the district, all of them, for the next two weeks: their quarters, their movements, their rest days, their strength. Not the gendarmerie patrols; the battalions. Can you bring it to me?"

He sat back and thought about it for eleven seconds.

"No," he said.

"Can you get it?"

"I can tell you what I know from the section's papers, which is two battalions' worth — the 11th and the 31st — and I know their quarters and the days they draw rations, which is a fact about their strength, because the ration boards are in the supply files and I read the supply files." He leaned forward. "I cannot get you their disposition, because it is not in my building. The order of battle for the police battalions is held by the commander of the Order Police of the district, whose office is on the aleja Szucha, and it is issued to the police and to the garrison's Ia, and the copy at the garrison's Ia is in the section Ia of the staff, in a room on the first floor of the Saski Palace which I have been in twice, both times with a major, and where the filing cabinet is locked and the clerk is a Feldwebel called Böhm who has been in the same office since 1940 and who knows the contents of that cabinet better than his own family." He paused. "It would take me four to six weeks, and it would need two things I do not have: a reason to be in that room when the Feldwebel is not, and a person on the staff who owes me. I could build the second one in a month. The first one I would have to manufacture, and if I manufacture it wrong I lose my posting, and if I lose my posting I lose the section, and if I lose the section I lose everything you came to Warsaw for. That is my answer. What I have for you instead is the fuel allocations for the police battalions, which are in my building."

She looked at him across the table in the cold room.

A liar, in her experience, had one of three shapes: the man who produced the impossible document on the second day, the man who explained at length why he could not, and the man who wanted something for it. She knew the shapes the way a dressmaker knows a badly tacked seam, and she sat looking at this one and understood that he had given her the truth about his limits in the manner of a man laying out a technical drawing, with the reason and the substitute and the timetable, and that she had been in the trade for four years and had never once, in four years, been given that.

"I will take the fuel allocations," she said.

"I know. I made them a copy before I came." He put a folded sheet on the table and pushed it across, and then he said, in the same voice: "And I want the police dispositions too, and I shall get them, and when I have them it will be because I have found the person who owes me, and not because you have asked me again. Do not ask me again."

***

And then, with the parcel memorised and the sheet of paper in her hand, there was nothing to do and half an hour left, and the two of them sat in a dead family's room with a coat on, at a table, with a stopped clock on the sideboard reading ten past four, and a hat on the hook by the door, and on the wall three photographs of people standing in front of a car.

"The clock," she said.

"Stopped at ten past four."

"The family?"

"They went to the country in September 1940, the mother and the two children; the man was in the resistance and was taken in 1942 and is in Pawiak or in the other place and nobody in this arrangement knows. The flat is ours because the wife's sister is in the underground and signed it over in 1941 in a kitchen. She comes once a month with a duster and does not come in." He looked at the hat. "The hat is the mother's. I asked. The people who use this room do not move the hat, because the neighbours know the flat is empty and a hat that moves is a sign."

"I know. I have stayed in rooms like this in three cities."
 
"My mother has a hat like it." He stopped. Then, in a different voice, without any change of posture, he said: "In September 1939, at the gate, he used one word. I have carried it for four years. It was correct and I have never once been able to be angry about it, and I have tried, and I can tell you the whole of the argument of the angry side and it is a better argument than his, and it does not work, and I am forty kilometres past trying to make it work."

"Do not do this here."

"I am not doing anything. I am telling you what is on my side of the table, so that you can judge the material." He folded his hands. "You asked about the clock and the hat and I answered, and then you asked nothing about the four years, and I am telling you: I have known since the eighteenth of December 1943 in a corridor of the Majestic, and I did not tell anybody, and I have not written one word of it down in any file in any building, and I have had eighteen opportunities and taken none of them, and I am not asking you for anything, and I shall not. That is the whole of my account."

"I sleep with him," she said. "I have slept in that bed since June 1943. I do the work he takes me to the cafés for; I type his letters; I am paid by the German administration in the salary book of the Warsaw garrison; and I have killed eleven days of a man's investigation by putting a Frenchwoman in a police car in Paris in January, and she is dead, and I did it to keep this arrangement, and it worked." She said all of it in the flat voice she had used for the shopping lists in the cellar on the Śliska, and she kept her eyes on him the whole time. "If you intend to use any of it, use it now, because you will not get a better opportunity than this room: I am here, alone, with a paper in my hand that would hang me, and there is nobody in the building."

Phillip Brandt looked at her for a long moment, and then he stood up and went to the window and hooked the curtain back halfway up, which was the arrangement, and stood there with his back to her, looking down into the courtyard of a cobbler's shop in occupied Warsaw.

"There is a page in the file," he said. "The index page. It has the case reference and the case officer's name and eleven lines on it, and the last line is the question I told you about." He took out of the inside of his coat a folded sheet of paper — thin, flimsy, the sort that comes off a duplicator, with a pencil shadow on the back of it where it had been written on — and put it on the table and pushed it across and did not look at her while he did it. "It is the index page of the file about you. It was in the tray on his desk yesterday afternoon and it is there again this morning because I copied it in the washroom in eleven minutes and put the original back, and if it is missed it will be a search of the whole section and if it is not missed, in eleven days the second copy of it will be in the hands of a woman I have known since I was nineteen."

She looked at the paper on the table and did not touch it.

"Why?"

"Because you asked me in a church for four sentences and I gave them to you and you refused the papers, and I have had four months to work out why, and the reason is that you cannot be bought and you cannot be saved and you are going to be dead in a year unless somebody in that building is on your side." He turned round. "I am in that building. That is the whole of the answer. Take the paper, and burn it in the stove, and do not thank me, because if Lindemann sees me being thanked by you through a window we are both finished."

***

She delivered on the eighteenth of April, in the cellar on the Śliska, with a candle and a table and a pistol, and Krupa — the Major, the man of the spectacles who had laid down the law to her across that cellar in March — did not sit down.

She gave them the establishment of the counter-intelligence section, name by name, room by room, with the telephone numbers; the field offices of the district and the name of the man in Siedlce who delivered reports to the Abwehr and the cover name he had been given, which Iga wrote in the ledger in a hand so small that it looked like a smudge; the two fuel allocations; the page of the Feldgendarmerie's movement order of the sixth of April, which was, by then, four days old and therefore worthless, which she said out loud before they could; and the paragraph from the Colonel's memorandum to Ia of the second of April on the deployment of the section's field offices for the summer, which was not worthless at all, because a police service which is redeploying for the summer is a police service which has been told what the summer is going to be.

"Again," said Krupa, from the dark of the corner, and she said the last item again, and he took off his spectacles and put them on again and said, "That is the third paragraph in four weeks from the same memorandum series. Tell me about the number on it."

"Sixteen slash forty-four."

"And the previous two were numbers twelve and fourteen of the same series."

"Yes."

"So there are papers in the series we have not seen, in an order, produced on a schedule. I want the schedule." He stood with his arms folded and looked at the candle. "You will not get me the schedule; he will. Understand that when you deliver, you are delivering the whole of what he gave you and not the parts of it that you have decided are important. The schedule is how we know what we have not got. That is what an officer is for."

Then he took his hat off the table and went up the steps, and Iga closed the ledger and snapped the string round it, and said, in her flat voice, without any expression at all: "You gave us two names in the Abwehr in Siedlce and a paragraph that I could not have got in any other way in this city, and I want you to know that I have written the date and the source, and that the source is a German officer, and that the ledger goes into the ground if this city is ever taken, and that the man in the corner has spent two hours tonight arranging that." She stood up. "Come on Thursday and not before, and do not come on foot through the Ruska, and do not look at the wall of the courtyard when you go out, and above all, Stankiewicz, do not do the thing that you are doing with your face at this moment, which is deciding that you are in love with him, because I have seen it twice in this cellar in two years and on both occasions it was the last thing that happened before the arrests."

***

Two days later, on the eleventh of April, the police sealed the exits of four blocks in Praga between the hours of five and eight in the evening and went through them for men of the conscription age and for the underground; and the attempt went wrong, in the way the AK sometimes did it, exactly according to plan: the eleven men who had been printed on the paper in the flat above the cobbler's went out over the roofs and through a courtyard on the other side of the street between half past four and five, before the cars came; and the police took a hundred and forty people, and kept nine, and three of them were men who had stayed behind to cover the roofs; and the notice went up on the kiosks with the names and dates in the ordinary way.

And on the fourteenth of April a Polish worker at the telephone exchange on the Zielna was arrested at his post and taken to the aleja Szucha; he was thirty-two, he had a wife and a child, and he had been passing the schedules of the exchange's German traffic to a man in the railway administration; and the file on him had been open since February, and the man who had opened it was looking for a leak in the telephone service of the capital's German administration, and had found one, and had nothing whatever to do with anybody else's case; and the exchange's Polish staff were interrogated for two days.

Iga Zaleska came to the cellar on the Śliska on the sixteenth of April with that arithmetic and put it on the table the way a person puts down a bottle.

"Eleven saved out of the block in Praga. That is your German's paper and it worked, and I have written it in the ledger with his information marked as confirmed, twice, which in my hand is the same thing as praise. One man shot on the twenty-first of April, at the gate of the exchange, and his name is Nowicki, and he was thirty-two and he was watching for the same police cars you were. Our people in the exchange told us he was arrested because of a leak in the exchange; we do not know him and he does not know us; the file was opened in February by a man in another office entirely." She sat down. "He is nothing to do with your German, and we do not know which of them your German bought. That is the arithmetic, and it is our arithmetic and not his, and this is the trade: we spent a man in April to buy eleven in March, and nobody in Warsaw can make that come out even. Do not look for a cleaner one."

***

Von Hagen came back from a conference in Kraków on the twentieth of April in the state of a man who has been in a hotel with staff officers for four days, and he came into the house at nine at night with his coat over his arm and the smell of the train on him and the particular glazed courtesy of a Prussian who has been drinking with his equals and has arrived home to what he owns; and the whole of what followed was his, and it was not violent, it was worse: it was thorough, proprietorial, and affectionate in the way of a man handling a good tool, and there was nothing in it that could have been complained about by anybody, and at the end of it he lay in the dark with his hand on her shoulder and told her about the conference.

Not the contents. The contents were sealed, and he kept the seal exactly as long as it took for a man to want to talk about his own importance: he told her about the general who had fallen asleep, about a major from the cavalry who had put forward a scheme for the defence of the bridges of the Vistula which had been laughed at by the staff, about the food in the hotel, about the fact that the conference was not really about the Vistula at all and that the staff was doing the exact thing that staffs had always done, which was to fight the last war's plan against the next war's problem; and then he said, in the middle of a sentence about the general's gout, something that had no business being in it.

"They have put me in the middle of the most interesting problem of the war," he said, in the dark, with his hand on her shoulder. "There is a section of this city which is not the army and not the police: it is a government. It has courts. It has a treasury. In February it sentenced to death four officers of the administration of the Government General, and one of them was shot in the street in Kraków in front of his wife, and I have read the sentence, because a copy of it was in a packet of cigarettes taken off a boy, and it was in Polish and it was written properly, with the grounds and the signature and the date, and it was the work of a state. That is what is in this town. My department has to find the state." He turned over. "There is one thing about such a man: he is inside the machine. Every one of them is inside something: an office, a railway, a telephone exchange, a household. The state is outside and the man is inside and the gap between the two of them is the only place where a police service can ever put its hands."

Then he said, in the flat, contented, drowsing voice of a man summing up, "There is a matter in my own section. One of the translators has been asking for files outside his department — twice in ten days, from the registry, in the afternoon. It may be nothing; a translator who is bored asks for files. What do you think of a man who is too curious?"

"I think he is either bored or employed by somebody else," she said, into the pillow, half asleep, in the voice of a woman who has been kept. "And I think you already know which."

"Yes," said von Hagen comfortably, and fell asleep with his hand still on her, and the pearls at her throat, which he had given her in Paris in December, lying on the skin of her collarbone like an answer.

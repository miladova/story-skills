# Chapter 16: An Order from the Forest

It came to her at a dinner table, in the third week of June, in the middle of the soup.

The dinner was at the villa and there were nine people at the table; the soup was a consommé with a piece of marrow in it, and the conversation was about the harvest in Galicia, and Janina came in with the wine on a tray, and the Polish waiter who had been borrowed for the evening from the restaurant on the Nowy Świat — because the villa's own two servants were not enough for nine and the Oberst liked a professional hand at the sideboard — came round the table with the bottle in the white cloth, and poured, and as he poured for her he put a card down on the cloth beside her hand: a card about the size of a playing card, folded once, of the kind that florists put in a box, and on the front of it there was a picture of a bunch of roses.

She had, at that moment, a fork in her left hand and a German major on her right discussing the price of a horse, and there were nine people at the table, all of whom looked at her when she laughed, and she laughed at a thing the major said, and turned the card over with two fingers under the level of the table, and read it, and put it in her sleeve, and ate her soup.

*The French network has been rolled up since February. Wiktor Janowski was taken in Paris on the sixteenth of February, held at Fresnes, shot at Mont-Valérien on the twelfth of April with twenty-two others. We have it from the wire, and the wire is good, and there is no doubt in it. You will be told where and when. Your work continues and the terms are unchanged.*

She sat at that table, in a grey silk dress, with the paste pearls at her throat, for one hour and fifty minutes.

The conversation went round: the harvest in Galicia, the price of a horse, a story about a general's wife who had bought a château in Hungary in April on the advice of a fortune-teller; the news from the front, which was not news, because the front was in the east and the east was a rumour and the rumour walked through the dining rooms of Warsaw in the summer of 1944 in the form of three sentences that everybody had heard and nobody had said; the question of the air raids, which had not come to the east of the Reich yet, and which everybody in that room had a theory about.

She did the arithmetic of the hour and fifty minutes, in the way she had been trained: at the table she was the Colonel's lady, which meant that she was the woman in the room with four officers and three wives, and that the whole of her work was to be neither interesting nor absent; and she talked to the major about the harvest and to the civil administrator's wife about a hat, and she laughed twice at the right places, and when the woman on the other side of her asked her about the singing, because it was known in Warsaw that she sang, she said that she would sing for them if the Oberst would permit a Schubert song and nothing German with brass in it, and the whole of the table laughed and made the Oberst permit it, and she sang, after the dessert, standing by the piano with the pearls and a glass of Hungarian wine, one song of Schubert in the thin, clear, small voice that the room had heard eleven times and had learned to want; and the whole of the sacrifice of the two hours — of the soup, of the card, of the wine, of the song — was this: that there are four officers of the Warsaw garrison who remembered, four months later, at the end of August, in the last week of their lives, that the Colonel's Austrian mistress had sung Schubert at a table in June, and that not one of them ever connected the song to anything, because what she had done was exactly what the woman in the room was for.

Then the guests went, and the house settled, and she went up to the bathroom at the top of the house and locked the door and turned both taps on.

***

What she thought about, in the bathroom, for two hours, was not grief.

That is worth setting down carefully, because she thought about it herself, on the tiles, at midnight, with the steam on the mirror and the card unfolded on the edge of the basin: grief was a thing that would come to her in 1947, in a room in Łódź, at a table, over a piece of paper, and would last four days; and what was in the bathroom in Warsaw in June 1944 was a much more practical and much colder emotion, which was the vertigo of a person whose files have been closed.

The rules: nothing in writing; nothing carried; nothing said in a place where it could be heard; and the four rules of Wiktor Janowski, in his dry voice, in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement in the autumn of 1942, which she had kept every day for a year and a half, and which had kept her alive: *one*, that she was never to be curious about the people above her; *two*, that she was never to ask when the arrangement would end; *three*, that if she was ever asked a question in a room she could not leave, she was to answer nothing, at all, for the first eleven minutes, because everybody breaks in the end and the way you spend eleven minutes is the difference between a lot and nothing; and *four*, sleep.

And the whole of it came with a sentence, which he had said once, in a kitchen, with his bad leg stretched out in front of him and a newspaper on his knee: "You have not been caught. That is the whole of our morality, and you are going to have to do without any other."

The man who had made the rules was dead in a field outside Paris with twenty-two others and a record in a German file; and there was nobody in France to whom she owed an account; and there was nobody in the movement on the Śliska who had known him, and there was nobody in the world who could tell her whether what she was doing in Warsaw in June 1944 was authorised or was the invention of a lonely woman with a good memory. She sat on the edge of the bath in the steam and understood that she would have to be her own standard, from that evening, for the rest of the war; and that the standard would be the four rules, and the sentence about the morality, and the taste of the man's voice, and that they were now a small private religion in a bathroom in a German villa, kept by one person, in the way in which people keep the religions of dead countries.

She burned the card on a plate, and ran the ash down the basin, and went to bed at half past one and slept four hours, which is sleep, and which is what the fourth rule is for.

***

Warsaw in June 1944 had changed its temperature, and the whole of the change was in the railway stations.

It was in the trains that stood in the sidings for a day and a night and went west: the administrative staff of four offices, the families of the men of the German administration, the wives and the children and the furniture of the Reich — crates, pianos in straw, a crate of wine, a woman with an umbrella and a birdcage and the whole of her life in a single case. The Poles watched them go, at the Ostbahnhof and the Gdańsk station and the Dworzec Wschodni, in the way in which a population watches a dying administration: without comment, without celebration, and with a kind of unhurried attention, because a departing occupier is a thing that has consequences and every one of those consequences is going to land on the population.

It was in the soldiers, who had changed from the soldiers of 1943: the older men with the ribbons of the last war and the soft, well-fed horses of the garrison were now the second grade, and the streets of the German quarter had in them, in June, whole units out of the east — men out of Russia, in the grey that was not quite the same grey, with the mud of the Pripet on their boots and the smell of a burnt country on their coats, walking in parties of four and six along the Nowy Świat with their rifles over their shoulders the way men walk who have been in a place where a man does not walk in parties of four; and the front-line units were moving through Warsaw all that month in the direction of the Vistula, from east to west, at night, with the horses and the lorries and the field kitchens, so that the citizens of the capital saw the German army of the east cross their city in the dark for four weeks.

It was in the underground press, which had stopped being careful: three papers in three weeks, all of them, in different words, saying the same thing, which was that the hour was near, that the orders would come, that the people of Warsaw were to prepare and to wait, and that the eastern front was on the river.

And it was in the courts.

The courts of the underground, which had been working since 1940 with the patience of an institution, had entered their last and busiest summer: in June 1944, in the cellars and the flats of that city, there were sentences being prepared and handed down every week, by men and women sitting at tables with a lamp and a file, in the name of a Polish state whose government was in London and whose army was in the city; and among the thousands of such sentences that year there was one, prepared at a table on the Śliska by four men over eleven days, on a szmalcownik called Feliks Kaczmarek, who had been executed on a stolen car on the Chmielna on the twelfth of May, and whose file, with the evidence and the sentence in it and no name under the line that said *by information received*, was sewn into the lining of a coat and carried out of the city by a girl of nineteen and put in a place where it would be found in 1946, and used by a court in the Poland that came afterwards, in the three years when half of Europe's criminals were working in offices and the other half were dead.

***

Iga Zaleska brought her the ultimatum on the twentieth of June, at a table in the cellar on the Śliska, with a map on it.

"You know Brandt is a witness," she said.

"I know he is a German officer who knows what I am."

"He is a German officer who knows what you are, who has known it since December, and who has not written it down; and he has brought us eleven documents and the establishment of the counter-intelligence section and the day book of the household and a page of the file about you; and there are four people in this section whose lives are in the hands of a man in a German uniform who could walk into an office on the aleja Szucha any morning and buy himself a transfer with one sentence." She put her hands flat on the map. "The section voted on Monday. It was close. Four to three, and the chairman was the one who carried it for you, and he carried it because of the eleven documents and not because of anything he feels. You have seven days."

"Seven days for what?"

"To bring him in," said Iga, "or to be told that the section will handle him, which means that he will be followed until we know the days and the hours and the habits of a man we have decided to kill, and then he will be killed in a doorway, by a group that does not know his name, and in a way that leaves a piece of paper on the body which will say that he was shot as a German officer by the Polish resistance, so that nobody in the world is ever going to know that the reason was you." She looked up. "I am not saying this to frighten you. I am telling you the process, because the process is the part of this that is not negotiable, and because you should hear it from me and not from a vote."

"You have a letter from him," said Krystyna. "You have three of the establishment pages. You know what he can do."

"We know what he has done. That is the past and it is in the ledger and it is confirmed and I have written it in my own hand, twice. The section deals in the next four weeks, because in four weeks the Russians will be on the Vistula, and in four weeks all the paper in this city is going to be burned or taken, and every German officer in Warsaw is going to be either dead or west, and the question the section asked on Monday was not whether Brandt is honest. It was: in the last week, on the day, whose side will a German officer be standing on, and what will he do with what he knows?"

"Bring him in, then," said Krystyna. "Where?"

"Here." Iga folded the map. "One hour of the road, the blindfold, the sandbags, the kitchen table, four hours of questions from the chairman, and a card in our files with a pseudonym on it, and after that he is ours in the way that everybody in this city is ours, which is his name in a drawer and a rope around his neck that we can pull whenever we decide that we have been wrong about him."

"That is what you offer him."

"That is what we offer everybody," said Iga. "Listen to me, because there is one more thing and then I will stop. I have watched you for four months and I know the shape of it, and I am not going to pretend about the shape, and I am not going to pretend to like it. I will pretend to be glad you are alive, because that one is true. The forest does not care who you love. It only wants the order to stand."

***

The room was a kitchen in a house in the Żoliborz with the windows papered and a table with four chairs, and it was used for this and for nothing else, and the whole of the arrangement was that she walked in with him at eight in the evening by the gate on the Mickiewicza and did not go in herself.

"You put this on," she said, at the gate, "and you put your hands behind your back, and I take your left arm, and there is a flight of steps inside, six of them, up, and the doorway, and you will not speak until somebody speaks to you, and you will not ask me anything, and whatever happens in the next four hours, you will do exactly what you are told, and if you are told to stand you will stand until you are told to sit, even if it is an hour, and there will be four people in the room and one of them will be a man in spectacles who does not sit down."

"And if I do all of that?"

"Then in four hours a man will write a name in a file and from that hour you will be a source of the Polish Home Army, and your name will be in a drawer, and one day when this is over, if there is an after and if anybody is left, somebody will take the name out of the drawer and it will either hang you or save you, and there is no way for either of us to know which."

Phillip Brandt stood in the street in the dark in the uniform of a captain of the Abwehr with a bag in his hand, and looked at the strip of black cloth in her hand, and said, "Tell them I will stand for an hour."

And she blindfolded him with her own hands, in the street, in front of the gate, and walked him through it.

The four hours are not the material of this book: they were paper and procedure and questions, and the content of them was established in the files of the section and was written down in a shorthand that has never been found. What is known about them, from the two people who were in the room and lived, is this: that the questions were put by Krupa in Polish and translated by her into German, and that the answers were given in German and then repeated by her in Polish, word for word, and that the whole of it went at half the speed of a normal conversation, so that a man who was asked four hours of questions had in fact eight hours of his life in that room; that the first two hours were about the German administration's filing systems, the formats of orders of the day, the registration of the paper of four offices, the pattern of the staff conferences of the garrison; and that at the beginning of the third hour, Krupa took his spectacles off and put them on the table.

"There is one question," he said, "and everything else that has been asked this evening was a matter of detail. I want you to understand that the answer you give will be written down and will be read by people who will act on it, and that it will be the whole of your value to the Polish state, and that we are not asking you to be a Pole. We are asking you to tell us what you will do in the last week of the war."

The room was quiet. There was a candle and a lamp and four people and a sandbag in the corner of the kitchen, and a woman with her hands in her lap translating; and if the whole of a novel can stand in one room, it stood in that one.

"In the last week," Phillip said, in German, "I will be in the garrison command's building on the Saski Palace, in the counter-intelligence section, at Room 14, at a desk, with the papers of the evacuation of the civil administration and the technical plans of the bridges of the Vistula in my room, because the technical plans of the bridges are held in the transport office of the garrison and are copied to four sections, and one of those copies comes to my section every month for the file on the state of the crossings, and nobody in my section has ever read it, and I have read it eleven times. On the day the orders come to destroy the bridges, the copies will be in the building, and the evacuation orders will be in the format that I can describe to you now, in three departments, with the code words for the population's movement in the second appendix. If I am alive and in that building in the last week of the war, and I am not arrested before it, then I will bring out the demolition plan of the bridges and the evacuation order, and I will bring them to the woman who is sitting at this table, and it will be the last thing I do in that uniform."

Krupa sat with his spectacles in his hand for four or five seconds.

Then he put them on and wrote, on the file card in front of him, in a small square hand, at the top, three letters and a dash, and the letters were KONRAD.

***

She took the blindfold off in the entry hall, with her own hands, in the dark, at half past twelve at night, and the two of them stood in a hall that belonged to nobody in a house in the Żoliborz with a bag at his feet.

"You are in the files now," she said. "You understand what that means. It means that if the house on the Śliska is ever taken, and the files with it, and if they ever read the drawer, then a card with a pseudonym on it and eleven pages of questions and answers will be in a German office on the aleja Szucha in a fortnight, and the four hours you spent in that kitchen will be read out to you in a different room by different men, and there will be nothing I can do about it, and nothing the Major can do, and nothing your father, and nothing your mother."

"I know what it means," Phillip said. He picked up the bag. "It means that I have chosen the side that will be doing the reading in the room. I have been a translator for four years and I have never once been in a room where the paper was mine, and I have wanted, every day of this war, to be in a room where the paper was mine. That is not noble. You asked me in Paris why I did not arrest you. Here is the answer, as an engineer's answer, so that we do not have to talk about it again: because a man who translates for four years knows what every document in a building is worth, and I have spent four years watching documents that were worth a city go into the wrong drawer. That is all. The rest of it — you, the orchard, the well at eleven metres — is a different department of my life, and it is not the department that signed the card."

"Good," she said. "That is the correct answer and I shall not tell the Major you said it, because he would be pleased by it, and I do not want him pleased."

***

The Colonel's summer was a slow fever in the villa on the Frascati Street and it had four symptoms, and she catalogued them all.

The first was the dinners: four in June, five in July, officers of the garrison and the administration, and the talk at them had changed from the talk of the spring, which had been the talk of a service doing its work, into the talk of a company that has heard something in the next room. They talked about the front the way men talk about a patient: the Hungarian plain, the Carpathians, the line on the Vistula, and the four sentences that everybody had heard and nobody would say out loud, and the sentences were shortened in July to two words, and the two words were *the river*.

The second was his ambition, which grew all that summer like a plant in a cellar: he wanted to be the man who broke the underground's intelligence before the Russians came, and he said so, at his own table, in his own study, twice; and he had put the whole of the counter-intelligence section on one programme, which was the identification of the underground's intelligence network in the city; and the programme had produced, by the middle of July, four arrests and one name, and the name was a railway clerk of forty, and the railway clerk's interrogations had produced a list, and the list had produced the arrests of two others, and von Hagen, at the end of July, had begun to believe that he was going to do it.

The third was the night.

He had begun to talk in the dark, in the small hours, in the way in which a man talks when the talking is not really a conversation: about the war as an accounting exercise; about his brother's farm in the Uckermark and whether the Russians would reach it; about Klaus, on the train from Posen, in the dark, with the crossing of a bridge, in the kind of detail that a man keeps for the room he sleeps in; and about loyalty. Twice in July he asked her, at three in the morning, in the dark, in the voice of a man talking about the weather: "What is loyalty, my dear? Not to the flag. To a person."

And she lay beside him in the dark with her hand on his chest and gave him the following answers, which were the truth in the way in which a lie can be the truth: that loyalty was a habit; that it was made by small things, in the morning, when nobody is watching; that a person is loyal to the thing he has already done three hundred times; and that she was loyal to him, and would be in August, and would be whatever he needed in the last week of the war.

The fourth symptom, at the end of June, was the trunks.

On the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth of June, four offices of the German administration in Warsaw began to send their registries west in crates on the ordinary trains: the labour office, the building administration, the food section, the office of the press. The removals were done at night at the goods yard of the Gdańsk station, and the Poles saw them, and the underground's analysts read them the way an accountant reads a ledger, and said, in a letter that went through four hands and came out on the Śliska, what they had said every week since April: an administration which is sending its archive west is an administration which has stopped expecting to come back.

***

On the last day of June she went to the Ogrodowa, to the address her brother had written on a scrap of paper in a courtyard in March and which she had carried in her head ever since, and took the fur coat to the woman who keeps coats.

The building was on the north side of the street behind a courtyard full of washing and a man asleep on a chair; the flat was on the third floor at the back, up the back stairs, with a card on the door that said *J. Grabska, tailoress*; and the woman who opened it was about sixty, small, in a black dress with a tape measure round her neck, with the hands of a person who has been sewing for forty years and the eyes of a person who has been doing something else for four.

She looked at the coat; and then she looked at Krystyna.

"Whose is it?"

"Mine."

"Whose was it? Sit down. This takes a minute." The woman turned the coat inside out on the table and went along the seams with her thumb and forefinger, feeling for a place, and found one, and took a needle out of her collar and picked at the stitching of the lining under the left arm and opened four centimetres of seam, and slid her fingers in, and took out a small piece of paper that had not been there when the coat was made in Paris in 1943, and unfolded it with one hand and read it, and put it back, and stitched the seam up with eleven movements in about forty seconds.

"That is a paper of the Building Section," she said, "with a file number on it, dated the twelfth of April. Which means that somebody in a German office put a piece of paper in your lining in April, and that the person who did it did it in a hurry, and that you have been carrying it against your ribs for two months in this city and in that villa and through every sentry post between." She put the needle back in her collar. "Your brother is a good boy and a bad tailor. The coat stays with me. I keep eleven coats. When you need it, I will have made it into something else, and when this is over you will come and get it, and if you are dead I shall sell it, and if I am dead my sister's daughter has the list of who the coats belong to, and it is in her head and not on paper."

"How much?"

"Nothing. It is a coat. I do not charge people whose coats are as expensive as your coat, because a woman who wears a coat like that has already paid for it in a way I do not want to hear about." The woman folded it, and put it in the wardrobe at the end of the room, among nine other coats, and shut the door. "Come back in the winter. Do not come back before."

***

Krupa sent for her on the fourth of July, at eleven in the morning, in a flat on the Zielna with a piano in it and a curtain that did not move, and he sat down for this one, which he had never done before.

"The section has an order for you," he said, "and it is the last order you will be given before the end of the war, or before the end of everything else, and I am going to give it to you in the whole of its parts, and you are not going to answer me tonight. You will answer me on Friday. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Four weeks," said Krupa. "By the end of July, before whatever happens in this city happens, we are to have the German garrison's defence plan for Warsaw: the dispositions of the units, the strong points, the ammunition state, the water supply, the demolitions — above all the demolitions, the bridges, the gasworks, the waterworks, the telephone exchange, the power stations. Everything a commander needs in order to know what he is holding, and everything we need in order to decide whether to fight in this city, and where, and with what, and for how long." He took his spectacles off and cleaned them on his handkerchief, which he did when he wanted a moment, and put them on again. "There are four copies of that plan in Warsaw. One is in the office of the garrison's chief of staff, one is in the fortress engineers' office in the Citadel, one is in the building on the aleja Szucha in the hands of the SS and police leader, and one is in the counter-intelligence section of the garrison command, in the study of the villa on the Frascati Street, in a safe behind a lithograph, and that one is the copy we are asking you for."

"And if I cannot get it?"

"Then the order says: or the identity of the person holding it," said Krupa, "so that the section may take the decision about that person." He looked at her across the piano. "You will not answer me tonight. You will note that there is nobody else in Warsaw who has ever been inside that room, and that the plan is a fortress plan and is being revised in July, weekly, which means the paper is being moved, which means there are hours in that house when it is on the desk and not in the safe; and you will note what I have not said, which is that the man who holds it is the man you sleep beside, and that the section knows it, and that the section wrote that sentence in the order on Friday, in my handwriting, and that I have read it eleven times since and have not found a better one." He stood up. "Friday, here, at the same hour. Bring me your answer and nothing else, and do not bring me a letter and do not bring me a reason. The order is not interested in reasons."

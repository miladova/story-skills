# Chapter 8: The Road to the East

The hotel in Berlin was in the Stresemannstrasse, three hundred metres from the Anhalter Bahnhof, and it had been taken over by the military administration in 1940 and had an officer's mess on the first floor with a chandelier that had survived the raids and a waiter of about seventy who had been there since before the other war.

They ate at a table with an Oberst of the SS and his adjutant and two civilians from the Ministry of Armaments, and the SS colonel was called Lanz and had a broad Swabian accent and a laugh he used the way a man uses a stamp, and his subject that evening was the east.

The east, according to Lanz, was not a country and not a front. It was an accounting problem, and it had been solved on paper in 1940 and the paperwork had never caught up with the arithmetic. There were four policies in operation in the Government General at once — a policy of labour, a policy of food, a policy of security and a policy which had no name and which the Ministry had asked about in writing — and none of the four offices knew what the other three were doing, and the reason for this was not chaos: it was design. "We have made a machine," he said, and he used *we* in the manner of a man who had never worn a uniform that got dirty, "which cannot be stopped by any one man, and cannot be understood by any one man, and that is its guarantee. A hundred years from now, nobody will be able to work out who gave the order, because there was no order, there was a process, and processes do not go to prison."

The Colonel, who had been drinking steadily and pleasantly since seven, said, "The process, Herr Oberst, will be investigated in writing by someone with a lamp and a table, and the paper will be found, because the paper is always found. I have been in this service for twenty-two years and I know one thing only: no one has ever burned all of it."

"Then we must burn more," said Lanz, and laughed, and the two civilians looked at their plates, and the conversation went on to the price of Hungarian wine.

She sat at the end of the table in a dress of grey wool with the pearls at her throat and did what she did at tables: she ate little, drank less, laughed at the right places, and kept, in a room in her mind, the following list, which she wrote out at two o'clock that morning on the back of the folded hotel menu with a pencil taken from the bedside drawer.

Lanz, Ostbahn, Prague. Four policies, four offices, one of them without a name. Then, from the adjutant's small talk: that the SD's section in Warsaw had been moved into the aleja Szucha building in January, that the new man with the Polish name — *let us be accurate, he is a Silesian* — was making himself unpopular with the military administration by requisitioning files, and that the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison command was to be reorganized as of the first of March, which was tomorrow, and that the office had not been informed and would be informed by telegram, which is the sort of thing an adjutant says when he is tired and important.

She also, in the corridor outside the dining room, while Lanz was having his coat brought, learned one more thing for nothing. The adjutant, who had been looking at her since the soup, said: "You are travelling to Warsaw, Fräulein. Take my advice and do not carry anything made of leather. They are taking leather at the frontier this month — handbags, cases, gloves — for the army, and a lady's bag made in Paris is worth a year of a customs officer's life, and he will take it, and he will be polite about it, and it will not be on any list." So she carried the compact with the false floor in her hand, and the pearls in the lining of her shoe, and the pencil-written list of the four policies in her glove.

***

The Ostbahn went north-east across the flat land of the Reich and then, at Kutno, over a line in the snow that was not a frontier in the sense of a wall or a fence; it was a line of lorries and a sentry post and a wooden building with a red-and-white pole, and beyond it the same fields and the same pines and the same snow with a different set of names on everything.

She had spent four years telling men what her views on Poland were, and had an answer for every question they could ask, and she sat in the corner of an officer's carriage for nine hours with a book open on her knee — a French novel, the only book in her case, because a book in Polish was a document and a book in German was a claim — and watched her own country arrive through the window in a series of specific facts, none of which she could say out loud.

The stations were the first fact. At every station there was snow and a queue: a queue for water at the pump, a queue outside a shop with a plank across the door, a queue of children by a heap of coal dust sweeping it into sacks with their hands. At two of the stations there were German soldiers throwing bread from the carriage windows to the children, and a man with a camera taking pictures of them doing it, and the children ran and the soldiers laughed and called them by a word she had heard eleven thousand times in Paris and had never heard from a Pole; and one of the children, a boy of about eight, got the bread and did not eat it, and walked off with it held flat against his chest with both hands, past the platform's end, into the snow, out of the photograph.

At a station whose name she never learned there was a siding, and on the siding, thirty metres from the passenger platform, eight cattle trucks stood with their doors wired shut with a double twist of wire and their ventilation slats closed by boards nailed across from the inside; and the snow had been on them for a day and there was no one near them at all. Everybody in the carriage looked at them for eleven seconds and then everybody looked at the newspaper, and the man opposite her, a paymaster with a chest full of ribbons from the other war, put out his hand with the bread roll in it and said, "Fräulein, you are not eating."

She and the Colonel and Rutz and the cases crossed the frontier of the Government General in the dark and went on through the night, and she did not sleep; and at about four in the morning, while the Colonel snored in the seat opposite with his collar and his mouth open and his hand on the briefcase and the briefcase on his knee, she leaned across and lifted the case by the handle, one finger on the catch, and took it off his knee slowly enough that he would have woken, and set it on the floor between them, and left it there.

The last fact was a station in the dawn where there was nothing at all to look at: a shed, a water tower, a man in a Polish railway uniform of the pre-war cut, the collar of his coat turned up, walking down the platform with a red lamp in his hand in the grey light, and no German at all anywhere in the frame, only a Polish railwayman going about his work on the railway that had been his work since 1928; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna looked at that man from the window of an officer's carriage for the twenty seconds the train took to go past him, and understood that she had come home, and put her hand flat on the cold glass, and the man did not look up.

***

Warsaw Ostbahnhof on the evening of the first of March 1944 was a platform with two hundred people on it, four arc lamps, a snow-fog standing in the air, orders shouted in Polish and German at once, and a crowd of men with hand sledges waiting behind a rope for the luggage of the officers' carriage.

She stood by the cases with her fur collar up and let it come in through all of her senses at once, the way a person lets cold water in: the smell of wet wool and coal smoke and pine, and under it the smell of a city in the fourth winter of an occupation, which has no name and is made of drains and dust and something burnt that has been burnt a long time ago; the sound of the language, in the station announcements and the porters and the two women on the far platform with their arms full of bundles, in the ordinary, tired, flattened Polish of a people at the end of a working day; and the snow, grey and trodden and crusted, with the marks of a hundred sleigh runners in it, and the whistle of a locomotive four hundred metres away, and a bell.

Then the car came up on the station forecourt with its headlamps masked, and Rutz put the cases in and jumped to hold the door, and the Colonel got in beside her with his gloves in his hands, and said, "Well. Here we are," and the car went out of the station and into the city.

She had prepared herself, in the way a person prepares, for the sight of it: the ruin, the posters, the queues, the German signs. What she had not prepared herself for was the geography of a city seen from a car at night in the winter of 1944, in which whole streets existed and were exactly as they had been, and whole blocks were gone; in which the tram rails ran through the snow as they always had and the tram stopped at a corner where there was no longer a corner; and in which the car, turning out of the station quarter, came on to a long straight street that she had walked on every day of her childhood, with the same kiosks, and the same plane trees, and, in the space where a block of four buildings had stood in 1939, a heap of brick with the wall of a stairwell standing in it and the ironwork of a staircase spiralling up into the air with nothing attached to it, like a drawing in a book about architecture.

"Look at that," said von Hagen, with real satisfaction. "The Poles in this city have been building for eleven months. You will not believe how much of it is standing. The Wehrmacht has put twenty thousand men on repair work since December — bridges, the water, the tram lines, the gas — and the city is fuller than it has ever been, because everything from the east that can walk is coming west with a handcart." He looked out of the window. "It is the busiest city in Europe and it is a ruin. Both things are true. It is what I like about it."

The German quarter began at the Ujazdowski Park and curled around it: the villas of the ministries, the requisitioned houses with the sentries and the masked lamps and the cars parked nose-in along the kerbs, the cafés where the waiters spoke German, and the pavements, in the middle of the German quarter, at eight in the evening, absolutely empty of Poles, because a Pole in that quarter after dark is a person with one chance.

The car turned off Bracka Street and stopped at a gate in a garden wall, and the sentry at the gate came to the window and looked in at the Colonel's face and stepped back and opened it, and the car went up a short gravel drive between banks of frozen snow to a white villa standing behind three bare trees.

The brass plate by the door read ZALESKI. Nobody had unscrewed it, and it had been there since 1940, and it was the last thing she saw before the door was opened by a Polish woman in a black dress who looked at the floor and said, in German, out of a mouth that had been taught the words the way a bird is taught them, "Good evening, gnädige Frau."

***

The house took forty minutes to walk through, and she did it with her coat still on, at half past eight in the evening, with the Colonel in front of her and Rutz behind her with a lamp, and the woman in the black dress standing in the kitchen doorway like a person waiting to be told whether she still had a job.

There were eight rooms on two floors and a cellar and a garden of nine hundred square metres with three trees, a shed and a well that had been filled in with rubble in 1939. On the ground floor there was a hall with a tiled stove, a dining room with a table for twelve and a sideboard that had been German property since 1940 and French property for a century before that, a drawing room with a piano and no furniture except four chairs and the piano, and a study with a desk, a telephone, two maps of the Warsaw district taken down from the wall and stacked behind the bookcase, and a safe.

The safe was a good one, a Viennese make of the nineteen-twenties with a three-tumbler lock, and it stood on the floor at the end of the study behind a lithograph in a plain black frame: a view of the Lilpop works at Burakowska Street, the big ironworks where the whole of her childhood's Warsaw had bought its axles and its cooking stoves, with the chimneys and the rail sidings and, in the corner, printed in 1913, the name of the firm in Polish. The Colonel had bought the lithograph in a shop on the Krakowskie Przedmieście in 1941 with a certificate of lawful purchase signed by an officer of his own staff, and he liked it, in the way of a man who likes an object for the shape of it, and by the end of the evening she had established three facts about the safe: that the key was not in the house, that the combination was not written anywhere in the house, and that the studies of the lock which she had learned in nine weeks in a flat above a laundry in 1942 would not open it and had never been intended to.

The piano in the drawing room was a Bechstein of about 1900, and it was out of tune by a semitone and had been for years, and the stool had a hinged lid; and under the lid was the sheet music of the family that had the plate on the wall outside, a stack of it two inches thick: Czerny, Schumann's *Album for the Young*, a set of Chopin preludes with the fingering pencilled in by a child in a hand that had been taught in a private school, the four-hands arrangement of a piece by Paderewski, and, right at the bottom, the score of a Polish opera with a name written inside the cover, in ink, in the neat teacher's script of a girl of about fourteen, and a date: 1938.

Nobody had touched the stool in four years. It was a good place to put things and she did not use it and never told anyone that she had looked.

Her room was the second room on the first floor and had a window on the garden, and the Colonel's room was the first, and between them there was a bathroom with a geyser, and the arrangement had been explained to her on the way out of Berlin in the tone of a man announcing a practical solution: "You will have the room next to mine as before. In Paris there were people. Here there are more."

She unpacked her own case. Rutz had brought it up and set it on the luggage bench at the end of the bed and had asked whether the Fräulein wanted anything pressed in the morning and she had said no, and he had gone; and then she unpacked it herself, item by item, standing in her coat with her gloves on, because the woman in the black dress was at the foot of the stairs with her hands folded and the maid's bell over the doorbell had rung three times in the hour they had been in the house and each time it had been for the Colonel, and there was no version of that first night in which a Pole carried a fur coat up the stairs for her.

The woman in the black dress was called Janina. She was about forty-five and had been at the house since 1940, and before that she had been at the same house for the family whose name was on the plate, and before that she had come from a village near Sandomierz; and when Krystyna said good night to her in Polish, at the door of the kitchen, the woman looked at her, once, full in the face, for exactly as long as it takes to read a name off a page — and then looked at the floor, and said good night in German, and shut the kitchen door.

***

On the morning of the second of March she went out at half past ten with a pretext she had made the night before, which was that she wanted to find a bookseller and a chemist's shop.

The Colonel said, "Take Rutz and the car," and she said that a woman who is driven to a chemist's shop in an occupied city is an object of interest to six separate offices, and he laughed and said that for a woman who had been kept by a colonel for eight months she had an unusual relationship with publicity; so she went out on foot at half past ten with her Paris shoes and her fur coat and her papers in her bag, and Rutz watched her from the window of the study with the notebook in his hand and did not follow her, and she walked down the gravel to the gate and out into the street, and turned left, and began.

The Krakowskie Przedmieście and the Nowy Świat were one street, and they were the street of her childhood: the church of the Holy Cross where she had been at midnight mass in 1935 with her mother's hand in the crook of her elbow, the university with its palm tree, the bookshops, the pastry cook's, the house with the baroque door-cases where a friend of her aunt's had had six rooms and now had one.

The street had been renamed. The whole of the city, at some point in 1940, had been renamed: the plac Piłsudskiego was called Adolf-Hitler-Platz, the aleja Szucha was the Strasse der Polizei, and the Nowy Świat had a German name that she never once managed to say out loud in four years, and the tram stop signs were in German and Polish, and the German came first and was bigger, which she noted in the way she noted the smell of the place: without comment, in the file.

There were three things on the street that morning that she had never seen in Paris.

The first was the notices. They were pasted on kiosks and on the walls of buildings and on a purpose-built stand at the corner of the Świętokrzyska, and they were German proclamations, printed on grey paper in Polish and German, of the executions that had been carried out in the city in the preceding week: the date, the district, the number of persons, and the names in two columns, also the names in the cells of the Pawiak, and, at the foot of each notice, the sentence that said that this was done by way of reprisal and that the responsibility lay with those who had done the murders of German soldiers. Four people were reading it, in the way people read such a notice: in silence, at a distance from each other, in the cold; a woman of about sixty, a man in a railwayman's coat, a boy of fifteen, and a girl with a basket, and none of them said a word and the wind had come under one corner of the sheet and lifted it, and the man in the railwayman's coat put out his hand and pressed the corner flat against the wall to stop it flapping, and then went away.

The second was the loudspeaker. It was on a pole at the corner of the Świętokrzyska and the Nowy Świat, at a height of about four metres, and it had been there since 1943 and it played from eight in the morning until nine at night: propaganda in Polish, in a voice that the city had a name for — the barker — the news read out from the German papers, and the instructions to the population about the surrender of weapons, about black market prices, about the punishment of those who helped the partisans; and it was the loudest single sound in Warsaw, and it came out of the machine as a stream of ordinary Polish sentences in a flat, cheerful, unpunctuated voice, and she stood on the pavement in her fur coat and listened to the language of her own country being spoken by a wooden box, and everything in her body made a movement that had to be stopped at the neck.

The third was chalk. On the wall of a courtyard on a side street, at the height of a man's shoulder, somebody had drawn in chalk a figure that looked at first glance like a simple anchor, an anchor with a curving line of cable, drawn quickly and badly and with the tail of the P roughly done — the mark that the resistance used, the anchor that stands for Poland Fighting — and somebody else had walked past since with a wet rag and smeared the middle of the chalk so that the mark could still be read by anybody who knew it and could not be described as such by anybody who did not; and she stood in front of that wall for a second and a half, which was a second longer than was wise, because the two men in the courtyard gateway were watching to see whether the woman in the fur coat would stop at the anchor; and she walked on.

She was not recognised.

That is the thing she had feared for three years, in the flat above the laundry, in the fittings at Odile's, in the bed of the Colonel in the house on the avenue Henri-Martin: not one person, but the city itself, and the city did not know her. No one looked at her twice. She passed three women of her mother's generation in a quarter where her mother had been known by name, and they looked at her coat and her shoes and her mouth and their eyes went over her face without stopping, because a face is the last thing a Pole in the fourth year of the occupation looks at; and a boy of about seventeen, selling the *Nowy Kurier Warszawski* from a board at the corner of the Nowy Świat with a stack of newsprint under a brick, looked at her for as long as it took to decide that she was a German lady and therefore no custom of his, and then looked at her shoes with the expression of a person looking at something out of a shop window, and then, as she went past, spat on the pavement — not at her, at the pavement, three feet behind her, and she heard it and did not turn around, and she got to the corner of the Świętokrzyska and stood there with her hands in her coat pockets and found that she was not able to breathe properly, and had to stand for eleven seconds and count, the way she had been taught to count, before she could go on.

***

She walked to her own house at twenty past eleven in the morning.

The palace on the Nowy Świat had been built in 1780 with a courtyard behind a Doric gate and the family's arms in the pediment, and it had been her father's house, and before that her grandfather's, and the sale of it had been arranged in 1940 by the German administration in the manner of all such arrangements: a letter, a valuation, a hearing, a period of fourteen days, a payment into a blocked account, and a requisition paper, which had arrived with the courtesy of a firm of architects and had told the family that the house had been taken over "for the duration of the present emergency".

Two things had been nailed to the pediment since. The first was a sign, painted on a board in the standard lettering of the administration, black on white: MILITÄR-KRAFTFAHRPARK, and under it in smaller letters the designation of the department, and, at the top of the sign, the eagle.

The second was a lamp. It hung on an iron bracket over the gate of the courtyard where the carriages had stood, and it was lit at twenty past eleven in the morning, because the workshops were in the outbuildings and the pit was under the coachhouse floor, and a mechanic's lamp on a long cable was hung on a nail under the gate arch and went on burning all day, so that the courtyard had a cone of yellow light in it at eleven in the morning with the snow coming down through the light in small separate flakes.

In the courtyard there were two lorries with their bonnets up and one with its bonnet down, and a Kübelwagen, and a small mountain of tyres under a tarpaulin, and four soldiers working, and a wheel off a lorry standing by itself in a puddle of melted snow. In the corner where the well had been, in the corner of the courtyard where her mother used to have a fir tree put up at Christmas for the children of the household's servants with a candle on every branch, there were two oil drums and a heap of coal.

She stood on the far pavement for four minutes.

The sentry at the gate looked at her twice, the second time with a little more attention, because a woman in a fur coat looking at a motor pool for four minutes is a thing that gets reported in the barracks that evening; and a man came out of the gate with a delivery book and a pencil behind his ear and looked at her and said something to the sentry and laughed — and she turned and walked on, up the Nowy Świat towards the brow of the hill, and did not cry, and did not pray, and did the other thing that she had been doing since 1940, which was to inventory: the gate had a sentry in the daytime only, and there was a bell that had been out of order since 1939; the lamp over the arch was on an iron bracket with a screw eye that would take a wire; the lorry park held eight vehicles in the courtyard and the outbuildings would take more; the coal heap was against the wall of what had been her mother's morning room; and the snow on the roof of the north wing had not been cleared and the gutter had pulled away from the wall in the corner, and if they did not clear it, the water would come down the wall in the spring and take the plaster off the drawing room, which had a painted ceiling of 1791, and nobody would care, and it would be gone, and she inventoried that too, because it was on the list.

***

The coal depot was two streets further on, on the corner of a courtyard, and there was a queue of about thirty people outside it in the snow: women with their heads wrapped, a man with a handcart, two boys kicking a stone, and an old woman with a sack on the ground in front of her.

She did not see her father at first. She saw the coat: grey, with the cuffs turned, with the collar too wide for the man in it, and then she saw the shoulders inside the coat and the back of the neck above the collar, and the neck was a very old man's neck, thin and white and bent forward, and there was a leather satchel over his shoulder, the satchel he had carried to the school in the years when he was still permitted to teach, and there were two dictionaries in it, and she knew that from the way it hung.

He was standing at the head of the queue with his feet on the snow in worn boots and his hat in his hand and his hand on the satchel strap, looking at the ground; and his hair was white, all of it, which it had not been; and he was — she measured it in the two seconds the measurement took, the way she had been trained — about nine kilos lighter than the man who had stood in the hall of the flat on Mokotowska Street in September 1939 with a lamp in his hand and said *Niemiec* to a boy in a German coat.

She was fifty-five metres away, on the far pavement, and her body was crossing the street before her mind had finished with the first sentence.

She got off the far kerb. There were five or six cars a minute on that street in 1944 and about forty pedestrians, and there were four people in the queue within fifteen metres of him, and the ones who would look at a woman coming across the road towards an old man would be the woman with the sack and the man with the handcart and the two boys and probably the entire queue, because a queue in Warsaw in 1944 is an audience with nothing else to do; and she was in the roadway, in the slush, three metres into it, when the car came round the corner of the Świętokrzyska with its headlamps on and slowed and stopped at the kerb in front of her, and the orderly in the front seat got out and came round the bonnet and opened the rear door and stood there holding it.

She stood in the middle of the road with the snow in her eyes and looked at the open door of the car, and looked back at the queue, which had begun to look at her, all of them, thirty faces, because a stopped German car on that street was the only event in the day; and her father had turned his head by then — he had turned it at the sound of the car, in the way of a man of sixty-seven, and he was looking at the road, at the car, at a woman's back in a fur coat getting into the back of a car with a German orderly holding the door, and she got in because there was nothing else to do with her body at that moment that did not end with a German soldier asking a Polish schoolmaster a question.

The door shut.

The car went on down the Świętokrzyska towards the German quarter, and she sat with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the back of the orderly's neck; and von Hagen, who was in the other corner of the back seat with a bundle of papers on his knee and his spectacles on, said, "You have been walking. Rutz tells me you were not recognised; I find that very hard to believe and I have decided to believe it."

"Where did you come from?"

"The office. I have been in the office since seven and I shall be in it until ten tonight for the next eleven days, because the counter-intelligence section of this garrison is now mine as of this morning, and the whole thing has been kept in a drawer by a man in a cavalry coat who has not written an honest line of paper since 1941." He took his spectacles off and folded them. "Which brings me to a domestic matter, and I want to say it once and then not again. Your papers are in order. Your ration book is in the drawer of the desk in the study, which is not locked, because nothing in that house is locked and nothing in that house is to be discussed on a telephone. The house is yours to run with Janina; she is a good cook and she will never like you, and you will not reproach her for it. You will have the car when you want it and Rutz when you do not, and the car has the right of the German administration to be on this city's streets after curfew, which is the only liberty in Warsaw that is worth anything." He looked out of the window at the ruins going past. "And you will not walk in this city alone. Not in the German quarter, not on the Nowy Świat, not to a chemist's shop. I have not asked you to be a prisoner, my dear; you are free to go anywhere you please, provided that you go in the car and come back in it. There are eleven of them in this town now, in the SD's building on the Szucha and in the Abwehr's office and in the police stations, and every one of them would be delighted to be the man who was seen with the Colonel's woman on the Nowy Świat at eleven o'clock in the morning."

He put the spectacles back on and unfolded the papers.

"You have come to a beautiful city," he said, "and it is not a city you can walk in, and I am telling you this because you are the first thing in my life that is pleasant, and I have no intention of losing you in a street in Warsaw for the sake of a chemist's shop."

# Chapter 4: The Christmas of the Wolves

The winter of 1943 was the third cold winter and the coldest, and Paris had a way of being cold that Krystyna had not known in any other city: not the flat, honest cold of Warsaw, which came at you off the snow and got into the seams of things, but a wet, breathing, indoor cold that got into the wallpaper and the plaster and stayed there like a smell.

Coal went where coal went. The Germans had their own allocation, and the concierges had theirs, and the hospitals had a great deal less than either, and everybody else burned whatever Paris had that was made of wood and not nailed down. In the courtyard of the building on the rue Saint-Honoré a walnut escritoire had stood since the start of October slowly reverting to the elements under a tarpaulin; by December it had lost its drawers and half of one leg, and the wood of a Frenchman's desk of 1780 was keeping a linen maid of 1890 and her two granddaughters warm at night, one drawer at a time.

At half past nine in the morning the temperature in the rue de la Pompe was two degrees above freezing and the pavements had a yellow-grey glaze on them that had been frozen and trodden and frozen again, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked on it in boots made for her in a shop that had a German licence on the window, and there were thirteen people standing still on the corner, and they were standing still because of the two cars.

The cars were French, black, with their headlamps masked down to slits, and there was a German officer sitting in the back of the first one with the window down, and he had his arm out of the window and his hand on the door, and he was not doing anything at all; he had the face of a man watching a train come in.

The police were French and they went into the building in the way of men doing an unpleasant job in front of their own neighbours, which is to say quickly and without looking at anyone. They were in the building for a quarter of an hour. At the end of the quarter of an hour they came out with a family: a man of about fifty-five in a good coat with no hat, a woman with her hair down and a fur over her nightgown, a girl of sixteen or so in a grey skirt and a cardigan, and an old man who came last, holding a clock.

The old man was the one everybody looked at. He came down the steps of the building with the clock held in both hands against his chest, a walnut clock with a brass face, and he was not being hurried and he was not being made to carry it, he was simply carrying it because it was his, and the policeman who walked behind him had his eyes on the middle distance and his mouth slightly open, and the whole street watched the clock go past.

At the car, one of the French police put out a hand to take it, and the old man said something that Krystyna did not hear from twenty metres away and heard perfectly, as the entire street did, in the pause the sentence made. The policeman took his hand back. The old man got into the second car with the clock on his knees and the door was shut by a man who then put his hands behind his back and looked at the pavement.

The girl had begun to cry, and had stopped, and the woman's fur had come off one shoulder and nobody had put it back.

The German in the first car lifted his hand an inch off the door and the cars moved off down the rue de la Pompe towards the boulevard, and it took eleven seconds, and then the street was a street again and the traffic came round the corner, and a woman in a queue outside the baker's, three metres away from Krystyna, said to nobody in particular, in the flat voice of a person discussing a draught: "They will want the building now. They will put some office in it. There was a whole family in there on the second floor and they were here before the war, and now they will put an office in it, and my sister will not be able to get her milk."

Krystyna walked on down the rue de la Pompe.

She had had, in the five seconds after the old man said whatever he said, the following experience: her legs had decided to cross the road, and her hips had turned, and her coat was already moving, and she had put her hand out to the left in a gesture that came from nowhere and that was, she understood with a kind of clinical distance, the beginning of a movement to take the clock and put it down on the pavement and stand in front of the old man; and then the training, which had been installed in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement by a man with a stick and no vices, had come up through the floor of her and taken hold of her by the spine and turned her the other way, and she had walked on.

There was nothing to be done. That was true, and she had spent two years learning the shape of it, and the shape was this: a woman who interferes at a round-up in the rue de la Pompe is a woman who is arrested, and a woman who is arrested is a woman whose handbag is searched, and in the handbag of Marion Herschlag on the twenty-second of December 1943 there was a compact with a false floor in it, and a scrap of paper with a telephone number on it that belonged to a hotel in Toulouse, and the names in her head of forty-one Poles in the Paris region whose names she could not say under torture even once, and if that sounds like a boast it is not: she had been given two lessons about the business of being asked, and she had learned them, and the whole of her courage, from 1940 onwards, had been converted from the sort that runs into streets into the sort that walks past.

She knew all of this. She went over all of it while walking the length of the rue de la Pompe with her hands in her pockets, and it worked, and by the corner of the avenue she was steady, and there was still, at the bottom of her, a small, cold, permanent thing the size of a fingernail that had been installed during those five seconds and that would be there for the rest of her life, and she catalogued it, too, without slowing down, the way a woman catalogues a splinter.

At Odile's, at eleven, she was fitted for a bodice and said nothing about the rue de la Pompe, and Odile did not ask, because Odile had known by nine o'clock, because everybody in the quarter knew by nine o'clock, and there was a rule between the women in that workroom that was never written down: no news, no predictions, no list of what had happened this week to other people.

At half past eleven, while the two women in the back room sewed, Krystyna took the lid off the box under the cutting table.

It was a dressmaker's box, pasteboard, with a picture of a rose on it. Odile had begun it in 1941 as a spool box and it had become, over two years and four months, what it was now: the contents of the pockets of the people who had been taken. A tortoiseshell comb. Two rings. A child's glove, knitted, with a thumb. A photograph of a young man in a military hospital with his arm in a sling and his cap on the bed behind him, inscribed on the back *à ma petite soeur, souvenir de Bar-le-Duc*. A prayer book with a name in it in pencil. A key. Four letters, banded with a rubber band that had perished.

"I have had that box for two years," Odile said, without turning round, "and I have never once looked in it, and if you take one thing out of it and put it in your bag, I shall take the whole box down to the courtyard and burn it, and you will not be angry with me, because you know the rule."

"I know the rule."

"You want to."

"Of course I want to."

"Then put the lid on it and stand still, and consider this: you are the only person I have ever met who is a worse sentimentalist than I am, and you have more to lose by it." Odile spat a pin into her hand. "Also, the shoulder is now right. Look at the shoulder."

***

The supper at the Majestic on the evening of the twenty-third was an event in the German social calendar of Paris, which is to say that it was held in a ballroom with eleven chandeliers and a balcony, and that the Reich paid for it in an accounting sense, and France paid for it in every other sense, and that a photograph of it appeared three weeks later in a German illustrated paper under the caption *Christmas Eve in Paris* and was reproduced, after the Liberation, in a great many accusations.

There were two hundred and forty people at it. There was an orchestra of French musicians who were paid in money and in passes, and who played what an orchestra plays. There was a buffet with a boar's head and oysters from Brittany and a bar at which any man in a German uniform could drink anything in the world. The wine list had been requisitioned from a cellarer in the Loire; the tablecloths came from a firm that had had a Jewish proprietor in 1938 and had a German administrator now; the holly had been flown in from Norway, in an aircraft that had been scheduled to take a general to Berlin.

And there was a woman standing at the end of the room in a grey dress with a pearl necklace, who was the Colonel's Marion, and who was known, by the two hundred and sixteen people who did not know her, as the charming one, the Austrian, the one who is too good for him.

She had done the arithmetic on the way there, in the car, with the flake of cold steel in her chest that was three days old: the room would contain, by her own count, the heads of eleven German departments in France, four colonels, one general, and, at a table near the balcony, the wife of the man who had ordered the deportation of four thousand stateless Jews from the southern zone in August, who was a small woman of about fifty with a sweet face and a taste for Schubert; and she intended, in the course of the evening, to be introduced to all of them, because the whole of her work had become exactly this: the acquisition, through the medium of being delightful, of the small facts that men drop in rooms that they think are safe.

They ate. She talked to a Luftwaffe major about the acoustics of a concert hall in Dresden, and to a French contractor about the price of cement in the Seine department, which he quoted to her to the franc, because he had told her four times that a woman could not understand a business and had then explained the whole of it, and to a German clerk of the administration's food section, who was drunk, and who told her that the meat that had come to Paris in the second half of the year had been coming from a slaughterhouse in the east, and that the section had a row with the railway over the refrigerated wagons, and gave her the terminus.

At eleven o'clock the orchestra stopped for the speeches, and after the speeches, as was the custom of that year, the room sang carols.

The Colonel put his hand on her elbow and steered her through the tables the way a man steers a boat he is proud of.

"Fräulein Herschlag will sing," he said, to a general who was asking about something else; and then, to her, in a lowered voice, with a pleasure in it that was genuine and that she never afterwards managed to hate in the way she wanted to hate it: "Do the third one. The one with the roses."

The third one was *Es ist ein Ros entsprungen*, which is a German carol of the sixteenth century about a rose growing in the winter, and which she had learned at the Institute of the Sacred Heart on the Rennweg in Vienna, in the year when she was fifteen and enjoyed singing, and which she could now perform in a manner that made German officers of fifty put down their glasses and look at the middle distance and remember a church in a town that was not on any map any more.

She stood by the piano, in her grey dress, two hundred and forty people in the room and the chandelier lights up, and she sang it in a clear, small, tender voice, in German, for the Reich, in the ballroom of the German military administration in Paris, on the twenty-third of December 1943.

They were silent while she sang. Some of the older ones put their hands behind their backs. The wife of the general who had the sweet face and the taste for Schubert had tears in her eyes at the third verse, and looked at her afterwards with an expression of pure love, and took her hand in both of hers and said, "Child, where were you in Vienna?" and Krystyna told her the street, correctly, and the woman said, "I know it, I know it, there was a confectioner on the corner," and there was in fact a confectioner on the corner, in the file, in a photograph, in a paragraph of a report on the parish of Rennweg written by a Polish monk in 1937, and it was this, this, that had cost four thousand francs.

Then there was applause, and a general made a small speech of thanks which did not name her, and the orchestra came back, and the Colonel, who had been standing at the piano with his hands behind his back and a look on his face of an almost unbearable satisfaction, took her by the elbow and led her out of the room through a side door into a small salon with two windows and a fire and no one in it.

"There," he said, and set her down in a chair as though she were a piece of porcelain that he had carried across a room without breaking. "You see. They will not forget that. In this country they will remember a voice for thirty years and a battle for thirty days, and when the war is over and the arguments begin, there will be men in Berlin, in important rooms, who will say, I once heard a woman sing a carol in Paris, and it will be in the record along with everything else."

He took the box out of his jacket pocket and put it on the table between them.

She knew the box. She had seen that box in the window of a jeweller on the rue de la Paix in October and had admired it in the correct way, which is to say for four seconds, in the presence of a man, and without ever mentioning it again, because mentioning a thing twice is the amateur's error and mentioning it once in the right voice is the whole of the art.

"There is a necklace in it," von Hagen said, "which belonged to the wife of a banker who is no longer in Paris, and which I have bought for you, on account of the singing."

She opened it. Twelve millimetres at the top and seven at the bottom, forty-one pearls, a clasp of white gold with a small diamond set in it, and a card inside the lid with the jeweller's name and the date and a figure written in pencil in a hand that was not the jeweller's. Nine hundred and forty thousand francs.

"Christian," she said. She used his first name three or four times a year and it cost her something every time. "I cannot take it."

"You will take it. It is bought and it is paid for and the money went to a man who is going to need it."

"It is far too much."

"It is exactly enough," said the Colonel, and took it out of the box and put it round her neck himself, standing behind her chair so that she had to hold her hair up with both hands, and his fingers were cold and a little clumsy and he was careful not to catch her hair, and the whole of it took eleven seconds. "There. Now. Some arithmetic, my dear, in the middle of the evening, because we are both of us practical people. You are twenty-nine years old—"

"I am."

"You are twenty-nine, you are an Austrian without a family, you have been kept since June by an officer of the German army at an expense that you have never once asked about, in a city that is going to be hungry in the spring and very unpleasant in the autumn. When I am posted, I shall be posted to the east. I am not a young man and I have no illusions about the Russian winter. If anything happens to me, you will be a woman in Paris in 1945 with an Austrian passport, no money, and a great many photographs, and the pearls will be the difference between a room and no room, and you will remember, I hope, that I thought of it. That is the arithmetic. There is also the other arithmetic, which is that I do not intend to be separated from the one thing in my life that is pleasant."

She turned in the chair and looked up at him.

There he was, in the firelight, in a well-cut dinner jacket, fifty-four years old, with the duelling scar on the left cheek and the enormous ugly careful hands, and there was nothing in his face that she could safely have called love and there was certainly nothing in it that she could safely have called contempt: there was possession, and there was habit, and there was a kind of dry, formal tenderness that was the exact product of his class and his generation and his dead wife and his dead son; and she found, sitting in that chair with the pearls already warming at her throat, that she could not meet his eyes, which was a thing that happened about four times a year, and which always frightened her far more than the fear of being caught.

"Say yes," he said.

"Yes," she said.

"To Warsaw."

"To wherever you are sent," she said; and because that was not quite what he had asked, and because she understood what he had asked, she added, smiling, with her hand out for his: "You will have to buy me a coat."

"You have four coats."

"I shall need a fifth. For the east."

He laughed, and pulled her up out of the chair, and put her hand on his arm, and took her back into the ballroom, and the orchestra was playing something from the year before the war, and in the middle of the room four couples were dancing, and a general of artillery was being told something funny by a Frenchman with a red face, and the woman with the sweet face was finishing a plate of oysters with the neat, quick, terrible appetite of a small bird, and the whole room smelled of perfume and roasting and pine and cigarette smoke, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna walked into it on the arm of the man who had just given her the price of a house and told her, in a German drawing room in the fourth year of the war, that she was the one thing in his life that was pleasant.

She danced twice, once with him and once with a general, and then, at half past one, with a young captain of engineers called Weiler, who had been amusing her at table with the story of a bridge at Orléans that had been built nine centimetres too narrow and had needed a Frenchman to be arrested for it, which he told entirely without malice, as an engineering anecdote, and which she had found, over three courses, to be one of the more revealing things she had heard that week.

He was not a bad dancer and he was twenty-eight, and he smelled of shaving soap rather than of saddle soap, and in the middle of the last turn he said, confidentially, with his hand flat on her back in the correct position, "Every man in this room hates me at this moment. You are aware of that."

"I am aware that it is Christmas."

"That is a much kinder way of putting it than the Colonel will."

The Colonel was twenty feet away at the edge of the floor with a glass in his hand, talking to a major of the Luftwaffe, and he watched them for the length of the turn and not longer, and when the music stopped she thanked the captain and left him in the middle of the floor and came, on her own, without being fetched, back to the man whose hand on her waist was the deed to her.

"Have a good time," von Hagen said.

"I have had a good time."

"That is the third word you have used for it."

"It is the only one there is."

He put his glass down on a table without looking at the table, which was a thing he did to tell her that he was angry. "Weiler is a fool. His father made the family's money in potash in the nineties and the son builds bridges for the infantry. When he is posted to Russia, he will be dead by March, and he will spend his last week writing letters about the width of roads." He turned and looked at her with an expression of perfect courtesy. "If you would like to dance with him again, my dear, you may. Nobody in this building would think anything at all. It is a party, and you are the decoration, and the decoration is permitted to be turned."

She had learned, in six months, that the way to survive this particular move was not to apologise, since apology implies a fault, and not to defend, since defence implies an accusation; the way to survive it was to become dull.

"I am tired," she said. "If you would let me call the car."

He looked at her a moment longer with the courtesy in place, and then he took out his watch and looked at it, and said, mildly, "Rutz is at the side door. My coat is the one with the fur collar. Wait in the car."

***

At the side door on the rue Dumont d'Urville, the car was standing with its engine running and its headlamps masked to two slits, and the breath of it went up in the cold in a long white wedge, and Emil Rutz was standing beside the rear door with his greatcoat buttoned to the chin and his cap on, holding the door open against the wind for a lady who had not yet come.

Rutz had been with the Colonel four years, and with the German army twelve, and before that he had been with a baker in a village near Bamberg whose name he said in the way other men said the name of a country. He was forty-one and heavy in the shoulder and soft in the middle, and he had a photograph of a niece behind the windscreen of the Mercedes and a wife in Bavaria who wrote to him every fortnight in a hand that got smaller each year.

"Fräulein Marion."

"Emil. It is very cold."

"It is very cold," he agreed, in the tone of a man who has said this sentence forty times since October and will say it forty times more, and he held the door and then, because she was early and there was nothing to do, he took out of his tunic the small black notebook he kept there, and licked his thumb, and wrote in it.

She had seen the notebook before. Everyone in the house had seen the notebook: the car's mileage, the hours of the sentries, the bottles of wine drawn from the cellar, the days on which the silver was counted, the days on which the laundry went out. A German orderly's day book is the most boring document in Europe and it is also, in the hands of the right reader, the most complete: it records every movement of every person in a house, in order, in a small careful hand, for four years.

He wrote the hour, and she watched him do it, from a metre and a half away, with her hands in her muff, in the light of the masked headlamps.

He wrote: *Fr. Marion, abgeholt 1.35.*

He looked up from the notebook while his pen was still on the paper, and his eyes came over the page and met hers, and he knew that she had read it — had watched him write it, in fact, which was worse, because it made the reading deliberate — and the two of them stood there in the cold at the side door of the Hôtel Majestic with two hundred and forty Germans and a French orchestra on the other side of the wall and the car's breath going up white between them.

Then Rutz closed the notebook and put it back in the tunic pocket over his heart, and said, "It is only the hours, Fräulein. The Colonel wishes to know when the car is used. It is the petrol."

"Of course," she said.

"Yes," said Rutz, and looked at the pavement, and then at the street, and then, at some length, at the small buckle on the car door, which did not need looking at. He had never in four years been anything but correct with her, and he was not being incorrect now: he was being a man with a wife in Bavaria and a photograph of a niece and no politics at all, standing in a cold street, having just written down the hour at which a woman left a building, and knowing — as every servant in every occupied country in Europe knew everything and said nothing — that this hour, and the eleven other hours like it in the book, were the beginning of a document.

"Fräulein Marion," he said, "you have not got your stole."

She had not. It was inside, in the charge of a nearly blind old woman in a grey overall with a brass ticket, and the reason she had gone back into the building at all.

She went back into the building, and along the corridor, and the rest of it happened in a service corridor in the half-dark with a little brass ticket in her hand, and a man on the top step of a staircase who said her name.

***

The side corridor at the Majestic was the corridor the waiters used and the officers used to smoke in, and at half past one in the morning it had four waiters in it, all of them sober, all of them French, all of them standing in the attitude of men who are counting the hours, and she went along it to the cloakroom to fetch her stole, which was in the charge of an old woman in a grey overall who took the ticket and looked at it for a long time because she was nearly blind, and it was while she was standing there, in the half-dark of a service corridor, with the pearls under her chin and the little brass ticket in her hand, that she heard the boots on the marble and turned and saw the man at the top of the stairs.

He was in the uniform of a captain, and he was holding his cap in both hands in front of him in the way of a man who has just come in out of the cold, and he was standing absolutely still, and he was looking at her.

He was not looking at her the way men looked at her at the Majestic. He was not looking at the dress, or the pearls, or the mouth. He was standing on the top step of a staircase in a German administrative building in Paris with his cap in his hands and his face doing nothing at all, and he was looking at her the way a man looks at a door that he has spent four years believing was bricked up.

She had, over the previous six months, been looked at by perhaps eleven thousand German officers, and she had a system for it, and every part of the system had stopped working, all at once, like a clock that has been dropped.

The captain opened his mouth.

Somebody on the other side of the corridor said *Herr Brandt, the Colonel has been asking after you*, and the man at the top of the stairs did not move and did not look away and said, in a voice that was entirely level and about two degrees off the temperature of the building, one word.

"Krystyna."

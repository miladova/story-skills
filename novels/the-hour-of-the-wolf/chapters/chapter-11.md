# Chapter 11: The Colour of a Uniform

The office was on the second floor of a building on the Świętokrzyska whose ground floor had been a bank and whose first floor had been a firm of patent agents, and it had been requisitioned in 1940 and fitted out with a map of the Generalgouvernement on a frame, two filing cabinets of German steel, a stove that smoked when the wind came off the river, a telephone on the wall and a second telephone on the desk, and a Polish charwoman called Pani Wanda who came at seven in the morning and was searched on the way out by the orderly, every day, in the same way, with her rag and her bucket and her tin of polish on the floor beside her, and who had never in four years been caught with anything because she had never tried to take anything, which was itself a piece of information that was in Phillip Brandt's head and not in any file.

There were five of them in the room: Hauptmann Lindemann, who was thirty-three and had been an architecture student and had a good ear and a laugh he used the way other men used a bayonet; two clerks, one of them with a limp and one of them with a stomach; a sergeant of about fifty who had been a policeman in Cologne; and Phillip, who was a Hauptmann of the Abwehr, on secondment to the garrison command's counter-intelligence section, and whose actual job, written down nowhere, was to be the man who read Polish.

The Polish in that building was the wall between them and everything they did. There was no department in Warsaw in 1944 that did not have a Pole in the cellar of its language: the German administration issued its orders in German and printed its proclamations in both, and the reports of the Polish police came in Polish, and the underground press came in Polish, and the intercepted letters came in Polish, and the denunciations — there were eleven of those a day, on average, at the Świętokrzyska, in a woman's hand or a man's hand or a spidery nervous hand, about neighbours and Jews and partisans and men who had been overheard saying something — all of them, all day, in Polish, in a language that no German in that building could read except the man who had been born in it.

So Phillip's morning was this: a stack of paper on the left-hand side of the desk, and the same stack, translated and boiled down, on the right.

The underground press first: the *Biuletyn Informacyjny*, when a copy had been taken off a boy in the street or bought on the black market for two hundred zlotys by a sergeant who put it on his expenses; the *Wiadomości*, the *Głos*, the papers of the Peasant Battalions and the Socialists, all of them, in five or six different degrees of recklessness. He did not translate them word for word; he summarised each of them into a paragraph of German in a standard form, with the date of issue, the source, the circulation estimate, and then the content: the murders of Poles by the Germans and by the Ukrainian police, the raids, the reprisals, the executions, the trains, the prison, the arrests of named men, the warnings about the szmalcownicy who sold hiding people to the SD, the descriptions of German personalities after whom the underground's courts had passed sentences, the obituaries, the appeals for money for the families of those shot, the appalling ordinary business of a city that had decided to describe its own destruction every fortnight in an eight-page stencil.

Then the police reports: the blue police, the Polish police of the Generalgouvernement, who were the most careful writers of Polish in the war, because every comma they wrote could get them shot and every comma they left out could get them shot too. Then the intercepted mail. Then the summaries for the section: a daily sheet in duplicate, the top copy to Oberst von Hagen and the carbon to the file, and Phillip had established in the first week that a sheet of paper with his summaries on it was read by four men and quoted by one, and that the one was the Oberst, and that the Oberst read the paper carefully and slowly with a pencil in his hand and made marks in the margins, and that the marks were always about people and never about facts.

Between us, Phillip, a man with Polish like yours and no wife is either an idealist or an idiot, and I have never known an idealist to survive a winter.

That was Lindemann at eleven in the morning on the second of March, in the second week of the new arrangement, with his feet on the stove and a newspaper on his face, and the two clerks and the sergeant and Pani Wanda all in the room, and nothing about it worth an answer.

He was thirty-three and he was going to be thirty-four, and the whole of his war had been conducted like a man hunting for the exit in a burning theatre: he had been in the Abwehr in Paris, in Warsaw, and in between at the General Staff's office in Berlin for eight months where he had learned the entire architecture of the coming collapse, and he talked about the war as a business cycle, and he was right about most of the things he talked about, and he had one of the best languages in the Amt, and by the winter of 1944 he was doing all of the arithmetic out loud in front of whoever was in the room, which is either courage or an entirely different thing. "Between us, when the east comes apart — and it will, presently, and I will not be the one who says so in a letter — there will be four hundred people in this town who claim to have been against it from the beginning, and I intend to have written evidence, in somebody else's hand, dated before the end. Have you ever noticed that the Poles keep their receipts?"

A week later, on the ninth of March, the crates came in.

They came on a lorry from the Ostbahn goods yard with a delivery note in three copies and two men to unload, and they were the Paris station's files, six crates of them, with the Abwehr's mark stencilled on the side and the rain on the stencil; and the whole office stood around them in the afternoon and read the numbers on the outside and argued about whose section they belonged to, because the Admiral's people had been taken apart in Berlin in February and everything that had belonged to them was now the property of whoever could reach the goods yard first, and there were three claimants in Warsaw that week and one of them was the SD, which was ten minutes by car away on the aleja Szucha and which was better dressed than anybody in the Świętokrzyska.

The Fräulein file was in the second crate, in a folder of brown cardboard with a ribbon on it, and it was forty-one pages, and Phillip was given it on the eleventh because he was the Polish speaker and because the file was about a woman.

He worked on it for two days.

That is the thing to understand about Phillip Brandt, and the reader has to be given it plainly before anything else happens to him: he was good. He had the best analytical mind in that section of the garrison command, and the whole of the discipline of the thing was in him, and he did the work for the work's sake and had done since 1939, which is the exact vice that makes a man like that destroy himself slowly, because the work is never asked to be good, only to be useful.

He read the file the way it had to be read: twice, and then once more from the back.

Page one was the case reference and the name of the officer who had opened it — *Sturmbannführer B. Kalbach, Sipo/SD Paris, avenue Foch, 14 January 1944* — and a note in the same hand that the case had been transferred to the Sicherheitspolizei's office in Warsaw with the papers of the Abwehr, and that the material from the French file was to be integrated, and that *the immediate hypothesis of the case is a woman.*

Pages two to nine were a chart.

It was a beautiful piece of work and Phillip had seen only four like it: a hand-ruled table of eight persons of interest to the case, with their names down the left, and then three columns which Kalbach had headed *Sprache* — language — *Stunden* — hours — and *Zugang*, which means access. Under the eight names there were four who had been crossed out, and beside each crossing-out there was a date and a single word: *verhaftet*, arrested; *verhaftet*; *entlassen*, released; *verstorben*, died. There was a chart for a whole city on that page: a dressmaker on the rue Saint-Honoré, a Frenchwoman who had worked in a German office on the avenue Henri-Martin, a chauffeur, a typist, a seller of papers, two men in the black market, and the fourth name in the column, which was in a different ink and which ran right down the page and out of the bottom of the chart into the margin, where there was a question mark in German, and the question mark was followed by three words: *Person bei Hagen?* — a person in Hagen's household?

Page fourteen was an artist's drawing, done from descriptions by a French police draughtsman of the prefecture, of a woman in a hat: seven drawings, actually, of seven hats and one face — and the face, drawn from a composite of three witnesses, was an egg with eyes and was no more like Krystyna Stankiewiczówna than it was like Pani Wanda, and Phillip looked at the seven hats for eleven seconds and had to put the file down and go out of the room.

Page twenty-two was the paragraph.

It was a summary of an interrogation of a waiter from a café on the rue de Rivoli, taken on the eleventh of January 1944: the waiter had served a German office on the first floor for two years and had been asked about voices, and had said, in the way of a man who has been asked a question three times and is beginning to understand that his life depends on the answer being interesting, that there had been one woman among the German ladies who spoke German with a particular thing in it — and the SD clerk had typed the thing and then written underneath, in Kalbach's hand, three words in Polish: *warszawski zaokrąglony akcent* — the Warsaw rounded accent, the vowel at the end of a word that a person from three hundred kilometres to the west can hear and cannot produce, the vowel that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna had carried into Germany with her, in her mouth, like a coin in a shoe.

He read page twenty-two four times, and then he closed the folder and put the ribbon round it and went down the stairs and out into the Świętokrzyska and bought a packet of cigarettes from the kiosk at the corner and came back up and opened it again and started writing.

What he wrote, that afternoon, was three pages of German summary in the standard form of the section. It was honest, and it was accurate, and it said, in the flat language of a staff paper: that the French material had produced no identification; that the chart listed eight persons, of whom four were disposals by arrest or death and four remained open; that the draughtsman's portrait was of no value; that the linguistic material was of interest, being a single witness's memory of an accent with no recording, no comparison material, and no corroboration; and that the hypothesis *a woman in the household of an officer of the Militärbefehlshaber* was unsupported by any evidence in the file except the fact that the case had been opened by a man who believed it.

It was true. Every word of it was true, and it was the best summary the file had received in eleven weeks, and it made the case look like what it also was: a hunter's work, and thin.

And in the last paragraph of it, in the section headed *Empfehlung* — recommendation — he wrote that the case would benefit from a comparison of the linguistic material against the personnel lists of the women employed by the German administration in the Government General, and that this should be requested from the police in the Generalgouvernement in the ordinary way, and he signed it, and put it in the tray with the carbon, and went out at half past four into the snow and stood in the street for a moment with his hands in his pockets, and then walked home.

***

The Brandts lived in Wola, on the third floor of a corner building near the gasworks, in two rooms and a kitchen, because in May 1940 the house on the Hoża Street had been requisitioned for an office of the labour section by the ordinary procedure — a valuation, fourteen days, a payment into a blocked account, which could not be touched and had not been touched and, by 1944, would have bought a bicycle — and the family had taken the flat in Wola because it was cheap and because Albrecht Brandt could walk to the works from it.

The works were still there. That is the other thing about that family that must be set down plainly: the Lilpop works at Burakowska Street were still standing in 1944 and still making things, and Albrecht Brandt was still going to them at sixty-one years of age, five days a week, with a leather satchel, and signing what was put in front of him in the capacity in which the German administration employed him, which was that of a *technischer Leiter* of a Polish firm under German administration, a phrase which meant everything and nothing, and which he had explained to his son exactly once, in 1940, in the following way.

"There is a paper in the drawer of my desk at the works. It is an application for membership of the Party, and it was submitted in my name by somebody in the administration in October 1939 without my signature, and it has never been withdrawn, and I have never been a member of anything in my life and I never will be. If they come for me, it will be that paper that they find first, and it will not matter what I say about it. I am telling you this because I want you to understand the exact arithmetic of your own career, and the arithmetic is this: a man who signs one paper is not the same as the men who rule with it, and there is no third thing. There is only the paper and the men who write it, and every honest man in this country is standing on a line that was drawn by somebody else."

Then he put his hat on and went to work, and Maria Brandt said nothing at all, which was her answer, and it was the answer he had been getting for thirty years and had learned to live with: his wife's silence, which was not agreement and was not forgiveness and was a complete statement.

The flat in Wola had two things in it out of the house on the Hoża: the photograph of the family in front of the works in 1928, taken by a photographer who was employed by the firm, with the furnaces behind and Albrecht Brandt in the middle in a hat and Maria on a chair with her hands in her lap and a boy of eleven standing very straight and looking at something other than the camera; and the piano, which was a small upright and which Maria played on Sundays, badly, and always the same three things, and always the Polish ones.

She spoke Polish to her husband when she was tired and German when she was angry, and she had been tired and angry in the same proportion for four years; and she had one subject that she never once mentioned, which was her own family, of whom nothing had been heard since the autumn of 1942 and about whom there was nothing to be found in any office in Warsaw, and Phillip had spent eleven weeks of his salary in 1943 making enquiries that had produced, in the end, a single line in a file of the labour administration: two names, a street in the Mokotów district, and the word *umgesiedelt*, resettled, and a date.

They ate at the kitchen table, the three of them, at eight in the evening, in two languages; and the mother asked her son, in Polish, about his day, and the son answered her in Polish, and the father asked, in German, about the office, and the son answered him in German, and the whole thing ran along the table like two currents in one river; and Phillip ate what was put in front of him and did not say what he did all day, and his mother did not ask, and his father asked and was told what a technical director's son can tell a technical director, which is nothing at all.

***

On Sunday the nineteenth of March at eleven in the morning, Lindemann took him to the café on the Nowy Świat, which was one of the four in the German quarter that Poles were not permitted to enter, and which had a plate-glass window, a doorman in a green uniform, an inside room with a stove and eleven tables with cloths on them, and a clientele of officers of the garrison and of the administration, and the girl at the bar was Polish and said nothing at all to anybody.

They were at the third table on the left with the window at their shoulder. Lindemann had ordered coffee and cake, and was talking about a transfer to the Ukraine and the price of a woman's bicycle, and at half past eleven the door opened and the room came alive in the way rooms do when rank comes in, and the whole party came in out of the snow: five officers of the garrison, of whom the first was a tall narrow Prussian of about fifty-four with a duelling scar on the left cheek who took his hat off and looked round the room the way a man looks at a room he has paid for.

"Von Hagen," said Lindemann, and stood up.

The Oberst came to the table with his gloves in his hand and looked at the two of them for a moment in the manner of an inspection, and then was cordial: he was, Phillip saw, a man who did the cordial part of the courtesy carefully and by hand and had no interest in whether it was returned; and he said, "Brandt. You are the summary. I have been reading your sheets for three weeks and I have been meaning for a week to tell you that the one you wrote on the twenty-ninth of February is the best piece of paper that has come out of this section in a year, which is a low bar, but the sentence about the paper circulation was correct and nobody in this town has yet told me anything correct about paper." He pulled out the chair. "Lindemann, you have been telling him about me."

And behind him, in the party, in a grey dress with a fur coat over her arm and the pearls at her throat, and a hat that was not any of the seven hats in the drawing, was Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, and she looked at Phillip for about as long as a stranger looks at a stranger, and then she turned to the man next to her and said something and smiled.

The room turned to look at her. It took about four seconds and it was a phenomenon that Phillip had seen once before, in a corridor of the Hôtel Majestic in Paris in December, and it had the same cause: a woman who is beautiful in a room full of officers has to give them something to do with their eyes at the end of it, and what she gave them was a laugh — small, private, entirely at ease, in German, at something an Oberst of the engineers had said about a bridge — and the whole room took it, and one major laughed too, and a captain at the far table looked at his plate.

Phillip sat, and ate cake, and translated nothing at all.

He knew, in the first four minutes, three things, and he knew them in order.

The first was that she was doing exactly what he had spent the winter refusing to put into words. Not the thing in the corridor at the Majestic and not the thing in the church on the rue Saint-Honoré, but the whole arrangement, every day of it: that she was in that room in a fur coat on the arm of an Oberst, and that the room was full of the men who ran the occupation of her own city, and that she was laughing and taking coffee, and that the file in the second crate on the Świętokrzyska was about her.

The second was that the man whose arm she was on was the man who had been given the counter-intelligence section on the first of March, and that the counter-intelligence of a garrison exists for nothing except to find the people who are doing to the Germans what she was doing to the Germans.

And the third was that he had, on the previous Thursday at four in the afternoon, written and signed a three-page summary of a file in which the case officer had written *Person bei Hagen?*

***

He held her chair because the Oberst made an introduction and because a man at a table with a lady standing next to him holds her chair; and that is the whole of what happened between them in the room, and it lasted about two seconds, and she put her hand on the back of the chair as she sat down, and her other hand, under the level of the table cloth, closed round his fingers for the length of one heartbeat and left something in them and was gone.

He did not look at it. He put his hand in his pocket as if he were putting his handkerchief away and did not take it out for four minutes, and in those four minutes he took part in the following pieces of conversation, in the following order: a comparison of the officer's brothels of Paris and of Warsaw, which he did not enter; the question of whether the Vistula bridges would be blown, which he answered in the flat way an intelligence officer answers a technical question — that the bridges had demolition chambers that had been built in 1939 by the Polish army, that the chambers had been surveyed by the engineers of the garrison in February, and that the report of that survey had been circulated to six offices and would therefore be in Berlin by the summer, which was the only kind of answer that could be given in a café; and Lindemann's theory about air raids on the eastern railway junctions. And he laughed twice, at the right places, and he ate the rest of the cake, which was dry.

Then the party got up to go, and there was the business of the coats and the door and the doorman, and the Oberst put his glove on and said, "Brandt. Monday, nine. My office, with the summaries," and went out; and she went out after him with the fur coat over her arm; and at the door, in the middle of the movement of five officers and a woman and a doorman and the cold air coming in, she turned her head, and looked at Phillip Brandt for the second time in the entire war, for about a second and a half, from the doorway, with her hat in her hand.

And then the door shut, and the room went back to being a room, and Lindemann said, "Between us, that is the most expensive woman in Poland, and I include the horses," and Phillip laughed, and paid for the coffee.

***

The flat in the Żurawia where he had a room was the flat of a widow called Zofia and had four rooms and a kitchen, of which the Germans had had three since 1940: himself, and two lieutenants of the railway section who were in Posen that week, and the woman and her sister in the back room, and a piano in the front room that nobody played, and a cat that belonged to nobody and slept on the sofa, and a Polish maid who came in the mornings and did the three German rooms and was searched on the way out, like everybody.

He shut the door of his room at four in the afternoon and put the thing from the café on the table.

It was a piece of paper of the kind that comes on a roll, folded twice, with the edge torn, and it had one line on it in Polish, written in pencil, in a hand he had last seen on a school exercise book that had been left in the hall of a house at Wierzbów with a silver pencil on top of it in the summer of 1939, and the line was:

*The girl from the orchard asks what your office knows about a woman in a hat.*

He sat and looked at it for eleven minutes.

It was not a love letter. That is the first thing to say about it. It was not the beginning of anything either, or the resumption of anything: it was an act of a kind he had read about in files and never once seen, which is a person in an impossible position calling in the only debt she has left — and it was, at the same time, exactly what a trap looks like, in the third week of March 1944, in a city where the SD's building was fifteen minutes by car and a man in a grey overcoat had written something down about Krystyna Stankiewiczówna on a railway platform in Paris.

He did the arithmetic in five lines, on the back of the cigarette packet, the way he had been trained.

If she was a German agent and the note was a trap, he had already passed the gate: he had held her chair in a café and had taken a folded paper from her; and the only piece of evidence that could convict him of anything was on the table in front of him, one line in Polish, in a hand nobody could connect to anybody, and he could burn it in thirty seconds.

If she was what he had spent a year and a half understanding her to be, then a woman was doing in the middle of a German administration what three hundred thousand people in that country could not do with rifles, and there was exactly one man in Warsaw who could tell her anything at all about the file, and she had found him in nine days, and she had found him in a café, in front of Lindemann, holding his fingers for one heartbeat under a tablecloth — which meant she had selected him, and which also meant she was desperate, and which also meant the file was worse than he had let himself believe.

He wrote the answer in the same Polish and with the same pencil, on a piece of paper from his own notebook:

*The man who wrote the file is called Kalbach and he is here. There are eight names on his chart and four are dead and there is a chart of accents and a drawing of seven hats and a face that is nobody. He has no name in the file. He will have one in the summer, because the man who is hunting you is regular, methodical, and does not stop; and there is one other thing you should know, which is that as of the first of March he is working for the Oberst who takes you to cafés, and that the Oberst reads every paper that crosses my desk, including the ones about you. Do not write to me again in a place where I can be seen. If you need the file, I will bring it to you on paper you can eat, and then you will owe me nothing and I will owe you nothing, and neither of us will have been in a café together.*

He read it through twice; and then he burned it in the stove, and burned the note as well, and then swept the ash out of the stove with his hand and burned the sweepings on a plate, twice, because he had written in 1941 that a man who leaves ash leaves a document that a person can read with a lens and a table, and then he went out into the snow at half past five in the afternoon of the nineteenth of March 1944 and walked to the corner of the Bracka and bought a bunch of violets he did not want from the flower kiosk there, and while he was paying for them he put a book in the window of the junk shop behind the kiosk, between the frame of the window and the glass: a Polish novel of the nineteen-thirties, second-hand, with a woman's name written inside the cover in pencil, and inside the back cover, in a hand he had learned in a kitchen in Wola, in ink, four lines of what a man who is on the wrong side of everything could find out about a hunter, in the summer, in a file, from the inside.

He was at that corner for four minutes, which is what it takes to buy flowers and put a book in a window and walk away, and he was seen by exactly two people: a boy of about ten on a sledge in the road, and a man in a grey civilian coat standing at the tram stop on the other side of the Świętokrzyska with his hat down and a notebook in his hand, who wrote in it, in a column, two words, and who — this is the part that Phillip Brandt would not learn for another two months, and would learn in a way that cost four people their lives — was not writing down what he had seen; he was keeping a list of the German officers who passed that corner on foot, because a German officer on foot at a buying-and-selling corner was a man with something to sell, and Kalbach's assistant had started the list in February and it had one name on it before, and it now had two.

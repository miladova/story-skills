# Chapter 7: The Trap of Small Talk

There are three ways to know that the weather has changed, and none of them is the weather.

The first, on the fourth of January, was the concierge. Madame Ravel had a lodge with a window on the courtyard, a cat, a husband who had been at Verdun and had a certificate on the wall, and a manner with German officers that was neither servile nor insolent and had kept her exactly where she was for four years. On the fourth of January she was called to the Militärbefehlshaber's office in the rue de la Pompe and questioned for two hours and forty minutes about the household on the avenue Henri-Martin: who came, who went, what the Fräulein did with her mornings, whether letters came for her, whether she had ever been seen with a parcel. She was questioned by a civilian, and there was a shorthand clerk, and she was given a chair and a glass of water and, at the end, a receipt for her identity card. She told the household about it, because she was a Frenchwoman in an occupied city, and there is no version of that life in which the concierge does not tell the cook and the cook does not tell the maid and the maid does not tell the house.

The second, on the fifth, was Odile.

There was a man in the workroom off the rue Saint-Honoré when Krystyna came at eleven for a fitting, which had never happened before in two years: a heavy man of about fifty in a civilian overcoat of good cloth, with the collar buttoned and his hat on his knees, sitting on the chair by the cutting table with the pattern paper under it, being shown the order book. The order book of a Paris dressmaker of 1944 is an extraordinary document. It contains the names of three hundred women and the measurements of their bodies and the dates of their fittings and, in Odile's hand, in the margins, a private system of marks — a small cross for a client who paid in food, a small circle for one who paid nothing and always would, and one letter that was not a letter, for the nine Jewish clients, three of whom had been sent away in 1943 and six of whom were in the same building as the shop, behind a partition in the attic, being fed twice a day and taught, in the evenings, to be somebody else.

The man asked Odile eleven questions about her clients, in French with a German construction, in the tone of a man doing an inventory of buttons, and at the end of the eleven questions he stood up, and put on his hat, and said: *we note.* Then he went out into the street and stood a moment, adjusting his gloves, before he crossed to a car that had been waiting with its engine running.

Krystyna stayed for the fitting. She stood on the box in the cold room for thirty-five minutes in a half-made bodice while Odile pinned the shoulder and did not say one word about the man, and the women in the back room sewed, and at the end, when she was dressed, Odile said, "The shoulder is right at last," and gave her the paper pattern folded in four, and inside the folded paper, as always, there was a cutting from a newspaper four lines long: the previous week's drop instructions.

The third, on the sixth, was a bicycle. Wiktor's courier was a boy of nineteen from the seventeenth arrondissement, with a face like a knife and a laugh he could not keep in, who came on a bicycle with a leather saddlebag and who had been doing the rue Saint-Honoré run since October. On the morning of the sixth of January the bicycle was found leaning against the wall of the passage at the corner of the rue Saint-Honoré and the rue de l'Échelle, with the saddlebag still on it and the leather strap still buckled, and nothing else was found at all, on that street or in that quarter or in any of the three places where a body can be put in Paris in winter and be found in the spring; and there is no way to say whether he talked, and no way to say whether he is alive somewhere in a cellar with his teeth gone, and the network's rule about it — Wiktor's rule, printed nowhere but taught in a flat above a laundry in the fifteenth in the autumn of 1942 — is that a missing courier is a dead courier, that the drop is dead with him, that you do not go to look, and that if you have ever liked him you must behave, for the next three months, as though you had not.

She walked from the passage to the fitting and from the fitting to the avenue Henri-Martin and did the whole of the journey without ever once looking behind her, which is a discipline and not a virtue, and by the time she reached the gate she had made the list, and the list had six names on it, and the fourth of them was Odile Boin, and the third was Wiktor Janowski, and the first was her own.

***

On the evening of the sixth of January, at half past ten, she found the sheet of paper.

The study on the first floor of the villa had a desk with a leather top and two drawers which the Colonel did not lock, because he had once explained to her, with the mild pleasure of a man setting out a principle, that the correct arrangement of a German officer's papers is that they are either in the safe, in which case they are safe, or on the desk, in which case they are not secret. The blotter was replaced every Monday by the maid and the used sheets were burned, and the reason Krystyna knew this was that she had made it her business, in July 1943, to know it, and had spent four months learning the day and the hour, and had read seven of those sheets in the two and a half minutes the house allowed her.

The sheet on the desk on the evening of the sixth was wrong.

It was wrong in four ways, and she took them in the way a seamstress takes in a badly tacked sleeve. It was not on the paper of that office, which was a grey rationed paper with the watermarks of a mill in Angoulême; it was a white paper with a rag content and a stiff, hard surface, of the kind the SD's own sections used. The typing was too clean: the machine that had produced it had a new ribbon and a perfect alignment, and every machine in the villa typed either with a dropped *e* or with a habit of climbing the last line of a paragraph — two faults she could have recognised in the dark. The ink was dry, and the memorandum was dated the fourth, which was two days earlier, and it had been typed that afternoon. And there was no carbon under it, which was the fourth wrongness and the one that made her stand absolutely still with her hands at her sides, because the Colonel was a Prussian of the old school who wrote everything in duplicate and triple, and a document in his study with no carbon under it had not been produced by him: it had been placed.

The subject of the memorandum was the naval batteries at Saint-Nazaire: the emplacements, the two new ranging sets, the ammunition that came by night from a siding at Montoir, and the fact that the defences of the Loire estuary had been reinforced in December with the guns out of the old battleships. It was the thing the network had been asking for since November. It was the most expensive piece of paper she had seen in her life.

She stood over it in the dark, with the sentry's boots going round the garden outside, and thought about the man who had made it, and what he knew, and what he thought he knew about her, and she came to the following conclusion, which she wrote down later in her own head, in Polish, because it was too dangerous to say even in French:

*They have not decided that it is me. They have decided that their source is a woman, because somebody has told them that the leak is close to an officer's household, and they have chosen a colonel who does not lock his desk. They put the bait where they think the source can reach it, and they will watch the paper, not the person, because a paper can be watched from a distance and a person cannot. Therefore it is not a trap for a hand. It is a trap for a market: the day it reaches London, they will know where the leak is.*

Which is exactly correct, and which had a second implication, and the second implication was the one that decided the rest of the chapter: on the day the memorandum reached London, the hunt would be closed, not on a name but on a house. On the avenue Henri-Martin.

She looked at the perfect clean sheet for another minute. Then she took a sheet of the villa's own grey paper out of the drawer, and copied onto it, in her small upright hand, the four essential facts of the memorandum — the two ranging sets, the siding at Montoir, the guns, the date — and folded it and put it in her shoe, and left the clean sheet exactly where it was, and went down the corridor, and put her hand on the banister rail, and up to bed at eleven minutes to eleven.

That night she lay in the dark and did not count the sentry, and made the plan in three parts, and the second part of it was a woman in the twentieth arrondissement and the third part of it was a dress fitting; and the first part of it was that a service which has not found its source will keep looking, and that looking is what kills networks, and that the only way to stop a hunt is to give it something it can eat.

***

The arithmetic was done in twenty minutes, on the morning of the seventh of January, in the bathroom at the top of the house, with the tap running.

She wrote the six names on a piece of paper in pencil and put a mark against each of them.

*Odile Boin, 55, dressmaker, rue Saint-Honoré.* Already questioned; already visited; her order book has nine Jewish women in it, six of them alive in her attic; another visit and it is not Odile who is lost, it is the six, and the six include a violinist from Łódź and the wife of a doctor who worked at the Rothschild hospital in 1939 and who has been sitting in that attic for eleven months listening to the sound of the shop bell. Odile would die before she gave a name, and she would give the six names because they are in her house and not in her mouth, and because a sixty-year-old woman with a search warrant in her workroom does not have the option of dying before they open the door of the attic. Odile was out.

*Wiktor Janowski, 52, rue Saint-Honoré.* Out, and for a different reason: Wiktor was the standard, and if the SD took Wiktor then everything she had was over, and — she wrote this down, burned the paper in the washbasin and washed the ash away with the tap — it would be over more quickly than if it were anybody else, because Wiktor had shown her, in a flat above a laundry in 1942, what a man does under a certain kind of questioning, and had said at the end of it: *You have not been caught. That is the whole of our morality, and you are going to have to do without any other.* He was out because he was hers, and she knew it while she was doing it.

*Marion Herschlag.* Out for the obvious reason.

*Céline Marchand, 39, avenue Henri-Martin, wife of a manufacturer who had gone to Vichy in 1940 and stayed there.*

She sat on the edge of the bath with the pencil in her hand and looked at the fourth name for a long time.

Céline Marchand was the best friend of Marion Herschlag. She was the person who had taken the Austrian girl who appeared in Paris in 1940 under the wing of the German administration and had taught her which couturier to use and which restaurants to be seen in, and who had made her laugh eleven times, and who had — in October 1942, at a table in a restaurant on the rue Royale — asked her, in the voice of a Frenchwoman discussing a hat, where exactly in Austria she had learned to say *the road to the north* with the vowel of a Russian, and had then, because she was Céline, laughed and taken a second helping of the pâté and forgotten the question entirely.

She had asked it once, in front of four people, and had not noticed the answer, and she was the only person in the German orbit of Paris who had ever heard the seam in the accent and had thought about it for longer than a second.

Céline Marchand was thirty-nine. She was a collaborator the way a cat is a collaborator: she liked the cream and did not care who owned the dairy. She was warm, indiscreet, uncurious, generous, greedy, frightened of dentists, and had a brother's photograph on her piano with nothing else in the flat that she would not sell for a price. She was sleeping with a Frenchman in the propaganda office, and she sold nothing to anybody, and she knew nothing that mattered, and she had a cousin in the préfecture whom she mentioned every fortnight because she was proud of him.

There was no case against Céline Marchand. There was a case against a Frenchwoman with a German-protected friend, a cousin in the préfecture, two rooms with a telephone in them, a husband in Vichy, a mouth with nothing in it but the accident of an accent question asked once at a dinner table in October 1942 — and it was a case that would end, in the SD's arithmetic, with the possibility that a woman in the German orbit had been selling the office she could reach, which is to say the office of the friend who had the Colonel's confidence, which is to say that the leak was a woman close to the household.

That is the whole of it, and the reason was not that Céline Marchand was cheapest: it was that hers was the only name in Paris whose arrest would close the file on the avenue Henri-Martin, and that the alternative was not her own survival — she had made her peace with her own survival in a flat above a laundry in 1942 — but the survival of an attic in the rue Saint-Honoré with six women in it and a dressmaker of fifty-five who had told her, in November, with pins in her mouth, that a person should not look at the box under the cutting table.

At eleven that morning she telephoned the villa to say she would be late for luncheon, and at half past two she walked into the flat on the avenue Henri-Martin with a dress box under her arm.

***

Céline Marchand's flat had four rooms on the second floor of a building of 1904, with a concierge who took parcels and a telephone in the hall on a table with a marble top and a plaited cord long enough to reach the drawing room, which was a thing she liked to mention, because it had cost eleven thousand francs in 1938.

They had tea, and they talked about the spring collections and about a German colonel's wife who had ordered a suit from Odile and then refused to pay because Odile was French; and Céline laughed about it for four minutes, and at half past three she went to change because she had an appointment at four with a hairdresser who was making a fortune, and she said, going out of the room, *mon Dieu, the state of the world and my hair* — and Krystyna was left alone in the flat with the maid, who was seventeen, from the fourteenth arrondissement, and who was in the kitchen with the door open; and with the hall, and the wardrobe of the small room, and the telephone.

She did the telephone first, because it could be heard.

There are things a person can do in another person's house and things a person cannot, and the difference is entirely made of time: four minutes alone in a room in Paris in January 1944 is enough to put a parcel into a wardrobe, and eleven seconds in a hall with a maid in the kitchen is enough to lift a receiver.

She telephoned the flat from the post office on the rue de Rivoli at ten past four, from the sixth box, with her collar up and a scrap of silk over the mouthpiece, and she spoke German. Not the German of Marion Herschlag, which half the officers of the Militärbefehlshaber knew and one of them slept beside; a soft, level, unplaceable German, and she said four sentences: that a parcel had been left at the flat that afternoon by a boy from a bureau; that it was wrapped in brown paper and was in the wardrobe in the small room; that Madame was not to open it before Saturday; and that the woman speaking was sorry to have missed Madame. Then she rang off, and the girl on the exchange made a mark on a pad because the call had gone on for a minute and a half, and nothing whatever came of the mark.

Thirty seconds later, from the same box, she dialled the SD's number on the avenue Foch and asked for the office of Sturmbannführer Kalbach, and gave the same soft German to a man with a Berlin accent and a cold, and she said six sentences: that if the gentlemen were still looking in Paris for the source of the material that had been reaching London since the autumn, they might look in the flat of a Frenchwoman on the avenue Henri-Martin, second floor, four rooms, telephone in the hall; that a parcel of papers had arrived there that afternoon in brown paper and would be gone by Saturday; that the lady of the house had a husband in Vichy and a cousin in the préfecture and a friendship with the German administration; and that the man receiving the call should write down the address and not ask who was speaking, because asking would only make him late.

Then she put the receiver down on its hook, and paid for the two calls in coins, and walked out into the rue de Rivoli as far as the corner of the rue Castiglione, and stood there for four minutes with her hands in her pockets, looking at nothing at all.

She was back in the flat at twenty-five past four, in the drawing room, with her gloves off, talking to the maid about the maid's aunt in Rouen; and Céline came out at half past four in a hat, and made her choose between two of them, and Krystyna chose the grey one and was right, and the two of them went down the stairs together, and Céline got into a taxi and Krystyna walked home along the avenue in the rain. That is the last hour of the friendship, it is nine hundred and forty seconds long, and it went perfectly.

***

They came for her at half past one the following day, which was a Saturday, at the restaurant on the rue Royale where she had gone to eat oysters with two other women, and Krystyna was eleven streets away in a taxi, coming back from the rue Saint-Honoré, when the taxi stopped at the lights at the corner of the rue Saint-Honoré and the rue Royale because a car had been drawn up across the pavement.

She saw the whole of it from behind the driver's glass, at a distance of about thirty metres, and she was not supposed to be looking and she looked, because a person who has arranged a thing must watch it happen or she will spend the next twenty years imagining it in eleven different versions.

There were two men out of the car and one woman, in civilian coats, and the woman was in the restaurant before the men; and then there was Céline Marchand in the doorway with a napkin in her hand that did not belong to her, and a waiter behind her with his arm out to steady a table, and her hat coming off her head and falling down the two steps into the street, and one of the men picking it up and not giving it back. She talked the whole way to the car, with her hands up, and the words did not carry thirty metres and her face did; and then a hand on the top of her head and she was in the car, and a man folded the hat in half and put it in his coat pocket and got in after her, and it was over in eleven seconds.

The light changed. The taxi pulled away. Krystyna sat in the back of it with her hands in her lap, in the grey afternoon, and thought — in her own small upright hand, in her head, in the office of a flat above a laundry — about the fact that the timing had been right to the minute, and that the report would be on Kalbach's desk that evening with the address on the avenue Henri-Martin in it, and that by Monday the file would be one page shorter.

Then the taxi went round the corner and the arithmetic was finished, and she found that she had bitten through the inside of her cheek, and she spat the blood into a handkerchief, and put the handkerchief back in her bag, and went to Odile's and had a fitting, because the fitting was in the diary, and because in the diary she was a woman whose friend had been arrested at luncheon and who had gone to her dressmaker because there was nothing else to do with the afternoon; and Odile pinned the shoulder and said nothing, and gave her the folded pattern, and there was nothing inside it.

***

Odile was taken on the twelfth of January, at ten minutes past six in the morning.

There were three cars in the rue Saint-Honoré, one of them drawn up on to the pavement at the corner, and the shutters of the workroom were up, which they never were at that hour, and there was a man by the third car smoking with one hand cupped over the cigarette in the cold; and Krystyna saw it at a distance of forty metres on the far pavement, on her way to the villa's car, and she kept walking, and turned into the rue de l'Échelle, and was out of the quarter in four minutes, because the first rule of the manual on the last page, in the tiny type, in Wiktor's dry voice, is that a person who is near an arrest leaves, and that a person who watches an arrest is a person who has been seen watching.

Inside, hidden behind the partition in the attic, six women spent that day and the next and the morning after it in the dark with one bucket and one bottle of water and no sound whatever, and on the third night the two remaining seamstresses of the workroom walked all six of them out of the building in three parties, through the courtyard into the rue de l'Échelle and up the boulevard to three addresses; and three of the six lived, and three did not.

For eleven days Krystyna did not know whether Odile had talked.

Eleven days is not long. It was the whole of the rest of January: the milky coffee at the villa, the fitting hour in her diary with no fitting in it, the orders she sent through the priest on the Thursday, the two requisitioning officers who came to the drawing room on the fourteenth and drank the Colonel's cognac and made jokes about the Crimea, and the nights when she lay in the dark and heard her own heart and could not sleep in the way the manual said to sleep.

On the twelfth day she went to the rue Saint-Honoré at the fitting hour anyway, because a woman who does not go back to her dressmaker's shop is a woman who has something to be afraid of, and the shop was shut with a padlock and a German paper on the door, and three women stood on the pavement in the cold looking at the paper with their hands in their sleeves, and one of them was the woman of forty. Krystyna walked past on the far side of the street. The woman of forty turned her head and looked at her across thirty metres of the rue Saint-Honoré, and neither of them acknowledged anything, and both of them survived the war.

She learned what had happened in April, in Warsaw, from a line on a piece of paper worth more than its weight in gold: a list of prisoners transferred from Fresnes to a camp in Silesia in February, with a mark against one name in a German clerk's hand, *verstorben 9.II.44, Lungenentzündung*, and above it the name Odile Boin in the card index of a case that had been closed because the dressmaker had not given up a name — not one, in eleven days of it, at fifty-five, with a bad hip and a bad heart and three hundred clients' measurements in a book the SD already had in its hands, and six women in an attic behind a partition that they never found.

***

He was in the chapel on the rue Saint-Honoré on the third of February, at the Thursday hour, in the last row on the left, in a German overcoat with no armband and his hat in his hands and his eyes on the altar; and she knew he was there before she saw him, the way a person trained in a flat above a laundry knows that the thing in the room is not furniture.

She had twenty minutes before the priest came out.

She could go out through the north door into the courtyard and be in the rue Saint-Honoré in nine seconds. She could sit at the other end of the nave and do the Thursday, and let the priest watch a German captain leave four minutes after the woman he had been seen with at the Majestic had knelt at the rail. And she could — and this is where her professional self, which had eaten everything the girl from Mokotowska Street had ever been, laid a card on the table, in words, in Polish — she could consider that a man who knows her name and who stands in the church where the network comes on Thursdays can be removed, that he had come without a warrant and without a second man, and that the man in the twentieth arrondissement had been paid in advance and had never once failed.

Then she walked down the side aisle and sat in the second-to-last row with one row of empty chairs between them, and neither of them turned their heads, and the two of them looked at the altar together in the cold for four minutes without speaking; and afterwards she was never able to explain to herself, in any of the languages she had, why she had done that instead of the other thing.

"I am going to say four sentences," Phillip said, in Polish, in a voice pitched for the communion rail. "You may stop me whenever you like. First: the paper on the desk in the villa, the one about the estuary, is not yours and never was. It was written in a room on the avenue Foch by a man of about thirty-eight with a good coat, and it was written to be taken; and it has a second purpose that you have not thought of, which is that the day it reaches London, the house it came out of becomes the file. Second: I am telling you this because the man who wrote it is looking at your house and at mine, and because when he finds nothing in a Frenchwoman's flat in January he does not conclude that he was wrong. He concludes that he was right and early."

"Three," she said.

"Three: the case file goes east. The Admiral's people are being dismembered and the papers are being put on a train, and the man with the good coat has asked to go with them, because the east is where the Poles are, and because he has decided that the source is a Pole. He will be in Warsaw before the summer. His section is on a street there called aleja Szucha, and it has a building with a courtyard that you will be able to see from the pavement and will do well never to enter."

There was a silence of about eleven seconds, in which an old woman three rows in front of them got up, shuffled to the side altar, lit a candle, and shuffled back.

"And four?" she said.

"Four: I have a set of papers in the pocket of this coat. A complete set, with a photograph taken from your file at the Militärbefehlshaber's, and a route that begins in Lyon and ends in Barcelona, and a name that belongs to a woman who died in 1941 and would have been twenty-six. If you want them they are yours tonight, and there is a bicycle in the yard of a house in the fourteenth, and eleven days of my pay in the lining of the wallet." He looked at the altar the whole time he said it. "I have carried them for a month."

She sat in the cold and looked at the altar and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic took her nine seconds, and every line of it came out the same way, and the reason was not courage.

"Take out the papers," she said.

He took out the wallet and put it on the chair between them, and she unfolded the documents and looked at the photograph of herself in the file of the Militärbefehlshaber in France, which had been taken from the identity card issued to Marion Herschlag, Austrian national, in September 1940 — the photograph of a woman with her chin slightly up and her mouth slightly careful, which is a better portrait of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna than anything hanging in the flat on Mokotowska Street; and she folded the papers again and put them on the chair on his side and stood up.

"February," she said. "The Oberst is posted in February. My papers are already stamped and I am going with him, and you will hear, in the third week of the month, of an Oberst of the Militärbefehlshaber who has been given the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison; and after that you will not stand in a church near me again, because the next time you do it I will not have a reason not to do the other thing."

"Krystyna," he said.

"Not that," she said. "Once was enough for a lifetime, and you are going to have to spend yours somewhere else."

She walked down the aisle and out into the rue Saint-Honoré, and their hands did not touch at any point in the twenty minutes; and the wallet with the papers of a dead woman in it stayed on the chair in the second-to-last row, where an old woman found it on her way out and gave it to the priest, who put it in the drawer of the sacristy with the other things people leave behind — which was the last time in the whole of the war that Phillip Brandt was in a room with any part of her, and neither of them ever knew it.

***

Wiktor did not come on the tenth of February, and he did not come on the seventeenth, and on the nineteenth she walked the length of the rue Saint-Honoré past the chapel and past Odile's shut shop as far as the Place du Palais-Royal and back; and the flat above the laundry in the fifteenth arrondissement was shuttered with a card in the window advertising an apartment for a family of five, and the two marks on the doorframe — the one that meant safe, the one that meant come again — had both been scrubbed off with a brush, and the plaster was still raw where it had been done.

She sent nothing, and asked nothing, and went nowhere, and on the twenty-first she went to the chapel and did the Thursday, and the priest was there and said the mass and said nothing to her at all; and afterwards, in the sacristy, with his back to her and his hands in a basin, he said one sentence in Polish: "He is gone and the door is shut and I do not know anything more than that, and you are to be quiet, and there is no one in this city you can go to about it."

"There is one thing I want to leave with him."

"There is nothing in this house that can be left with him," the priest said, "and the man you are talking about has been in the hands of the SD since Tuesday, and you know what that means and so do I, and if you say one more word about it in this room I will put my coat on and go out of that door and never come back to you, because a priest who is useful is a priest who is not asked anything." He dried his hands. "Go and be quiet. That is the whole of the instruction, and it is not mine."

***

The house on the avenue Henri-Martin was packed in the last week of February by four soldiers and a French removal firm with a German licence, and it took them two days: the silver in straw in a crate, the paintings crated, the wine laid in cases and marked, the cellar sealed, a list in triplicate; and the whole of the requisition reversed and re-signed in the Colonel's own hand, in a good humour, with the concierge standing in the hall with her keys and her cat and her face.

He telephoned the Warsaw office three times on the Tuesday. She heard the second of the three from the corridor, through the door of the study, in which he was speaking German more slowly and more loudly than he spoke to her, in the way of a man talking on a bad line about a house: a villa, four rooms plus, a garden with three trees, on a street whose name she did not catch, near the Frascati park, with a piano in the drawing room that the previous owners had left and that was to stay, because the previous owners had been told it was to stay.

And on the Wednesday, at the table, with the first of the crates standing in the hall, he put his hand over hers and told her about the papers.

"I have had you stamped as an Austrian national," he said. "It was done in Berlin in three days, which is a month faster than anything else has ever been done for me, and it cost me a favour from a man in the Auswärtiges Amt who will remind me of it in 1946. You will be travelling as a civil employee of the military administration, which is the only category that exists. The east is not the west, my dear. In Paris a rumour is a piano in a drawing room; in Warsaw there will be people in offices who look at a woman's papers for the pleasure of it, and a Polish woman travelling in the service of the Reich is a thing they will have opinions about, and an Austrian is a thing they have no opinion about at all." He looked at her steadily. "I have made you a lie to live in. It is the only present I have ever given you that cost me something, so take it and do not argue."

"Thank you," she said, and meant the first syllable, and he did not notice, because he was a man who had just done an excellent thing and who was, at that moment, entirely happy.

***

They left on the morning of the twenty-eighth of February 1944 from the Gare de l'Est, in the snow, from a platform that had been cleared of the ordinary travellers by two gendarmes and a rope; and Emil Rutz stood at the door of the carriage with the cases in a row at his feet and the count of them in the notebook in his tunic pocket, and the Colonel came down the platform with his coat open and his gloves in his hand and his breath going up in the cold, and said the last things a German officer says on a platform: the numbers of the cases, the number of the carriage, the hour at which the car would meet them in Berlin, the name of the hotel.

She stood at the carriage door with her fur collar up and looked back down the platform at the crowd behind the rope: French faces, a woman with a basket, a boy on a man's shoulders, a porter with a barrow of sacks. And forty metres away, at the end of the platform by the bookstall, there was a man in a grey overcoat with his hat down and no luggage, standing absolutely still among the people who were moving, with a small notebook in his hand and a pencil; and he looked at her across the forty metres for three seconds, with his head slightly on one side, the way a man looks at a house he is thinking of buying, and then wrote something down, and closed the notebook, and walked away into the station without hurrying.

"Do you know him?" said von Hagen.

"No," she said.

"Good," he said, "because I do. That is Herr Kalbach of the Reichssicherheitshauptamt, and he has been trying to get his hands on my files for a year, and if he is on this platform then somebody in that building has made a mistake about me that I shall hear about in the spring." He put his glove on. "Get in, my dear."

The train went out of the Gare de l'Est at twenty past nine in the morning into the snow, and by eleven there was nothing but flat white fields and telegraph poles going backwards; and in the corridor behind her, at about the frontier, she heard a German soldier say to another, in the voice of a man announcing a fact about the weather: *in the east the war is different*; and she sat with her hands in her lap and her papers in her bag, stamped, Austrian, and looked out of the window at her own country coming towards the carriage at sixty kilometres an hour, and understood that she was on the other side of the wheels, and that on the other side of the wheels was where she came from.

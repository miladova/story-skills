# Chapter 9: The House of the Hound

She had two things for Warsaw and both of them came from Wiktor, and she had carried them across Europe in the only place she had: in her head, and in the order he had made her learn them, which was the address first and the sentence second, because a person who is being questioned will give up a sentence sooner than an address and it saves nothing.

The address was a stationer's shop, number 11 Śliska Street: paper, notebooks, ink, pencils, envelopes, and in the window a display of leather goods — briefcases, wallets, two attaché cases — which had been in that window since 1938 and which nobody had bought since 1939.

The sentence was: *I have brought a message from my aunt in Lwów.*

And the rules were four, and they were Wiktor's rules and they were made for a country he had left in 1939 and had been told about by men who had not been back either: go once, and if the woman in the shop does not answer correctly, do not go a second time; never go in the car; never go at the same hour twice; and after the first visit, expect to be watched for ten days by somebody who will not be a professional, because in Poland the amateurs are the ones who are still alive.

On the sixth of March, at four in the afternoon, in a temperature of minus six, she went into the shop on Śliska Street in an astrakhan hat that was wrong for the quarter and bought a notebook and a pencil and a box of nibs; and there was a woman behind the counter with grey hair pinned up and an apron, and there was nobody else in the shop at all.

"The nibs are for a good pen," the woman said. "Those are for a bad one."

"I have brought a message from my aunt in Lwów," said Krystyna.

The woman put the box of nibs on the counter and looked at her for three seconds — at the coat, at the hat, at the mouth — and said, in the same flat voice in which she had discussed the nibs: "I have no aunt in Lwów and neither has anyone in this shop, and the last man who said that to me was taken in the street in front of my window in November, and if you come here again I will have you reported to the police myself, and I will enjoy it." She pushed the notebook across the counter. "That will be one zloty eighty."

So: once. That was the rule, and the rule was the whole of the value, and Krystyna paid for the notebook, and said thank you, and went out into the snow and did not go back, and did not look at the window, and walked east along Śliska towards the ruins.

Now she had nothing at all: no address, no sentence, no contact, no handler, and a city that had four hundred thousand people in it who would each have liked to see her dead for the coat she was wearing.

She went home and did her arithmetic again, which is the only thing a person in that position can do, and which took her a day and a half, and which came out in the following form: that she was not invisible, that she had arrived in the German quarter in a German car, that she had been seen on the Nowy Świat at eleven in the morning looking at the posters and at the motor pool, and that a city in the fifth year of an occupation has more eyes per square metre than anywhere else on earth, and that some of those eyes belong to the sort of people who put a chalk anchor on a wall in the Świętokrzyska street.

So she became visible on purpose.

It is a piece of tradecraft that Wiktor had made her practise for a week in Paris in 1942 in a corridor in a government office with a stopwatch, and the theory of it is this: if you are going to be watched, choose the watcher, and the way to choose the watcher is to give the watchers a pattern so clean and so dull that only one sort of person will care about it — and then that person is yours.

For four days she did the same thing: the car to the office at nine; the car back at one; and then, in the afternoon, the same errand on foot with Rutz at four metres and waiting in the street, because Rutz was a soul of the old army and had been in the house since before either of them had thought about the east, and because a Furier who has been told to accompany a lady does not shadow her, he accompanies her with his hands behind his back.

In four afternoons she went to the same six places. The church on the Krakowskie Przedmieście where the nuns said the office at three. The second-hand bookshop on the Świętokrzyska where she bought two French novels and left them on the counter for an hour while she looked at the shelf behind. The window of the pharmacy at the corner of Szpitalna Street. The confectioner's where there was nothing to buy but there was a window with a marble sill and a chair. The passage on the Świętokrzyska, where she went in at one end and out at the other, and which has a courtyard in the middle with a well and a coal shed and a gate on to the next street. The bookshop on the Nowy Świat with the sign of the pelican over the door.

And at the end of the fourth afternoon, a boy of fourteen in a coat four sizes too big for him was standing in the passage on the Świętokrzyska kicking a rag football against the wall, and he said, without looking at her, in Polish, in the voice of a person reciting something he had been made to learn: "The pelican is shut on Thursday and the gentleman's coat is inside out."

Then he kicked the ball again, and it went over the fence into the yard of a house, and he climbed after it, and by the time she had walked the length of the passage there was nobody in the passage at all.

Inside out. The coat inside out.

The sixth place on her list was a second-hand clothes shop on the Śródmieście side of Marszałkowska, which had a rack of coats in the doorway and a mirror and a woman asleep behind the counter; and she went in on the afternoon of the tenth of March and bought nothing and looked at the mirror and understood the instruction, which was that a person cannot turn a coat inside out in the street, and that the place where a person turns a coat inside out is a courtyard, and that there was a courtyard with a coal shed in it in the passage on the Świętokrzyska.

So on the eleventh she went into the passage at four in the afternoon with Rutz four metres behind her, and turned into the courtyard as if she had mistaken it for a shortcut, and behind the coal shed, out of sight of the street for something between six and nine seconds, she took off the astrakhan coat and put it on again wrong side out, which is a thing that takes four seconds if a person has practised it in a corridor in Paris in 1942, and went out of the other gate into the next street with the fur inside and the lining outside, in which she was a woman in an old black cloth coat with a silk lining, and Rutz came after her with his hands behind his back and said, in the flat voice of a man who has decided not to see whatever it was: "The Fräulein is cold."

"The Fräulein is cold. Let us go home, Emil."

They went home. And the following morning, at nine, when the car came up the drive, there was a boy of about sixteen in a cloth cap standing at the gate, and he came to the window of the car and put a piece of paper in Rutz's hand and ran off, and Rutz gave her the paper and she unfolded it in the car and read it and put it in her glove, and it said, in pencil, in Polish, in a schoolboy's hand: *Thursday, the workshop on Śliska, the fourth courtyard, the cellar under the bench, seven in the evening. Come alone and come in the coat.*

***

The fourth courtyard on Śliska Street, on the sixteenth of March at seven in the evening, was the courtyard of a two-storey building that had once made agricultural machinery and now made handcarts, and it was dark, and there was a smell of snow and iron and coal, and the gate was open, and there was a light moving behind the third window on the ground floor: a flame, not a bulb.

The cellar under the workshop had a stone floor and a barrel-vaulted ceiling and whitewashed walls gone the colour of weak tea, and it was eight paces by four, and there was a table made of two doors on trestles, and on the table a candle in a jam jar with a paper collar, and beside the candle a pistol.

There were two people in the room.

The woman was about thirty, and she was sitting on the far side of the table with her back to the wall and her hands on the table in front of her, and she was wearing a good dark suit that was four years old and had been altered twice, and her hair was cut short and cut badly, and she had the face of a woman who has slept four hours a night for two years. She was, in fact, beautiful, in the way that a locked door is beautiful: the whole of the effect was in what it refused.

The man was standing behind her to the right, in the corner, in the dark, with his back to the wall and his arms folded, about forty-five, going grey and going heavy, in spectacles and a shabby overcoat. He did not sit down at any point in the next two hours, and he did not speak until the end.

"Sit down and put your hands on the table," the woman said.

Krystyna sat down and put her hands on the table.

"You are Stankiewicz," the woman said.

"I was."

"You are Stankiewicz, you are twenty-four years old, you were at school at the Sacred Heart until 1937 and at the flat on Mokotowska Street, and in September 1939 you went south to relations, and in June 1940 you went to France through a labour recruitment scheme and you have been in France since. In France you were the mistress of an Oberst of the Militärbefehlshaber in Paris, and you have come to Warsaw with him, and you live in a villa on the Frascati that was taken from the Zaleskis, and you have brought nothing with you that anybody here can use. That is what we have. If any of it is wrong, say so now, because I shall be writing it in a ledger in either case and I do not want an error in my own handwriting."

"One thing is wrong," said Krystyna. "I did not have one Oberst in Paris. I have one Oberst in Paris and now in Warsaw, and he is the same man, and I have been his mistress since June 1943."

The woman looked at her for a moment longer than she had before.

"Add that to the ledger," she said.

Then the questioning started, and it went on for two hours in the cold, and it was conducted in the manner of an enquiry, not an interrogation: the questions were put in order, one at a time, and each answer was written down by the man in the corner in a small hand in a notebook that he did not look at while he wrote.

Who trained her. Where and when, and by whom, and the name of the flat and the number of the weeks. When she had arrived in Paris, by which route, through which frontier, with whose money. What she had done in Paris in 1941, in 1942, in 1943. The trade she had for her cover, the dressmaker with the German licence, the fittings, the papers, the identity card's number; the name of the Austrian town she had never visited and the street of the school she had never attended, and she gave them, all of it, in order, twice, because they asked twice; and she noticed that the second asking was not to catch her out but to test the memory, which is a different and much more frightening thing, because the memory can be trained and the man in the corner knew it and was writing down whether she had been trained.

At half past eight the woman said, "Now the paper."

"What paper?"

"The paper, Stankiewicz. Every officer in this city has a desk, and every desk has paper on it, and the papers on that desk are the only reason you are sitting at this table instead of being sick into a bucket in the basement of the Szucha. Describe the last piece of paper you saw in that house, and do not describe it as a woman who has been in a house. Describe it as a typist."

So she described it as a typist: the departmental heading, the reference number, the layout, the routing block on the reverse side with four initials and a date stamp, the carbon. When she had done it, the woman on the other side of the table nodded once, slightly, in the way in which a professional acknowledges that a colleague's hands are clean, and said, "Whose initials?"

"Mine," said Krystyna. "The initials on the routing block are his, and mine are under them, because I file the copies. In that office I am not a woman who is kept. I am a woman who is employed, and I am paid, and the pay is in the salary book of the administration, and there are eleven people in that building who have seen my hands on a filing tray."

"Good," the woman said, and then she asked the question, and she asked it the way she had asked all the others, without any change in her voice at all. "Did you enjoy it?"

The man in the corner stopped writing.

"Which part?"

"The bed, Stankiewicz. Which part do you think I mean?"

"No," said Krystyna.

"Say that again and look at me."

"No."

"Twice is twice," said the woman, and closed the notebook that was on the table in front of her, which had nothing written in it, and which had been there to be closed at the right moment. "Now listen, because there are no more questions and everything after this is terms." She put her hands flat on the table. "My name is Iga Zaleska. It is not my name; it was on the brass plate of a house on the Frascati until 1940, when the family was taken out of it and sent to the other side of the wall, and I took the name afterwards because names of the dead are the safest paper in this city, and because it means that every time I walk down that street I have to look at my own front door with an eagle over it. There is a man in this room whose name you will not be given, and he is a lawyer by profession and he has been a soldier since 1939, and he has decided that you are worth this meeting, and against my advice."

"You are recorded as dead," said the man in the corner.

It was the first time he had spoken, and the voice was a light, dry, precise voice, and it came out of the dark of the corner with no emotion in it whatever, and it was not a statement about her but about the file.

"In June 1940 your family informed the parish, the police and the building that you had died of typhus in a hospital in Toulouse. There is a mass said for you once a year in the church on the Mokotowska Street. Your sister wears your mother's ring on a string round her neck and your father has your photograph in a Latin dictionary. That was done by your father in the first month of the occupation and it has been maintained for four years, and if it is discovered that you are alive — by anyone, for any reason, including your family, including by your own hand — the story will not survive three days." He took his spectacles off and polished them on his sleeve and put them back on. "Which is why I am telling you now, before you are given a single task. You will not go to Mokotowska Street. You will not speak to your sister. You will not write. If you are seen within a hundred metres of that building by one of our people, you will be taken off the street and held, and you will be held for as long as this war lasts. That would be very bad for you, and it would be very bad for your father, who is ill, and who is being kept alive by three people, one of whom is seventeen."

The candle on the table in the jam jar leaned sideways in the draft from the door and stood up again.

"Those are the terms," Iga Zaleska said. "Paper: nothing in writing, ever; nothing you are given is to be written down, and nothing you take is to be carried on your body unless you are prepared to eat it, which you cannot do, so you will memorise and carry it in your head and deliver it in the same form. Contact: with me only, by arrangement, on days and at places that I shall change every time; if I do not come, you leave, and you do not come back to that place for a month. Family: as the Major has said; the subject is closed and will not be raised again in front of me. Delivery: you will bring me something within ten days that I cannot buy, and after that we will talk about the eleventh day." She pushed the pistol half a hand's width along the table and back again, which seemed to be a habit. "If you fail us, or if we believe you have failed us, the arrangement ends at a place and an hour of our choosing, and it ends with a small notice in the *Nowy Kurier* under the executions, and no explanation to anybody, including your family, who will go on believing that you died of typhus in Toulouse. I am telling you this in words because the rules are the rules and you are a professional and you will want them said out loud."

"They are said."

"Then here is the shopping list," Iga said, and now something in the room changed, because this was not the security and this was the work, and the work is what she had come for and it was coming out of the woman's mouth one item at a time, in the manner of a person reading a bill. "One: the staff list of the garrison command, with the names and the ranks of the officers and the departments they came from, because there is a new establishment in this city as of the first of March and nobody outside it knows what is in it. Two: the garrison's engineer records — the bridges, the crossings, the culverts, the state of the road and rail bridges over the Vistula, and who is responsible for them and what is loaded under them, because there are charges under two of them and we did not put them there. Three: the ration and fuel allocations for the civil population of this city as issued in February and March, from which a trained eye can read how many people the Germans intend to feed in the summer, and therefore what they intend to do with this city. Four, and this is the last, and it is not a document." She leaned back against the wall. "I want one sentence from your Colonel's mouth. I want to know whether the Germans in Warsaw intend to hold this city when the Russians come, or to level it and leave. He will know. Every officer of his rank in this city has been told something, and it is the thing they talk about at dinner, and it is the only thing in the whole of this business that cannot be read off a piece of paper."

"He will know," Krystyna said.

"Yes," said the man in the corner. "That is what we are betting on. Forty people are betting their lives on it in this room, and I include you, and I have written the date in the notebook." He put the notebook in his inside pocket and stood away from the wall and looked at her for the first time that evening, and his face in the candlelight was tired rather than hard, and he said: "You have brought me gold, and it was carried through a German's house on the hips of a Polish girl. I am not a priest and I do not care. Bring more."

Then he went up the cellar steps and out into the courtyard, and the door shut, and Iga Zaleska sat looking at the candle for a moment, and said, without any change of tone at all:

"You will need money and you will not be given any. You will need papers and you will not be given any. You will have one thing that is worth more than both, which is that German officers have been trained for two hundred years to believe that a woman in a bedroom is not a filing cabinet, and all of them believe it, and you are going to make them keep believing it, and there is nobody in this country who does not know that." She stood up and put the pistol into the inside pocket of her jacket with one movement, the muzzle down and the hand flat. "The password for the next arrangement is not a password; it is a piece of paper, and the piece of paper is this." She took from her sleeve a cigarette packet, drew out of it a cigarette already rolled, and snapped it in half, and gave Krystyna one half. "When you are given the other half, you will know that I have sent for you. Keep it dry, keep it out of the house, and do not smoke."

***

Walking home, in the snow, with the coat the right way out again and Rutz forty metres behind her at the end of Śliska waiting with the car, she came past two women in the gateway of a courtyard on the corner of the Żelazna, and one of them was holding a hand-barrow with a pianino on it wrapped in sacking and tied with rope, and the two of them were arguing about whether the legs would come off or whether the whole thing had to be carried in two pieces up to the second floor.

"—because it is not my piano and I am not paying the joiner a single zloty, and the woman can carry it up her own stairs if she wants it on the second floor—"

"Which old man?"

"On Mokotowska, in the courtyard with the pump — the Stankiewicz, the old man who was at the school, the one who never comes out. Two rooms let to a widow and the rest of them in the back, and the girl selling whatever is not nailed down: my mistress bought a clock off her in January, and a coat, and now she wants the name of a joiner, or a horse, or anything that is not cabbage." The woman took a breath and adjusted the rope. "The old man is ill — he coughs all winter, my mistress hears it through the wall — and there is only the girl and the son, and the son is never there. And the other one died in France in the war, the older girl, typhus in a hospital in Toulouse, they had it in the family prayers for four years, and the mother died in '41. If the war ended tomorrow they would still be eating cabbage."
 
"Typhus in Toulouse."

"Typhus in Toulouse, and a fine thing it is to die of, in France, in a hospital. My sister's girl only got to Katowice."

Krystyna stood in the snow on the corner of the Żelazna and listened to the whole of it with her hands in her pockets like any other woman waiting for a tram, and nobody in the world looked at her, and she thought the following two things in the following order.

First: that the family had done it properly, and that a story which is kept up by a family for four years, in a mass once a year, in front of the neighbours, in front of the maid, is a piece of security work of a standard higher than anything the network in Paris had ever asked of her; that her father had understood, in the first month, that a living daughter in the German service would kill the family and that a dead daughter in France would keep them alive, and had done it himself, with her mother, in the dark, in the autumn of 1940, and never told her, because there was no way to tell her.

Second: that Hanna was selling the pianino to buy coal.

***

She went to Mokotowska Street that night.

It was a quarter past eleven, and she had gone to bed with the light out and lain for forty minutes until the house was down: the Colonel asleep at ten to eleven, in his own room, with the door open as he liked it; Janina asleep over the kitchen; the sentry on the gravel at the gate turning every eleven minutes, which he had done in Paris and which he did here because the army is the army.

She went out through the pantry door into the garden, which had not been bolted because nothing in that house was bolted, and across the garden, and over the wall in the corner by the shed, in her shoes, in the snow, with her coat over her arm; and she walked nine hundred metres through the back streets of a city under curfew, in the dark, past one German patrol car standing at a corner with its lights off and one Polish man walking the other way who crossed the street to avoid her, which is what a man does in a curfew city when he sees a woman walking alone and does not want to know why.

The courtyard on Mokotowska Street was the courtyard of her childhood: the gate with the iron bar at the bottom that a person had to lift, the yard with the pump and the four dustbins and the long shed where the caretaker had kept his coal, and the window of the flat on the first floor at the front, over the courtyard, which is the window she was not permitted to stand in and had stood in on the night of the third of September 1939 with her hands flat on the sill and a boy in the rain on the pavement below.

There was a light on in the corridor. It came through the transom over the door of the flat and it made a rectangle on the wall of the courtyard, and it moved once, slowly, from one side to the other, which is what a lamp does when a person carries it from one room to the next; and then the light in the corridor went out and another light came on, further back, in what had been her parents' room, and stayed on.

She stood in the courtyard for eleven minutes with the snow coming down through the light from the window and did not go up.

She could have gone up. That is the thing to say about that night, and it has to be said plainly, because a person's whole life is sometimes made of eleven minutes in a courtyard: there was no lock on the gate that she could not lift, there were sixty-one steps in that stairwell and she had counted them all her life, the door of the flat would be opened by her sister, who was seventeen, who had been sold to the neighbours as an orphan of a typhus ward in Toulouse, and who was at that moment eleven metres above her, awake, because a girl of seventeen who is selling a pianino to buy coal does not sleep at midnight in a flat where her father is coughing.

And she did not go up, and the reason was not the terms, and the reason was not the Major, and the reason was not even the patrol car at the corner; and she knew what the reason was, in the courtyard, at eleven minutes past midnight, in the snow, in her own city, and it was this: that if she went up those stairs she would not be able to come down them again, and that on the other side of the door was the last thing in the world she had that was not already spent.

She took the cigarette half out of the lining of her glove and looked at it, and put it back.

She walked back through the gate, lifting the bar with her foot as she had done since she was seven years old, and was in the street with her head down against the snow when a girl came round the corner of the Wilcza running, in a coat over a nightgown, in boots two sizes too big with no laces in them, with a scarf round her chin, breathing hard, seventeen years old, with her mother's ring on a string round her neck under the nightgown, and stopped three metres away in the middle of the pavement with the snow coming down between them and did not move at all.

Krystyna did not move either.

The girl's face was her own face before the war had finished with it: the same grey eyes, the same high colour in the cold, the bones their father's. She looked at Krystyna for four seconds — at the coat, at the shoes, at the hands, at the mouth — and then her mouth opened, and she said one word, out loud, in the street, in the snow, in the fifth year of the war, in a city where a German patrol could come round that corner in a car at any second of any night.

"Krysia?"

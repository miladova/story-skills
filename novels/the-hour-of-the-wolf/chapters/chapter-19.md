# Chapter 19: W-Hour

The German quarter at four o'clock in the afternoon of the first of August 1944 was the most peaceful place in Warsaw.

The sentries were at their posts on the corners of the Ujazdowskie and the Szucha with their rifles slung and their caps on the backs of their heads in the heat; the staff cars stood along the kerbs with the drivers asleep at the wheels; a girl from the office of the district administration came out of the gate of a villa with a parcel and a bicycle; two officers in shirtsleeves walked along the pavement on the Bracka side talking about a permit for a piano; and in the cafés of the Nowy Świat the waiters were setting the tables for the afternoon with the plates and the little vases of flowers, because the administration of the German Reich in Warsaw in August 1944 had decided - by an order of the military commander, in writing, in the month of July - that the cafés were to stay open, the trams were to keep running, the cinemas were to open at eleven, and the city was to be treated, at the level of the administrative paperwork, as a place where nothing whatever was going to happen.

She had walked that quarter for five months, twice a week, in a fur coat in the winter and in a grey coat in the summer, past the sentries, past the notices, past the café and the dress shop, and had known, in the whole of that time, about a hundred and forty people by sight; and the sentry at the corner of the Bracka and the Nowy Świat, a man of about twenty-five with a greatcoat and a schmiss on the cheek from some other war or some other man, had said "Gnädige Frau" to her eleven times since March.

She said, "Good afternoon," to him on the first of August at eleven minutes past four.

He said, "Good afternoon, gnädige Frau," and shifted his rifle on his shoulder, and looked up at the sky above the roofs, and then at the pavement, in the ordinary way of a man on a hot afternoon with two hours of his shift left.

She went through the gate of the villa on the Frascati Street at twenty minutes past four, and the sentry at the gate came to attention and opened it for her, and she went up the gravel drive between the beds of flowers that Janina had kept in order all summer, and into the house, and up the stairs.

***

At ten minutes to five she sat in the window of her room on the first floor in a light dress with a book in her lap, and looked out over the roofs of the German quarter towards the west and the north.

The room was as it had been: the bed, the wardrobe with the madame's name of a family that had been taken out of it in 1940, the dressing table with the row of objects, the picture on the wall of a country road under snow with a windmill in it. On the chair by the door there was a coat — the grey coat, the summer one, with the lining that had been opened under the left arm by a woman on the Ogrodowa and closed again with eleven stitches in the same hour in July, and inside that lining there were, at ten minutes to five on the first of August 1944, in three flat packets wrapped in oiled paper, sixty-one pages of handwriting in cipher made that morning between the hours of six and eight in the bathroom of that house with the tap running; and under the coat on the chair there was a shopping basket, and in the basket there was a hat, and in the hat there was nothing at all.

Below her, on the ground floor, the Colonel was in the drawing room with three officers who had come at four and had stayed: she could hear the voice of the chief of the section of the district administration, who had a laugh with a squeak in it, and the clink of glass and the small flat noises of a room in which four men are drinking the wine of somebody else's cellar on a hot afternoon; and in the yard Rutz was polishing the bonnet of the Mercedes with a rag and a tin of polish, in his tunic, in the sun, in the way of a man who has been doing the same thing for eleven years and has never once been asked what he thought of any of it.

She had rehearsed the hour in the way in which she had been taught to rehearse everything: she had the two routes out of the house and the four routes out of the quarter in her head, the three places in which a woman with papers and a basket could stand for eleven minutes without being looked at twice, the hour and the minute at which the sentry at the gate was relieved, the fact that the kitchen door gave onto the garden wall and the garden wall onto the yard of the building on the Bracka; and she had the thing that is the whole of the art, which is the order in which a person decides to do the wrong thing.

At ten minutes to five the telephone in the study rang, and stopped, and then, eleven seconds later, rang again; and she heard the Colonel answer it in the hall and say something short; and then the officers in the drawing room stopped laughing and began to talk all at once, in the way of men who have been given a piece of news, and she heard the word *Ostbahn* and the word *bridge*, and she sat in the window with the book open in her lap and looked out over the quarter towards the west and the north, where a hundred thousand people were waiting.

At four minutes to five she heard it.

It was a rattle of small arms, flat and fast, from the north-west, from the direction of the Wola, half a kilometre away, and it lasted about nine seconds; and in a house in the German quarter on a hot afternoon it could have been a lorry with a bad engine, and it was followed by nothing, and the men in the drawing room went on talking, and one of them laughed the laugh with the squeak in it, and Rutz went on polishing the bonnet of the Mercedes.

At two minutes to five she heard it again: two bursts, one behind the other, from the same direction and further off, and then, after a pause of about a second and a half, a machine gun coming in from a different part of the city altogether, in the south, on the Mokotów side, in the long, level, continuous way that no backfiring lorry in the world has ever sounded like.

And the country road with the windmill hung on the wall of the room, and the clock on the dressing table said two minutes to five, and the sound of the machine gun came in over the roofs and through the open window, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna sat absolutely still in the chair with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the street below and let herself have eleven seconds — she had promised herself eleven, and she took them — of the only feeling she had permitted herself in four years, which was not joy and was not relief and was not triumph, but the enormous, terrible, physical loosening of a body that has been held in one position since September 1939 and has been told, at last, in a language that cannot be lied about, that the position is over.

**Then** the gate of the villa opened, and Rutz came up the drive at a run in his tunic with the polish rag still in his hand.

***

She went down the stairs.

The hall had filled with men: the three officers out of the drawing room with their tunics open and their caps in their hands, the two batmen, the sentry from the gate with his rifle up and his mouth open, the clerk of the section who lived in the annexe with his spectacles in his hand; and the Colonel was in the middle of them with the telephone receiver in his left hand and a map of the city already open on the hall table under his right, saying, in the flat, level voice of a man who has commanded a supply column on a road under fire and who is at home: "—no, I do not want the district command, I want Ia, and if Ia is not at the telephone then the chief of staff is to be told that the Ujazdowskie is closed at the Szucha and that we have firing in four districts, and that there are men on the roof of the Prudential building."

And he saw her on the stairs, and he said: "Ah. The lady of the house." And then he said the sentence that the rest of the day has to be understood through, because it is the whole of the German officer in that hour, and he said it lightly, and with a certain pride, and entirely without any idea whatsoever of the thing that was walking up the stairs towards him out of the south:

"My dear, you are going to see something now. Get your camera, if you have got one, and do not go out of the gate; this will be finished by tomorrow noon, and I want you in this house with the doors locked and the blinds down until I come back, and I want the cellar stocked with water — Rutz, water, eleven cans, and the lamps, and the small radio out of the study, and if anybody asks for the Fräulein in the next four hours, nobody is to know anything."

"Jawohl, Herr Oberst."

She stood on the fourth step with her hand on the rail and looked down at the lot of them in the hall of the requisitioned villa with the day of the city's war beginning outside the window, and she took the last decision of that life without any dramatics at all, which is the way large decisions are actually taken, and the decision was: not tonight, and not the cellar, and not the small radio.

"Rutz," she said, from the stairs, in the ordinary voice of a woman who has remembered something, "my coat. The grey one, from the cupboard under the stairs — and the basket; and take the coat and the basket out to the gate, and give them to the sentry at the gate, and tell him I am walking to the chemist on the Bracka before the shops shut, because with all of this" — and she gestured at the map and the men and the telephone — "the Oberst is going to want the powders before tonight, and I shall not be ten minutes."

The Colonel did not even look up from the map.

"Ten minutes," he said, "and then this house is shut."

"Ten minutes," she said, and went up the stairs and along the corridor and into her room and shut the door, and put on the grey coat, and took the shopping basket with the hat in it, and went down the back stairs through the pantry and out through the kitchen and across the garden and over the wall at the end into the yard of the building on the Bracka; and the coat she was wearing, with its three flat packets of sixty-one pages of cipher in the lining under the left arm, was the coat in which she had walked into the house in March, in the snow, at half past eight at night, four streets from the river, with her hand on the banister rail.

***

The city of the first hour was not a battle. It was a hundred separate courtyards and a few of them had men in them.

She came through the yard on the Bracka and out into the street, and the street was full of people standing still and looking up: shopkeepers in their doorways with the aprons on and their hands on the jambs looking up at the roofs, a woman with a child on her arm looking up, four boys on the pavement with their faces raised like swimmers, an old man with a stick and a straw hat, standing in the middle of the pavement with his mouth open in an expression not of fear and not of joy but of a complete, hopeless, entirely Polish attention.

The firing was in the north-west, three streets off, and it was not a battle line, it was a series of rooms: a courtyard, a staircase, a corner of a street, a school building, a tram; and she went down the Bracka towards the centre of the city in the last light of an August afternoon among about four hundred people who were doing the same thing, which was walking; and in the middle of it, at the corner of the Chmielna, a barricade was being built out of an overturned tram and the cobblestones that two hundred people were pulling up with their hands.

There were women in the middle of it, and boys of fourteen, and a man in a white collar with a briefcase standing on the tram giving orders; six men lifting a piano out of the door of the building on the corner and putting it down on the paving stones of the barricade with a crash that was heard four streets away; a cart unhitched and its shafts turned up into the air; sandbags; a mirror out of a hall; a horse trough; and the cobblestones coming up out of the street in the hands of four hundred people, and a woman of sixty on her knees in the gutter working at one with a hammer.

And when she was level with the tram, the firing from the direction of the Śliska came over the roofs in a burst that was very close and very high, and the whole of the street stopped.

Four hundred people, in the middle of the last hour of the ordinary life of that city, stood still in the middle of the road with the cobblestones in their hands and their heads up, and the burst went over them and away into the roof of a house on the other side, and a woman near Krystyna said something in Polish about the tiles; and then a man laughed, and two boys began throwing the cobblestones as if it were a game, and the work went on, faster than before.

She had been in that city for five months as a German lady. Nobody had looked at her in the street except to look away; she had been invisible in the exact way that a woman in a good coat in an occupied city is invisible, which is that people avoid her eyes the way they avoid the eyes of a dog; and it was in this hour, in the middle of four hundred people who were pulling up the street with their hands, that this stopped: a woman with her sleeves rolled up and a brick in each hand turned round in the crowd and looked at her, one woman of about fifty, in an apron, with sweat on her face, and said, in Polish, without any hostility at all, in the way one worker speaks to another, "You are standing in the way, lady. Hold this," and shoved a cobblestone into her hands.

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, in the grey coat, with the papers of the German counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison in the lining under her left arm, took the cobblestone out of the hands of a Polish woman of fifty in the street in the first hour of the Rising, and put it where she was told to put it, on the top of the tram, next to a woman's mirror and a length of iron pipe, and went on down the street towards the Śliska with the dust on her hands and the whole of her body ringing.

***

The assembly point was a building on the Śliska Street with a courtyard, and the courtyard was full of women.

There were about forty of them and they were doing the work that there is to do in the first hour of such a thing: a table with a map and a candle and a list; a woman of about sixty-five in an apron sitting on a chair in the middle of it all doing the stitches on a bag of dressings and not looking up once in an hour; girls of seventeen carrying the armbands out of the office of a building in a cardboard box and putting them on the tables; a woman with a stencil on the ground doing the printing with a roller in the middle of a courtyard where men were carrying a crate of bottles; and in the middle of it all, at the table, in a shirt with the sleeves rolled and a cigarette in the corner of his mouth and a wall of paper in front of him, Antoni Krupa.

He looked up, and saw her, and stood up.

"The pages," he said.

She put the basket on the table and took the hat out of it, and there was nothing in the hat, and then she turned the coat inside out on the table in the middle of the courtyard of a building on the Śliska Street in the light of a candle and a hurricane lamp in August 1944, and took a penknife off the table, and opened the seam under the left arm with the point, and took out of the lining, in three flat packets wrapped in oiled paper, sixty-one pages of carriage of the German garrison's plan of defence of the city of Warsaw; and the quartermaster took them with both hands and looked at the first page for about four seconds, and then sat down at the table with the candle and the map and put his two glasses on his nose, and did not say anything at all for eleven minutes.

Krupa had come round the table; and he looked at her for a while in the way of a man who has spent the whole of a war in a cellar with a ledger; and then he reached out and took her hand, which he had never done, and held it for about two seconds, which was the whole of what he had to give, and said, when he let go: "You will want to be somewhere tonight. Go with Basia."

***

The girl who tied her armband on was seventeen years old and her name was Basia Marciniak, and she tied it with the hands of a person who has tied eleven of them that evening, and she was talking the entire time, in a way that Krystyna had not heard since the courtyard on the Mokotowska Street in March.

"—and it goes above the elbow, no, here, like this, and the pin is on the inside so that it does not come out, and you do not put your hands in your pockets, and you do not go into the courtyard of the printing house because there is a well there that the boys from the sixteenth are using as a latrine, and if there is shooting in the street you walk with your back to the wall and you count the windows so you know when you have got to the corner; and where did you come from? Because you say *towards* the way the people from the Praga say it, and nobody says it like that here, well, my grandmother did, she was from the Praga, and where have you come from, honestly? Because you have been in a fight, you have got dust in your hair and your coat smells of cigarettes of a kind that nobody in this city has smoked since 1940, and you have not got a rifle and you have got a face like a woman who has been to a funeral and come out improved by it."

"From the Mokotowska," said Krystyna.

"Which side?"

"With my father."

"Then you are lucky," said Basia, very fast, with the pin in her mouth, "because both of mine are in the ground in the Ochota since 1942 and my aunt is in the cellar with the radio and my brother is with the group on the Marszałkowska and if I am told one more time by a man of twenty that I am to stay in the courtyard with the dressings I shall put a bullet in somebody myself, and that is not a joke, and you look like a person who has heard a joke before. There." She stepped back. "Basia. What are you called?"

"Krystyna."

"Krysia?" said the girl, and looked at her for a second longer than a girl of seventeen looks at a stranger, and then said, "Right. You are in the fourth room on the top floor with three girls from the Żoliborz and me, and you will get the floor by the wall because you have been somewhere today and the rest of them have been in the courtyard since six a.m., and you will sleep, and in the morning they will find you something to do, because with that handwriting they are going to want you in the office, and God help you, that is worse than the courtyard."

***

In the first hour of the sixty-three days, the city of Warsaw rose.

It was not at the level of a general staff and it was not one act; it was two hundred courtyards finishing what they had begun, and by six o'clock the barricades on the Marszałkowska, the Nowy Świat, the Hoża, the Chmielna and eleven streets on the other side of the river were finished or half finished; by seven the smoke was coming up in two columns over the Wola and the flags were coming out of the windows; at eight the Prudential building — the tall skeleton of the pre-war city, the pride of the insurance companies of 1933 with the radio mast on the roof, eleven stories of steel and concrete above the Świętokrzyska — was in the hands of a company of the Home Army's boys from the Old Town, who had gone up through the staircase with one pistol between five of them and had taken a German garrison of a hundred and eleven men out of the top three floors with thirty rifles and two of their own dead, and who by nine o'clock had hoisted the flag of the Polish Republic on the mast above the roof of it, where it was visible from the whole of the centre of the city; and the people in the streets on that evening stood on the pavements and looked up at that flag on that building in the last light, and the whole of it was out of a book of 1918 or a picture in a school, and every one of them was crying, and not one of them had any idea at all of what was going to be done to them.

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the courtyard on the Śliska Street at nine in the evening with a rifle in her hands, which had been put there by a boy of nineteen who had gone off to get himself a better one, and looked up over the roofs at the smoke and the flag, and did the arithmetic of the sixty days and the million people of that city; and she had, at nine in the evening of the first of August 1944, three things in her pocket: a card with a pseudonym on it, an armband with an anchor on it, and, at the bottom of the grey coat's pocket, where it had been since the second of December 1940, in a leather purse with a broken clasp, a silver compact with a false floor and one tablet in it, which she had last looked at in a bathroom on the avenue Henri-Martin in March, and which she carried the way a person carries a ticket.

***

The Colonel stayed in the study until two in the morning.

The telephone line to the garrison command went dead at half past six, and he used the set in the annexe until that went dead too; and by nine he was working entirely from the map of the city on the hall table and from two officers who had come in on foot from the Ujazdowskie with their tunics off and their pistols drawn; and at midnight he sat down at the desk in the study in the dark with the blackout down and looked at the wall in front of him, in the place where the lithograph of the Lilpop works was hanging, for about eleven minutes.

Rutz came in at a quarter past midnight with a glass of tea. He stood by the door with his cap in his hand and said, in German: "Herr Oberst, the lady has not come back."

And von Hagen, at the desk, with his hands flat on the blotter, said: "Where did she say she was going?"

"To the chemist on the Bracka, Herr Oberst, and then you said ten minutes, and I gave her the coat and the basket at the gate at eleven minutes to five; and the sentry at the gate saw her go down the gravel to the street, and she turned to the right towards the Bracka, and I did not watch her go because there was firing from the Szucha side at that moment and the sentry at the gate was called into the hall, and it was four or five minutes before we thought about it at all."

"Then she is on the other side of it, or she is in the cellar of a house on the Bracka with the whole of the quarter's population," said von Hagen, "or she has been shot in the street, in which case we shall hear about it; and in the morning the order is to be given to every post in this quarter that I want the lady of this house found and brought back, and if she is found by anybody who does not know that order, Rutz, they will take her for a Polish woman in a good coat and put her against a wall; so you will draw a paper tonight, and I shall sign it, and it will say that the woman is an employee of the administration of German nationality and is to be conducted to the Frascati Street at the expense of the Reich, and it will have her description in it."

"Jawohl, Herr Oberst."

"Her description," said von Hagen, and looked at the blackout curtain; and then he said, in the voice that the whole of the villa, and the whole of the counter-intelligence section of the Warsaw garrison, and the whole of a Frenchwoman in the rue Royale in January 1944 would have recognised in one syllable, which was the voice of a man giving an instruction to a subordinate about a matter of paper:

"Marion Herschlag, Austrian, twenty-four years of age, height one metre sixty-eight; grey coat, dark hat, mole on the left side of the neck. Written in triplicate. And Rutz — send a man to the Szucha in the morning, from me, in writing, and ask the office of Sturmbannführer Kalbach for the loan of the Fräulein file for twenty-four hours, together with everything that office has collected on the matter since the fourteenth of January."

He stood up and went to the window and put two fingers against the blackout curtain and looked down into the blacked-out garden.

"Because I want to know," said Wolfram von Hagen, "who it is that has been living in my house."

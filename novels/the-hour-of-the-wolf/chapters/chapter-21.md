# Chapter 21: The Garden of Ash

The intelligence section of the Home Army's Old Town sector was in the back rooms of a house on the Długa Street, in a flat on the second floor whose windows had gone in the first week and were hung with a blanket, and it had in it: a table, four chairs, a chest of drawers used as an archive, a candle in a bottle, a lamp with a cloth over it, eleven files, three hundred sheets of paper, two typewriters of which one worked, and the whole of the written intelligence of a district of a city under siege.

There were nine people in that room at the end of August: a captain of about forty with a wound in the left hand and the entire memory of the sector's German order of battle in his head; a woman of thirty who had been a clerk in a bank and who did the cipher; two girls of eighteen who wrote a fair hand and did the fair copies; a scout of fifteen who ran; a sergeant of the blue police of the pre-war city who had come over on the first day with his pistol and his knowledge of every German post between the Długa and the river; a priest of the district who came in twice a day to sit with the wounded and once a day to sit with the papers, because a priest can carry a satchel anywhere; a woman of fifty who cooked for the whole house out of what there was and who never once went down to the cellar during a bombardment, on the stated grounds that she had been through 1920 and that the shell that had her name on it would find her in a cellar as easily as a kitchen; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, who read German.

That was her function in the Rising, and it was the last form of the trade that had taken her through Paris and Warsaw, and it was the strangest of them all, because the work was honest.

She translated. She translated the German newspapers, the German orders, the German maps taken off dead officers, the German letters taken off prisoners, the German labels on the medical stores and the German signs on the boxes of the ammunition captured in the first week; she translated the tank recognition tables and the insignia of the units engaged in the Wola and the insignia of the units on the bridges; she translated the sentences of the German military courts that had been found in the safe of a police station on the Długa, and the ledgers of the German garrison's canteen, and a hundred and eleven pages of the correspondence of the office of the waterworks of the city of Warsaw, of which the last page in the file was the one on which the German director of the waterworks wrote to the garrison command on the first of August 1944, at ten past five in the evening, the following sentence: *the working of the pumps continues; the men have gone home; there is firing in the streets; I am going home myself and I will come in the morning.*

She was good at it; nobody in that building could check her work; and she was, in the economy of a besieged district, worth about a company, and the whole of the section knew it, and the whole of the section also knew that it did not know where she came from.

There were four people in the Old Town who knew what she had done before the first of August 1944, and they were the woman with the ledger, the man with the spectacles, her brother, and a priest in the Mokotowska Street who had told her that he would put his coat on and go out of the door if she said one more word to him about a dead man in February; and there were, in the intelligence section of the Old Town, about eleven people who had worked out, each in their own way and without saying anything to anybody, that the woman who read German had been somewhere in German company.

On the eleventh of August she heard the sentence, and it was not said to her.

She was at the second table with the lamp and the cloth, translating a captured order of the day of a police battalion into Polish for the captain with the wounded hand, and there were five people in the room, and one of them was a young lieutenant of about twenty-two of a sabotage section who had come in to draw a map, and who said, at the chest of drawers, waiting for the paper: "There is a truck of ours with two women in it on the Świętojerska; they came over with the ambulances from the Śródmieście; both of them are dressed like ladies, one of them has got shoes on her that cost four thousand zlotys, and I have been told they came out of German houses. It is a fine thing that we are fighting the whole of the German army so that a couple of German officers' whores can come and drink our tea—"

And Iga Zaleska, who was standing at the window with a list in her hand, said, without turning round, in her flat voice: "The woman is mine."

There was a silence in that room of about four seconds, in which the typewriter stopped and the girl of eighteen at the fair copies looked up and the young lieutenant turned round with his mouth still open, and then Iga went on with her list, and the lieutenant took his map and went out of the room, and nobody in that room ever said anything of the kind in front of her again, and nobody ever explained to the lieutenant what the four words had meant, and he died on the twentieth of August at his barricade on the Miodowa without finding out.

***

The warmth in that chapter is not in the rooms and it is not in the fighting. It is in a cellar on the night of the fourth of August, in the Śródmieście, in the first week, and it is a conversation with a girl of seventeen who was killed on the eighth.

Her name was Basia Marciniak and she had tied the armband of eleven couriers in one evening, and by the fourth of August she had the whole of the intelligence section's dispatch route in her head, which is not a thing that is taught and is a thing that cannot be faked; and on the night of the fourth, during a bombardment that went on from nine till midnight and took the roof off the house next door, the two of them sat on the floor of a cellar with two other girls and the whole population of the building, and Basia stole one of Krystyna's cigarettes out of her coat, the way she had stolen three of them in the first week, and smoked it with the concentration of a girl of seventeen who has never been given one in her life except by soldiers, and asked the question.

"What is it like?"

"What is what like?"

"Sleeping in their beds." She had her knees up and her arms round them and her chin on her knees and her eyes on the candle, and she asked it in the flat, sweet, curious, entirely merciless way of a person of seventeen who has decided that the truth is a thing that grown-ups owe her. "I am not asking you for my mother, and there is nobody in this cellar who is listening except the two of them and they are asleep, and I have been carrying your messages for eleven days now, so I think I have bought the answer, and you have been in rooms that nobody in this city has been in, and I want to know, and if you say you do not want to talk about it I shall call you a coward and I shall be right."

There were, Krystyna found, three answers and none of them was true, and she gave all three in the course of about four minutes, and the girl took them all apart.

The first answer was the professional one: that a bed is a piece of furniture and a body is a machine and a person can be in a room and not be in it, in the way that a woman in a hospital can lie in a bed for eleven weeks; and Basia said, "That is the answer for a report."

The second answer was the arithmetic: that every hour in that room had bought paper, and paper had bought lives, and that she had been running the account for four years and had never once found the sum wrong; and Basia said, "That is the answer for your officer with the spectacles, and it is a good answer, and my aunt says it about her son who is in the police."

And the third answer was that it was terrible; and Basia said nothing at all to the third answer, and smoked the cigarette down to her fingers, and then ground the end out on the floor of the cellar of a house in the Śródmieście and said the thing that Krystyna carried out of that cellar and out of that city:

"When my brother was at the school in the Ochota, the Germans came in 1942 and took eleven boys from the yard and shot nine of them in the street and my brother was in the nine and he was fifteen, and they made the ones they did not shoot look at it from the wall; and I have thought about it for two years, and I have decided that a person who is in a room with the enemy and comes out in the morning and gives me a paper that saves eleven people at the barricade is not a whore, and that a person who says she is one is either a fool or a braggart, and that you are neither, so I am going to ask you a different question, and then I shall leave you alone, and the question is: when you are in a room with them, do you count in Polish or in German?"

"In Polish," said Krystyna.

"There," said Basia, and lay back against the wall of the cellar with her arms folded and her eyes shut and the whole of her face suddenly like a little girl's on a pillow, "there, you see, they can never tell that, and I knew it, because you cannot lie in the language you count in. My brother used to say that you can tell a Polish girl anywhere because she counts in Polish when she is frightened. He was fifteen. He was an idiot. He was right about that."

***

The interrogation was on the sixteenth of August in a cellar under a house on the Długa Street, and it was conducted by Krystyna Stankiewiczówna because there was nobody else in the Old Town who could do it.

The prisoner was twenty-two years old and his name was Gerhard Lippmann and he was a Gefreiter of a signals company, and he had been taken in the courtyard of a house on the Świętojerska in the morning by a section of boys who had found him under a bed with a telephone set and a pair of pliers and no weapon, and who had wanted to shoot him, and who had been persuaded by an officer of twenty-four to bring him in instead, because a signaller of a signals company of a division is a piece of paper worth a company of men.

He had been hit in the face with the butt of a rifle in the taking of him, and the left side of his face was swollen and closed; and he sat on a stool in the cellar with his hands tied behind him and his tunic off and his shirt open, and he was shivering in the heat of August in the way of a man in shock, and when Krystyna came down the steps with a lamp and a chair he looked at her and said, in German, in the voice of a boy asking a teacher: "Are you the interpreter?"

"I am the interpreter."

"Thank God," said Gerhard Lippmann, and began to cry, and stopped, and answered every question she put to him for two hours and forty minutes with the exactness of a man who has decided that the whole of his life depends on being useful.

She did it in German. It was the first time in five months that she had used the language to ask a question instead of to be somebody, and it came out of her mouth in an accent that was neither the Viennese woman's nor her own: it was the German of the flat above the laundry, the German of nine weeks of hours, the German in which Wiktor Janowski had made her learn to put a question in four different ways so that the subject would not know which of the four was the important one.

The company. The regiment. The division. The call signs, the frequencies, the relay post on the Wolska, the cable that ran down the Towarowa under the pavement and had been laid by his own section in June, the location of the exchange in the building of a school on the Ochota where there were four hundred lines and eleven men and a major of the signals who was the second cousin of somebody important in Berlin. He gave her all of it, and he gave her the state of the supply of the batteries of his own company, which was two days, and the code word of the day, which was *Hagen*, and the fact that the batteries were issued by a sergeant he could describe down to his watch chain, and by the end of the second hour the captain with the wounded hand was sitting on the third step of the cellar with a pencil and a piece of paper and the whole of his face gone quiet, because a boy on a stool in a cellar on the Długa had just told them where the German artillery was getting its orders from in the Wola.

Then, at the end, when the captain had gone up the steps with the paper in his good hand, the boy asked her the questions of his own.

He asked about his mother: her name was Gerda Lippmann and she lived in a village called Osterode in East Prussia, in a house with an orchard, and she had a bad heart and she had not been told that he had been sent to the east; and he asked whether, when the war was over, somebody would tell her that he had not been in the SS and had not done any of the things that the paper would say that the German army had done in this city. And he asked whether he would be sent back to the camp of the prisoners, or whether the Polish army was going to shoot him, because he had been told in his own company, by the sergeant with the watch chain, that the Poles shot the prisoners; and he said it in a way that was not a plea but a question of fact, addressed to the only adult in the room who spoke his language and who was therefore, to him, the last official in the world.

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna said to him, in the German of a woman from the neighbourhood of Vienna, in a voice with a small amount of human warmth in it, that he was a prisoner of the Polish army and that the Polish army did not shoot prisoners, and that he would be sent to a camp for prisoners of war and that his mother would be able to write to him through the Red Cross at the end of it, and that he was to keep his head down and do what he was told and be patient, and that the war would be over in the winter.

Every sentence of that was a lie except one.

The order of the Home Army's command on the treatment of prisoners and the fact of the transit camp at Pruszków and the state of the country in the autumn of 1944 meant that a Gestrandeter of a signals company in a cellar on the Długa Street had, on the arithmetic of that week, a life expectancy of about eleven days — not because anybody would shoot him, but because the Old Town was about to be taken apart, and because in a besieged building there are no prisoners who can be fed and no guards who can be spared and no way to send a boy of twenty-two with a bandaged face across a line; and the AK's practice in that district in those weeks was to take the prisoners out on the day before the evacuation and to hand them to a German post under a flag, which meant the papers, which meant the boy's own officers would ask him what he had said in the cellar.

She walked up the steps of that cellar into the light of the courtyard of the house on the Długa Street, which was a hot, bright, ordinary August morning with the smoke of the Wola coming over the roofs, and she went into the yard behind the water butt and was sick, twice, in the way she had been sick in a bathroom in a villa on the Frascati Street in July; and then she wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and went back inside and typed up the interrogation report in Polish in eleven minutes with a fair copy in triplicate, which the captain with the wounded hand called the best piece of work that had been done in that room in three weeks.

***

The news of Basia came into that room on the nineteenth of August at half past four in the afternoon from three separate directions, in the way in which news comes into a besieged district, and none of the three of them was official.

The first was from a boy of fifteen who ran in off the Miodowa with his shirt torn and said that a courier had been hit at the corner by the passage of the theatre, in the open, in daylight, at about noon: shot through the chest by a rifleman in the tower of the church of the Holy Cross, at a distance of three hundred metres, in the way the German marksmen had been working that street for eleven days, and that the boys from the fourth house had brought her in and that she had died in the passage in about four minutes.

The second was the pouch itself, which arrived at five: a canvas bag with the buckles and the leather, with the dispatches in it, all three of them, undamaged and in order, handed over at the door by a girl of seventeen who had picked it up off the ground and who said, in the way of that city: "She had it under her." And there was nothing on earth in the bag that should not have been in it, and the captain with the wounded hand opened it and read the three messages and signed the receipt and said to the girl, "Good," and the girl went away, and the receipt went into the file; and on the wall of the room where the file was kept there was a list of the section's couriers with the dates, and afterward, when Krystyna looked at that list at the end of September, in the Śródmieście, she found that Basia Marciniak's name had been written into it in another hand with the date of the eighth of August and the letters *poległa*, which is the word the Polish army used in that war for the dead, and that there was nothing else against it: no age, no address, no number, no family, in a file which contained three pages on a German sergeant's watch chain.

The third direction was the worst and it was the one that belonged to her personally: her own item, the one she had written on the morning of the eighth in her own small upright hand, on a quarter of a sheet of paper, in cipher, in the fair copy of the section's cipher book, and handed to Basia Marciniak at eleven o'clock in the morning at the door of a house on the Miodowa Street in a pouch with the buckles and the leather: a message of eleven groups about the movement of two companies of the German gendarmerie from the Saxon Garden towards the Old Town, which was, professionally, exactly what it should have been, and which was found under the body of a girl of seventeen in a passage on the Miodowa Street, in the pouch, in order, dry, and which came back to the section at five in the afternoon and was filed, and which nobody in the world except Krystyna Stankiewiczówna ever connected to a conversation in a cellar on the fourth of August about the language a person counts in.

She wrote her report on the events of the nineteenth of August in the ordinary form, in three lines, in Polish, at the table with the lamp and the cloth, with a steady hand.

***

The new intelligence came in through the middle of the month in the way of a tide rather than an event, and by the twenty-fifth of August the picture in that room was complete and it was the following.

The Germans were going to take the Old Town.

They were going to do it with artillery fired over open sights at the ends of the streets, with the engineers and the flamethrowers, with the armoured train on the bridge, with the units that had come out of the east and the south — and among them the battalions that the army had raised out of the eastern peoples and the Silesian units and the two groups that had already done the Wola; and they were going to do it house by house, and the whole of the operation was being organised from the Saxon Garden and from the buildings on the Plac Teatralny, which is four hundred metres from the Długa Street.

What that meant, in the arithmetic of a besieged district, was that about four thousand people in the Old Town — of whom eight hundred were wounded and eleven hundred were civilians, of whom two hundred were children — were going to be reduced by about a third, and the AK's command had decided at the end of August to take the fighters and the wounded and the couriers out through the sewers to the Śródmieście and to leave behind the dead and the houses.

And on the twenty-ninth of August the captain with the wounded hand asked her to stay.

He asked it badly and honestly, in the way of a man who has been doing an impossible job for four weeks: that the evacuation would be in three days, that the section's papers would go through on the first night, that the last hours of the Old Town would produce more captured German documents than any other three days of the rising — the orders, the maps, the instructions to the assault groups, the schedule of the artillery, and, most important of all, the identification of the units, because a district going to pieces needs to know to the last hour who is coming — and that there were eleven people in the sector who could read German and that three of them were dead and four were wounded and two were couriers and one was a boy of fifteen who had been taught the alphabet by his mother in Vienna in 1930, and that none of them could do it at the speed at which it had to be done; and that the woman who read German was not, under the section's orders, required to stay.

She said yes in four words, at the table, with the lamp and the cloth, and went back to the translation she was doing, and the whole of why she said yes was in the four words and in the translation and in nothing else: because the information was the last thing that she had that was worth more than her body, and because, in the fifth year, the thing that a person is, is the work.

***

The news of her family, in that month, came to her twice, at a distance, second-hand, from the mouths of people who did not know that they were carrying it.

The first was on the twenty-first of August, in the corridor of the house on the Długa Street, from a woman of about sixty who had come over from the Śródmieście with a dispatch and who was sitting on the floor of the corridor with her shoes off, resting, and who said, when Krystyna gave her a glass of water and asked her, in the ordinary way in which everybody asked everybody that month, whether she was from the centre of the city: "Mokotowska Street, the courtyard with the pump, my sister has the flat on the ground floor with a shop, well, she had a shop; and there is an old man in that building, a schoolmaster, Stankiewicz, he was hit in the leg by a piece of a mortar on the second of August in the courtyard, and he has been in the cellar of the house since then with the leg going bad, and they cannot get him over to the hospital, and the daughter — a girl of seventeen, very thin — is with him and will not leave him, and they are all in the cellar of that building, eleven of them."

The second was on the twenty-seventh of August, in the intelligence room, from the scout of fifteen who had been down to the Śródmieście and back with the post: that a group of the sabotage section had come into the Old Town three nights before through the ruins, and that among them there had been a man from the Mokotowska who had asked in the corridor of the house on the Długa Street— and here the boy stopped, and looked at her, and then said the whole of it, in the flat way of a person of fifteen in the fifth year of that war who has learned to give the news in the order it matters: "There was a man who asked for the woman who reads German, and the captain said he could not see anybody, and the man said that he was her brother and that he had nothing to tell her, and that he was going through with the first group."

She was in the room when the boy said it; and she went on translating the sheet in front of her to the end of the paragraph, which was eleven lines about the state of the ammunition of a German artillery regiment; and then she put the pencil down, and stood up, and went out of the room.

***

The last days of the Old Town are not this chapter's business, and the reason is that they are the book's business, and the book has been carrying them since the first page; what happened in those houses between the thirty-first of August and the second of September 1944 happened to about thirty thousand people at once, and each of them saw a piece of it, and every piece of it was the same piece: a wall coming down, a courtyard with a well and no water, the hospital in the cellars of a house on the Długa with the wounded of four districts in it and eleven women doing the dressings by the light of a lamp, the paintings of the museum wrapped in blankets and stacked in a corridor with a boy of seventeen asleep on them with a rifle across his knees, the children under the staircase with the whole of the day's bread in one basket, the trap door to the cellar passage, and the burning of the papers.

She burned the papers on the first of September.

The section's archive went into the courtyard of the house on the Długa Street at four in the afternoon, in two sacks and a suitcase, and it was burned in the middle of the yard in a fire that took forty minutes and was fed with the whole of the five years of that work: the maps, the order of battle of the German garrison of the district of the Old Town of the city of Warsaw of the month of August 1944, the identical copy of the plan of the fortress with the marginal pencil marks and the word *Bedingt* next to the Gdańsk railway station, the interrogation reports including the eleven lines of the report on the events of the nineteenth of August concerning the death of a courier, and the ledger of the names, which had come over from the Śliska Street in a woman's satchel on the second day of the rising and which Antoni Krupa put into the fire himself with his own two hands, sheet by sheet, standing in front of the fire in the heat of the flames with his spectacles in his pocket, so that no living person would ever read the ledger of the people who had worked for that country in the city of Warsaw between 1939 and 1944.

At six in the evening the section went down into the cellars under the house, and from those cellars there was a passage of four hundred metres to the mouth of the sewer on the Kilińskiego Street, and the crossing that night went by groups of twenty, with a guide to each group, and the guide for the section's group was a man of about forty from the city's water service who had worked in those tunnels before the war and who was going to die in them before the morning; and in the column, in the dark of the cellar, standing in the queue of forty people waiting their turn to go down a hole in the floor of a city that was burning above them, there were: the captain with the wounded hand, the woman of fifty who had never gone down to a cellar in a bombardment, two boys of fifteen and seventeen, the priest of the district with a satchel of the last eleven documents and the sacraments, eleven wounded who could walk, one who could not and would be carried, three women with children, and the woman of thirty with the ledger in her head who answered to the name of Iga Zaleska and who had been hit in the left hip by a splinter on the twenty-eighth of August and could stand only with the aid of a rifle she had cut down into a stick.

Krystyna was the eleventh of them in the queue and Iga Zaleska was the fourteenth, and the two of them stood in that cellar in the dark for forty minutes with their shoulders against the wall of a passage that had been a coal bin in 1939, and in the fortieth minute Iga said, in Polish, in her flat voice, in the dark, with the plaster coming down off the ceiling above them at intervals of about eleven seconds:

"Your brother went in nine hours ago."

Krystyna did not move.

"I am telling you because the captain decided this afternoon that you were not to be told until the crossing was made, and I agreed with him at four o'clock and I do not agree with him now, and the reason is that we are standing in a queue to go into a tunnel that is four kilometres long and has been under fire for two days, and a person who is told a thing on the other side of a tunnel is a person who has been robbed of the hour in which she could have done something about it." Her stick moved on the stone floor. "He was in the first group through, at nine this morning, with the stretcher party of the sabotage section, nineteen men and two stretchers, and they were to come out at the far end on the Warecka Street, and the group that went in at one o'clock found the guides' rope broken in the second passage and could not pass it, and the group that went in at four came back at half past six, and none of them had seen the first group, and none of them had seen anybody coming the other way. So he is in there. He may be in there because the passage is blocked; he may be in there because he is carrying a stretcher and will not leave it; he may be in there because he is dead. I do not know. I am not going to be there. The hip is finished; I am going to stay in this house with the wounded of the second company and I shall be taken on the day after tomorrow, and I have already burned the last of the section's paper and I have got nothing on me that will hang anybody, and in a week I shall be in a cell on the aleja Szucha answering questions with my name, which is what I have been preparing for since 1942 and I am not going to talk about it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because at the mouth of that tunnel there is a guide with a rope, and in the tunnel there are about four kilometres of black water, and I have known you for five months and I know exactly what you are going to do with it, and I want it written down in somebody's head that I told you nine hours late and that it was not my choice." The queue moved; the man in front of Krystyna stepped down through the hole in the floor and disappeared, and there was the sound of a rope taking the weight of a person, and then another man stepped forward. "Take your coat off, Stankiewicz, and put your armband in your pocket and give your papers to the priest, and put your hands on the rope and go, and if you find him in there, do not stop. Whatever you find in there, do not stop. If you stop in that tunnel with forty people behind you, you will kill all of them, and he would be the first to say so, and he is the only person in this city who has the right to say anything to you at all."

"Iga."

"Go," said Iga Zaleska, and put her stick down on the floor and her back against the wall of the coal bin, and closed her eyes, and stayed behind in the Old Town with the wounded of a company that had eleven hours left, and she was taken prisoner on the third of September, and she did not give a name in eleven weeks of interrogations on the aleja Szucha, and the man who interrogated her wrote in his file, in German, one line that has been preserved: *the woman maintains that she is a clerk and knows nothing; she is lying and she is very good, and in time, perhaps, but there is no time.*

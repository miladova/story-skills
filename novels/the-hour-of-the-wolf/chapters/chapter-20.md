# Chapter 20: The Slaughterhouse

Being a German in the middle of a Polish rising, in the first week of August 1944, was a matter of portfolios and handwriting.

He had walked into the Śródmieście on the last day of July with a bag in his hand and four hundred and twenty zlotys and a set of German papers in his pocket: an identity card of the administration, a pass of the garrison command, a Wehrpass, a photograph with his ears showing. On the first evening of August, at half past six, in the courtyard of a building on the Żelazna Street, four men of the Home Army had stopped him, taken the bag off him, put him against the wall of the coal shed, and read all of it in the light of a torch; and then one of them had gone away for twenty minutes and had come back with a man of about forty in a coat, who had read the papers again, and had looked at Phillip for four seconds, and had said, in German with a bad accent: "The card says Konrad. I do not know what that is and I do not want to. But there is a man on the Śliska who will."

And so he had been walked through the back streets of the centre of the city in the dark of the first night while the city was firing at nothing and at everything, from one courtyard to another, in the way in which a person is walked through a house in a waking dream: a gate, a passage, a stairwell with a dead man's coat on it, a kitchen with four women and a lamp turned down, a cellar with a barrel of sauerkraut and two beds, a room over a printing shop where there were eleven people and a dial telephone that did not work.

By the second night he had been in six cellars and had been fed twice and had answered about a hundred questions and had been looked at in about four hundred ways.

The looks are the thing he remembered afterwards, and he remembered them in the order of their temperature rather than in the order of their hours.

There was the look of the woman of sixty in the cellar on the Zielna who had looked at him once, at his shoes and his hands, and had then put a bowl of soup in front of him and gone away and never looked at him again in two days, and who had said, when the boy of the house asked her how she could feed him, one sentence: "My son is with the group on the Wolska, and he is shooting at those, and this one is ours, and the arithmetic of it is not for me."

There was the look of four men of about twenty on the stairs of the printing house, which was a frank, cold, exact, unembarrassed inspection of the kind that farmhands give a cow at a fair: the shoulders, the boots, the hands, the question of what he could lift.

There was the look of an officer of the AK's security section who questioned him for two hours in a room with a candle in the blackout and one hand on a pistol on the table, and who read him the way a man reads a ledger, and who said, at the end of it, "The card in your case is a name in a handwriting I have seen in eleven files, and that handwriting has never once, in five years, been put to a false use. That is the whole of what stands between you and the courtyard, and I would like you to keep it in mind at all times, and I would like you to understand that it is a provisional fact."

And there was the look of the boy.

He was sixteen years old, he wore a stolen German helmet four sizes too big for him, his boots were Polish army boots of 1938 with the soles tied on with wire, and he carried a Błyskawica submachine gun, which is a weapon made in a cellar in Warsaw by men who had been machinists before the war, out of a design that had been drawn with ink on a kitchen table, and which would fire, most of the time, if it was treated with respect; and he had fired his twice.

On the third night of the rising, in the courtyard of the building on the Śliska Street, the boy came up behind Phillip in the dark while Phillip was carrying a crate of bottles to the well, and put the muzzle of the Błyskawica against the back of his neck, and said, in Polish, in the flat voice of a person doing something he has rehearsed: "Turn round."

Phillip turned round with the crate still in his arms.

"You are the German," said the boy.

"I am the German."

"You have a German pass in your pocket and German boots on and you talk like a German and my father was killed in the Ochota in 1942 by men in boots like that, and there is a courtyard at the back of this building with a wall, and it is four metres, and I have got twenty-two rounds in this and nobody in this courtyard is going to say a word about it to anybody." The boy's hands were steady and the muzzle was not. "Say the thing that the Poles say."

"I do not know what the Poles say."

"Then say something in German."

"I am not going to say anything in German to you with that in your hands," Phillip said, "because it is a bad idea in theory and a worse one in practice, and because you have already decided not to do it and are waiting for a reason, and I am going to give you one: I am carrying this crate of bottles to the well because the woman in the second room asked me to, and if I put it down you will have to carry it yourself, and you have got two hands."

There was a silence of about four seconds in the courtyard, with the firing going on four streets away in the direction of the Wola, and the boy with the helmet put the muzzle of the Błyskawica up in the air and said, in a voice about four years younger than the one he had been using: "You are lucky you are useful. That is the whole of the reason. Do not do anything else useful in the next ten minutes, I need to sit down."

And that was the boy, and he was dead eleven days later on a barricade on the Wolska with the helmet still too big for him, and Phillip Brandt, who had been about two seconds from the wall, thought about him every day for the rest of his life, and never once in a way that could be put on paper.

***

The news came through the cellars in fragments, and the fragments assembled in the following order.

On Friday the fourth of August, at about eleven in the morning, the firing that had been in the north-west since Tuesday changed: it stopped being small arms in the streets and became a continuous thing with a rhythm to it, which is the sound of mortars and of a great many vehicles moving; and at noon a boy came into the cellar on the Zielna with the first sentence, which was: "There are troops coming into the Wola from the west, and they are not the Wehrmacht."

What came into Wola in those days had four names and a single character, and it was made up of the material that the Reich had left at the bottom of its drawers: a regiment of the SS's own disciplinary troops recruited from the poachers of the German forests and commanded by a man who had a doctor's degree and a taste for schnapps; a brigade of the Russian and Ukrainian and Cossack volunteers that had been raised in the east by a Polish-born Soviet officer who had changed sides; a battalion of Azerbaijani troops; two police battalions of the German order police; and behind all of them the ordinary apparatus — the gendarmerie, the railway police, the units of the garrison, and the administrative officers of the very district that the offices had spent four years administering.

Phillip, who had worked in Warsaw for four months and had read the German papers of that district, could read the orders in his sleep. There would be an order to clear the streets of the district; then an order that the population was to be collected in courtyards and assembly points; then a movement order for the civilian columns to the transit camp at Pruszków; and then, underneath all of that, in the language of that particular week in that particular district, the actual instruction, which was written down nowhere at all and which every man in those columns understood, and the instruction was: the population of the Wola is to disappear.

He heard it from the other side of the line, standing in the upper window of a house at the corner of the Towarowa and the Grójecka with a pillow on the sill and the whole of his hand on the glass, in the flat of a schoolmistress who had taken him in on the fourth night of the rising when he had needed a roof and no questions; and what he saw through the glass at a distance of eleven hundred metres on Saturday the fifth of August 1944 between eight in the morning and eleven o'clock was not a battle.

It was a city being emptied.

There was a column of about four thousand people on the Grójecka, moving west in the ordinary way in which a population moves when it has been told to go: with the handcarts, the bundles, the children, the old men on the carts, the women carrying the sacks; and on both sides of that column there were soldiers of the SS with their rifles at their hips, and behind it, at the corner, other soldiers were going into the houses and coming out of them with more.

There was a building on the Wolska with the roof on fire and a man on the third floor of it, alone, at the window, doing nothing at all, for the forty minutes that it took the fire to reach him; and there was, twelve metres away from that building, another building with a courtyard, into which a column of people had been put at about nine o'clock, and out of which, at about eleven, there came the noise.

There was a boy of about seven walking alone down the middle of a street with his hands up, and stopping, and standing still in the middle of the road for four minutes while the soldiers went past him on both sides and nobody touched him and nobody spoke to him, and then walking on to the west, and disappearing among the people.

There was a piano being carried out of a doorway on the Wolska Street by two men in the striped jackets that the prisoners from the east wore: out of the door, down the two steps, across the pavement, and set down on the cobbles in the sun, one of them with his hands still flat on the lid for a moment, and then they went back into the house for the next thing, and the piano stood on the pavement on the Wolska Street in the sunshine with nobody in the whole of the world paying any attention to it at all, and that is the picture he kept, and he kept it for fifty-one years.

The history of that week was written afterwards by four hundred people with the numbers and the places in it, and Phillip Brandt never learned any of the numbers, because the numbers were not what he saw; what he saw was the afternoon of the fifth of August at eleven o'clock, from the window of a flat on the corner of the Towarowa and the Grójecka, at a distance of eleven hundred metres, with a pillow on the sill and a woman behind him in the room saying, in Polish, in a voice of the kind a person uses for a litany: *Jezu, Jezu, Jezu* — and the fact, which he carried from that window to the end of his life, that he could not do anything at all except know precisely, in the professional way of a man who had translated such orders into Polish for four months, which of them were being carried out in the streets below him and in what order, and that this knowledge was not a small thing, and that there is nothing on earth that can be done with it.

***

He went west on the sixth.

He was not ordered to go. He went because there was no version of the man he was in which he sat on a chair in a flat in the Śródmieście while his mother and his father were eleven hundred metres away on the other side of a fire; and because of a calculation that he made on the towel with a piece of soap and then rubbed out with his hand, which had three lines in it: that a man in Polish civilian clothes with a bag of dressings was a man who could walk down a street in the Wola for a quarter of an hour in the middle of a massacre, because a massacre has enough to do and does not examine everybody; that the way through the ruins of the ghetto was open, because the ruins were two kilometres of broken brick in which nobody at all had any business, and he knew the two gaps in the wall on the Bonifraterska from the German side of the paperwork; and that he would be back before dark.

He went on the sixth of August at four in the morning, in a jacket and a pair of the schoolmistress's late husband's boots, with his German papers left folded in the lining of a chair in the flat, which was the first time in five years that he had gone anywhere without papers, and with a bag of dressings from the AK's store because there is no story in the world that a bag of dressings cannot tell.

The Wola on the sixth of August smelled of burning, in the way in which, from that week on, the whole city smelled of burning; but in the Wola it was at a different temperature and had a different content: not the smell of a building on fire, which is a smell of resin and plaster and old paper, but a smell of a great many things burning at once which the human nose puts together in the first four seconds and then refuses to assemble again; and after the first four hundred metres he stopped taking it in at all, which is a mercy of the body that every witness of that week has described and which no witness of that week has ever been able to be glad of.

He walked through streets he had walked in as a child: the Wolska, the Towarowa, the Grójecka, the Młynarska, the Karolkowa with the church, the Żelazna with the tram depot, the streets of the district that had been the industrial heart of the city since 1880 with the factories and the gasworks and the slaughterhouse at the end of it.

The yards were open and the doors were open and the windows were open, because in that week the doors of the Wola were left open; and in the open yards there were bundles and handcarts and suitcases and the small objects of the lives of about forty thousand people: a hat, a shoe, a pot of jam, a child's pram with a bag of sugar in it, the cart of a pedlar with the goods still on it, and, at the corner of the Karolkowa and the Skierniewicka, in the middle of the road, a pair of women's shoes standing side by side and upright, in the way in which shoes stand when a person has walked out of them and gone on barefoot, except that there was nobody in the whole of the street.

At about six in the morning he went into the yard of the works on Burakowska Street, which was his father's works, where he had been taken by his father on a Sunday in 1937 to see the furnace being tapped, and where the family photograph of 1928 had been taken in front of the gate with the furnaces behind.

The works were standing. The gate was open and the yard was empty and the machines were in the sheds with the belts on them; there were two lorries in the yard with the bonnets up and the tools out, and a German staff car with its bonnet in the air and a driver's jacket over the seat, and a great many papers on the ground — the archives of the German administration of the works turned out of a cupboard and walked over by a great many boots and left in the yard, the sheets going over on their edges in the wind off the river; and in the middle of the yard there was a single thing that told him, in one second, what had happened in that place, and it was a man's leather satchel standing upright on the ground in the middle of the yard with nothing near it.

He knew the satchel the way a son knows a thing that has been on a chair in the corridor of his house since 1931.

He picked it up. It had his father's initials on the strap in the ink of 1912 and it weighed what a satchel of papers weighs, and in it there were three drawings of an axle, a table book of the works for 1944, a pair of spectacles in a case, and a request, in German, on the writing paper of the works, dated the fourth of August and signed by *Brandt*, for a permit to remove the firm's drawing office records to a place of safety; and the signature had been rejected in the margin, in pencil, in the small precise hand of a functionary, with the word *abgelehnt* and a date, the fifth of August.

Two streets further on, in the cellar of a house on the Młynarska that had been a laundry, he found his mother.

There were nineteen people in that cellar, which was four metres by six, and they had been in it since Friday night, and the whole of the cellar's population looked up at him in one movement when he came down the steps with the satchel in his hand, and the look was the look of people who have been waiting for three days for the door to open and who have decided, all together, that the person who opens it will be wearing a helmet.

And in the corner, on a chair, with a rosary in her fingers and her hands over her ears, in a black dress with a coat over her knees, there was his mother.

She had been fifty-eight years old in April and she looked, on the sixth of August, like a woman of seventy: her hair was down and grey and had not been done since Friday, and there was a wound on the back of her left hand where she had burned it on the stove in the first hour of Saturday when the gas went off, and her eyes, when she looked up and saw him in the doorway of the cellar, did not change at all: they did not light and they did not widen; and she looked at him for four seconds with the entire weight of her whole life in her face and then said, in German, in the voice of a woman in a drawing room in the Hoża Street in 1936: "Wolfram?"

"Mutterschen. It is Phillip."

"Phillip," said Maria Brandt, and then she said it again, and then she stood up and put her arms round her son in the cellar of a laundry on the Młynarska Street in the middle of the fifth day of the murder of that district, and the whole of the nineteen people in the cellar looked at the two of them and then looked away, all at once, in the way of a crowd in a church.

His father was not in the cellar.

What his mother could tell him was four sentences long and she told it in German in the flat voice of a woman reciting a thing she has gone over and over for forty hours in order to be able to say it at all: that the works had been held on Friday morning by the German administration, that the technical staff had been told to stay in the drawing office and to keep the machinery, that her husband had walked to the works at seven in the morning of the fourth of August with his satchel, and that on Friday evening the column of the technical staff of four works of the district had been taken out of the yard on the Burakowska Street by the police and had gone west in the direction of the transit camp at Pruszków, and that a boy from the office had come back to the district at midnight on Friday with the news that the column had not arrived at Pruszków, and that the second half of it had been taken aside in the fields at the edge of the district, and that nobody had seen Albrecht Brandt after that, and that the boy who had brought this had been fifteen years old and had gone out again on Saturday morning, and that she did not know any more.

That is the whole of what the book has about the death of Albrecht Brandt: a satchel standing upright in the middle of an empty yard with a refusal stamped on his last request, and a woman of fifty-eight in a cellar on the Młynarska Street telling her son four sentences in German with her hands over her ears.

***

He took nineteen people out of the Wola.

It was not a plan; it was an arithmetic, and the arithmetic was: the morning of the seventh of August, in the gaps between the columns and the patrols, along the Młynarska to the Ghetto's boundary, and then through the ruins, which were two kilometres wide and in which there was nobody, and out at the other side into the Śródmieście and into the cellars of the streets on the edge of the district that were still in Polish hands.

The party was nineteen people: his mother; the woman of the laundry, who was fifty-four and had a nephew in the AK; the nephew, who was fifteen; four men, of whom two had been in the fire brigade and two had come out of a hospital courtyard on the Wolski side; six women; a girl of nine; and an old man of eighty-one who was a retired railwayman named Kaczmarek and who could not walk.

They went at four in the morning, and the first four hundred metres took forty minutes, and the last two hundred metres took three hours.

The old man was carried on a door with the hinges on it: four men at the corners, of whom Phillip was one, with his mother walking next to him with her hand on the door's edge; and they set the door down in the ash and the brick between every thirty and forty metres and stood and breathed; and between the tenth and the eleventh of the twenty-three stops that the journey took, the old railwayman, who had been in and out of a half-consciousness for the whole of the day, said in Polish, to nobody, in a voice that carried through the whole of the party in the way that voices carry in dead cities: "This is the Gęsia. I worked at the Gęsia from 1919 to 1939. There was a tram stop here in the summer of 1939, and there was a man who sold the water with a lemon in it, and my wife's sisters were in this street, in the house at the corner, and now it is a heap of the same bricks and I am eighty-one years old and it is all the same heap of bricks."

They crossed the Gęsia, the two of them, at about eleven in the morning, and it took an hour and eleven minutes, and the sun was up and the ash was hot and there was no shade in sixteen square kilometres of that city; and about two-thirds of the way across, when the four of them had put the door down and were standing with their hands on their knees, a woman in the party began to cry in a way that was not a woman's crying at all but was as regular and dry as a pump, and one of the firemen said, in Polish, "Not now," and she stopped, as simply as that: she stopped, and stood, and picked up her end of the sack of potatoes, and walked on; and that, Phillip said afterwards to one person only, in 1946, in a kitchen in a different country, was the thing he learned in the rising: how far a person can be taken on the strength of one other person saying *not now*.

They were found at five in the afternoon, on the other side, in the cellar of a house on the Pańska Street, by a section of the Home Army's boys out of the Śródmieście: nine of them, led by a man of thirty with a German pistol in his belt, with the dirt on them of a week of fighting, and with the Błyskawica boy among them, the boy with the helmet, who saw Phillip coming up the cellar steps with a woman on his arm and stopped, and looked at him, and then raised the submachine gun.

"This one," he said, to the man of thirty. "This one is a German. He has a card with *Konrad* on it and I have been told not to touch him and I want to be told again, right now, in front of these people, because I have got ninety rounds in this and there is a woman behind the wall of that courtyard whose whole family was shot in the Wola yesterday."

And the whole of the cellar courtyard of the Pańska Street was quiet for about four seconds.

And Krystyna Stankiewiczówna came up out of the cellar behind him, in a grey coat with the dust of two districts on it and an armband on her arm, and put her body between the boy and Phillip Brandt without saying one word to either of them.

She did not say anything at all. She came up the last three steps, and looked at the boy, and stood there, in front of the man, with her shoulder four inches from the muzzle of a submachine gun held by a boy of sixteen, and she stayed like that, in the middle of a courtyard, in the middle of the rising, in the middle of the whole of the war, for eleven seconds, until the man of thirty put his hand on the boy's arm and pushed the muzzle down towards the cobbles.

"Take him to the staff," he said. "He is somebody's file."

***

They put her in a cellar, and him in the same cellar, the same night.

The cellar was under a house on the Świętokrzyska whose courtyard had a well and whose upper floors had gone in the first week; it had a candle, a table, four beds of laths and straw, and eleven people, of whom two were wounded and one was a woman of eighty with a broken hip who would die three days later; and the whole of the night the firing went on over the roofs, and the whole of the night the water dripped off the broken pipe in the corner into a bucket, and in the morning the smell of the bucket was the smell of that cellar and the smell of that city for the whole of the sixty-three days.

His mother was three feet away, asleep at last on the fourth bed with her rosary in her fingers and her hands over her ears; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna sat on the edge of the bed next to Phillip Brandt with a bowl of water and a rag and washed the blood off his hands.

It was not his blood. It was the blood of the old railwayman, who had died on the door between the Młynarska and the Gęsia at about half past two in the afternoon, at eighty-one years of age, having asked for the door to be put down twice and then having stopped talking, and whom they had carried on the door for another eleven hundred metres, over the ash and the brick, knowing exactly what they were carrying, because the alternative was to leave him, and in that district in that week, the alternative was the ash.

She washed his hands; and then she washed his face; and then she took off his boots, one at a time, and pulled them off by the heel, and turned them up, and shook the ash out of them, and set them by the bed, in the way in which a woman does these things for a man in her room in a time of peace.

And neither of them said, in the whole of that hour, one sentence that could have been quoted in a report; and the whole of what passed between them in that cellar was this: that when she had put the boots down and turned round, Phillip Brandt took hold of her wrist — the left one, the one with the seam of the coat — and held it for about four seconds in the candlelight, and then let go of it and lay back on the bed with his arm over his eyes; and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna sat and looked at the candle until it went out, and then lay down on her own bed, one metre away, with her boots still on and her coat over her, and slept for four hours the way a person sleeps when everything that has been arranged in the world has finally arranged itself around a fact.

***

On the eighth of August, at six in the evening, she was sent for by the staff.

It was in a big flat on the third floor of a building on the Świętokrzyska with the windows out and the blackout hanging in strips and a whole wall of map, and there were eleven men in the room and two telephones that did not work and a dispatch rider asleep on a chair in the corner; and there was a colonel of about fifty with a bad hand, sitting at the table with three maps in front of him and a plate of soup he had not eaten, and a captain with a pencil behind his ear and the eyes of a man who had not slept since Sunday; and the two of them took her through the documents of the intelligence section, in the flat, dry, hurried way of men who have work: the German garrison's order of battle, which they had out of her and out of the plan and out of the prisoners of the first week; the state of the bridges, which they had out of her; the demolitions, which they had out of her; and the question they had written at the top of the paper and could not answer, which was where the material for the defence of the city was coming from, and what was coming, and how fast.

And at the end of an hour and a half, the colonel with the bad hand put down his pencil and said: "There is a man in the cellar on the Pańska. He came out of the Wola on the seventh and he has been in three houses in the Śródmieście since the first of August and there is a card on him with a name in the Major's handwriting, and nobody in this building knows what to do with him. He is a German officer of the Abwehr. He is not my business, and he is not my prisoner, and I have been told that he is yours."

"He is not mine," said Krystyna. "He is the section's."

"Then the section has a problem, and the problem is the following." The colonel put his spectacles on. "We have four crates of German paper out of an office on the Świętokrzyska and out of the post office on the Królewska and out of a railway wagon at the Gdańsk station, and there is not one person in the whole of this rising, including the whole of my staff, including the two of us at this table, who can read German staff paper at the speed at which it has to be read in a siege. We have got a woman in this room who is the best intelligence I have had in four years and who has been under an officer's eye since March and who cannot be used anywhere in this city where she would be recognised, which is everywhere. And we have got a German officer of counter-intelligence down the stairs with a card in his file." He looked at her over the top of his spectacles. "So I am going to ask you, on the section's authority, whether the man can read German maps."

Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stood in the middle of a room in a house on the Świętokrzyska Street on the eighth of August 1944 with the strips of the blackout moving at the window and eleven men looking at her, and knew, in the second she stood there, that she was in the middle of the only true and complete arrangement of her whole life.

"He can read them," she said, "and he is better at it than anybody in this city, including the Germans, and I will answer for that with my own card in the same ledger, and there will be four people in this building who will never forgive me for it, and it will be correct, and he is in the cellar on the Pańska Street with his mother and a broken window and he has just carried an old man nineteen hundred metres over the ghetto on a door."

"Good," said the colonel, and wrote something on the paper, and pushed the plate of soup away, and said, without looking up: "Bring him in the morning."

And it was when they were going out, when the two of them had got as far as the corridor, that the Błyskawica boy came up the stairs with his helmet in his hand and his face grey under the dirt, and stopped, and said to the room in general, in the flat, ordinary way in which that generation delivered the largest news of its life:

"The girl Marciniak. Basia. On the Marszałkowska barricade this afternoon, with the dispatches in her belt. She was nineteen — no, she was seventeen, she was seventeen in May, and she had the pouch and the dispatches on her and she was carrying them to the Šródmieście command and they got her at the corner by the Krucza, and the boys from the fourth house brought her in and she was still holding the belt." He put his helmet on. "The dispatches went on. They were picked up by the man on the stairs. There is nothing left in the pouch."

# Chapter 13: Salt

The route between the German quarter and the north of the city, if a person wanted to go from the Ujazdowski Park to the streets behind the Krasiński Square without passing a checkpoint, ran through the ruins of the ghetto, and Krystyna had found it in the second week of April, and by the last week of April she had taken it four times.

Sixteen square kilometres of it, from the Bonifraterska to the Gęsia, and nothing in it above the height of a ground-floor window except chimneys, and the chimneys were the thing that people talked about, because they had gone up in April and May of 1943 and had stood through what happened and were still standing, hundreds of them, in rows, on the ground plan of the houses they had been part of, so that a person walking through could see the whole of a street in the disposition of its chimneys and nowhere in it a wall.

The brick was grey. The ash had gone into the brick and the brick had gone into the dust, and the dust was in the air of that district on any day without rain, and it was in the coat — she had learned on the second walk that a fur coat coming out of those ruins smelled of them for eleven days, and on the third walk she had begun to wear, for that errand, an old woollen coat of Janina's that she had asked for in the kitchen and been given in silence.

And what people did in it was what people do anywhere: they walked. There was a path from the Bonifraterska to the Świętojerska where the ground was made of broken brick trodden flat and the path was a metre wide in some places and four in others; the black market used the cellars, because nobody followed anybody into a cellar in that district; a man with a barrow of wood went the other way; two boys of about ten were digging in a wall for bricks and stacking them and selling them by the hundred to a man with a horse; and along the edge of it, on the northern side, the guards of the Sipo-SD's posts watched the district's border from the towers of the wall that was not there any more.

She walked it because it was the shortest way between two German streets, and because in it she was, for twenty minutes, nobody: there is no category in an occupied city for a woman in an old coat crossing a brick desert in the middle of the afternoon, and no officer of any police force in Europe in 1944 was looking for her there.

The whole of the city walked through it. It was not a monument, and the people in it were not being solemn; they were going from one place to another place, and the ones who had been made to come there in 1940 and 1941 and 1942 and had been taken away out of it in 1942 and 1943 were not present in it as an atmosphere: they were present in the way that a grave is present to a man who is crossing a field, which is to say that a person crosses, and does not stop, and remembers the field, and does not say anything about it, ever, to anybody.

In the third week of April a man spoke to her in it.

***

He was in a good coat and bad shoes and standing on a heap of brick at the corner of the Świętojerska where the path came out, and she had seen him three times in eleven days, which she had noted in the way she noted everything, and which she had put down, in the first instance, to a man who collects bricks.

"Good afternoon, madame," he said, in Polish, and took off his hat.

She looked at him for the length of time a woman in an old coat looking at a man in a good coat would look at him, and walked on.

"You come out of that villa on Frascati Street," the man said, behind her, in the same pleasant voice, at the same volume. "I have seen you come out of the gate twice: once in the car and once on foot, in the rain, on the ninth. And you speak Polish when you think nobody is listening. You said something to the brick man about the price of his bricks, madame, and you said it in Polish, and if you had said it in the Polish of a Viennese lady it would have cost you nothing, and you said it in the Polish of this city, in the Polish of the Mokotowska, in the Polish of my aunt." He came down off the brick and stood in the path with his hat in his hands. "And a woman who walks twice a week through these ruins, alone, in a coat from Janina, on the route between the German quarter and the north of the town, where there is nothing at all to buy and no German to see, is a woman with something in her life that she does not wish to have explained in an office."

She turned round and looked at him then, deliberately, the way a professional looks at a problem: fifty, thick through the shoulders, a good coat worn soft, a silk scarf, bad shoes with the heel gone on the right, a copy of the *Nowy Kurier* folded under his arm, and a face like a bank manager's, with a small moustache and wet eyes.

"You have made a mistake," she said, in German.

"No," he said, cheerfully, in Polish. "I have not. I have made four mistakes in this war and I remember all four, and this is not one of them; and if you want a fifth opinion, madame, I shall give you one for nothing, and it is that the man in the villa has a driver and a sentry and a girl in the kitchen who cannot lie, and that if a person telephones the office on the Frascati and says, in a voice, that the lady of the house walks in the ruins of the ghetto twice a week on foot, then the person who answers the telephone will write it down, and after he has written it down he will say something to somebody, and after that there will be a conversation with a lady in a cellar on the Szucha which will not take place in Polish." He put his hat on. "Twenty-five thousand zlotys, madame, on the first of every month, in a place I shall tell you. Five thousand today."

She had eleven seconds, in the path, with the smell of the brick and the ash in her nose, and in those eleven seconds she did three things: she read his shoes, she read his teeth, and she decided not to kill him in that place.

She gave him five thousand zlotys out of the household money, standing in the ruins, in the open, with her hand steady; and he counted it with his back half turned, and folded it, and put it in his inside pocket without hurrying, and said, "The first of May, the same place, the same hour, and come alone, and do not bring me a German, madame, because I have known Germans for four years and I do not like them, and I have known you for eleven days and I like you very much."

***

She went home and did the arithmetic, and it took her two weeks, and it was done in the way Wiktor had taught her, which is: not the man but the circle around the man.

His name was Feliks Kaczmarek. He was fifty-three years old, and before the war he had been a clerk of the court in the district court of the Mokotów, dismissed in 1939 because he had made an error of 400 zlotys in a register that he had then been unable to explain; and in the war he had found a living, and the living was the edge of the ruins and the edges of the city's other holes, and the business was this: a man who knows a thing about a person and who has no scruples and who is not himself afraid of the police can be rich; and Kaczmarek had been rich, and had lost weight in 1943, in the way of the trade, because in the summer of 1943 the courts of the underground had sentenced eleven people in this city to death for what he did, and one of the eleven had been a member of the trade on the Praga side who had been found in the river.

He lived on the Ochota in two rooms with his sister, who was a seamstress and who believed that her brother worked in the supply of the German army; he had a tailor on the Marszałkowska who had made the good coat in 1942 and who was owed 300 zlotys for it; he bought his cigarettes, which were of a good brand, from a kiosk on the corner of the Żelazna, at the same hour every morning; he drank on Thursdays at a café on the Chmielna with two men; and he had, since January 1943, a relationship of a very particular kind with the Sicherheitspolizei's office on the aleja Szucha, which was that he sold them people three or four times a year for money, and that both parties understood that the relationship was temporary, commercial, and dangerous, and that the SD kept the card with his name on it in a drawer with a green band, which in that office meant: use, and do not trust, and destroy when convenient.

She could have killed him herself.

She had the training, she had a kitchen knife and a night in the house when the Colonel was in Kraków, and she had a woman's freedom to walk in the German quarter where he would not have gone walking; and she sat in the bathroom of the villa at the end of April with the tap running and took the idea out and looked at it for a long time, the way one looks at a tool. It was possible. It was also the single most dangerous action available to her in the whole of that spring, because a blackmailer who is killed by his client is a blackmailer whose client is examined by four separate kinds of people, and because a woman in service in a German house must never be the subject of any enquiry whatever, and because — and this was the sentence that finished the idea, and it took her eleven days to write it — because if she killed him herself, in a doorway, with a kitchen knife, then the next time the movement asked her for something that required a person to be spent, she would find that she had become the sort of person who does that, and she would be right, and nobody in the world would be able to say she was wrong, and in two years she would be exactly what the SD said she was.

So she took it to the cellar on the Śliska on the second of May, with the facts written on nothing at all, in the order of a case.

Krupa listened to the whole of it standing up with his arms folded, and when she had finished he took off his spectacles and looked at the candle for a while, and then he said, "How long?"

"Since the twenty-first of April. Three payments of the first of the month: five thousand on the day, and the first of May he took twenty-five thousand, out of my own money; the pearls went for it. He will come on the first of June, and then on the first of July, and every month until the end of the war, and he will come to my funeral."

"Does he know what you are?"

"No. He knows what I am not."

"Does he know that the man on the Frascati is counter-intelligence?"

"He knows a name on a plate and a car at a gate." She heard her own voice stay level, which was a professional matter and took work. "He is a clerk. He cannot put two and two together about an office, but he can put a woman and a gate together, and he can send one letter, or make one telephone call, and after that the arithmetic is not his business, it is the SD's, and you know what they will do with it because it is the only thing they ever do: they will open a file on the lady of the house on the Frascati, and they will put one man on it, and the man will be a good one, and in three months they will have her."

"Yes," said Krupa.

He walked to the end of the cellar and back, which took him eight paces, and then he said: "I am going to tell you what happens now, and I want you to understand that I am telling you the procedure and not asking you for a preference. The man is a szmalcownik: he sells the lives of Poles to the Sipo-SD for money, and the courts of the Polish state have sentenced eleven of that trade in this district in eleven months, and I have three of those sentences in a drawer and one of them is a man I went to school with in 1912. He is not your problem, Stankiewicz. He is a case. It will be referred to the court of the district on the ordinary papers; the sentence will be prepared within a week, and the sentence will be a sentence of death; and the execution will be carried out by a group of the Kedyw and not by anybody in this room, and it will not happen near you, and it will not happen in a way that any office in this city can connect to the household on the Frascati. You will be told the week but not the day. On the day you will be seen, publicly, in the German quarter, for five hours, from six in the evening, at a place where there are a great many officers. That is the whole of it, and the timetable is a fortnight, and the price is a man, and the price is not yours to talk about afterwards."

"What is my part in it?"

"Your part in it is that you have brought me a case and a description, and that from this hour until the execution you will not go into the ruins, you will not go to the Chmielna, you will not speak to him, and you will pay him nothing on the first of next month, because he will not be alive by then." He put his spectacles on. "And you will not be told his name again in this room, or by anybody, ever. It is a case now. It goes into the ledger. That is what the ledger is for."

She sat with her hands in her lap for a moment, and then she said the thing that she had come to say and had not said yet: "May I know what the sentence says?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it is not yours," said the Major. "Because in the file of the paper that goes to the court there will be a page of the evidence, and on that page there will be a line that says the case came to the attention of the court by way of information received, and there will be no name under that line, and that is so that when somebody is arrested in three years' time and the file is read by a man with a lamp, the file says nothing at all. You will have the satisfaction of the mathematics and no share of the sentence, and that is the whole of the honour available in this trade, and it is not negotiable, and I am sorry if you have been in France, where they do it differently."

***

The pearls went on the twenty-sixth of April, in the morning, on the Marszałkowska.

The pawnbroker's was a shop with a green blind and three gold watches in the window and a bell over the door, and the man behind the counter was about seventy and had a loupe in his eye and a hand like a pair of tweezers, and he looked at the necklace for four minutes with a lamp and then told her, in a low voice, exactly what it was: forty-one pearls, twelve millimetres at the top, a white gold clasp with one small diamond, of a good house, bought in Paris in December 1943, and stained at the third pearl from the clasp by perfume, which is the mark that tells a jeweller that a woman has worn a thing every day against her skin.

He offered eighteen thousand zlotys. She asked for twenty-two; he said twenty; she took it; and the whole negotiation took nine minutes and neither of them looked at the other's face, and he counted the notes out twice on the counter and did not write anything down in any book, and she went out of the shop with her collar up and the money in the lining of her bag, and did not feel anything that she had expected to feel.

The paste was on the Kercelak market the same afternoon, from a man who sold artificial jewellery out of a suitcase next to a woman who sold buttons: a strand of imitations of forty-one pearls, of the colour of skimmed milk, with a clasp of silver plate; nine hundred zlotys, and a second clasp of white metal with a stone in it for another two hundred.

She did the substitution in the bathroom at the top of the house on the evening of the twenty-sixth, at the mirror over the basin, with the door locked and the geyser going: the pearls, which she had worn against the skin of her collarbone every day since the twenty-third of December 1943, off; the imitation on; and the whole of it a matter of eleven seconds, and then forty minutes of work with the clasp, the silk cord, the flocking of the clasp box, a length of the black adhesive tape that the German administration used on parcels and that is called by soldiers *der Panzer*, cut into two narrow strips, warmed with a candle until it was adhesive in the way that a jeweller's wax is adhesive, and worked into the clasp's box and the cord with a needle so that the imitation joined the real box and the real wax and the real thread and did not show, from any angle, at any distance, under any light, to anybody who was not going to spend a quarter of an hour with a loupe and a lamp.

She looked at it in the mirror to check it, then, with the taps running hot and the steam rising off the bath, and it hung on her neck exactly as the pearls had hung, and it was exactly as good as the drawing of seven hats, and it was 4,500 francs' worth of glass on the throat of the woman in the file.

Then she put the receipt of the pawnbroker's, which was a slip of paper with a number on it and no name, into the lining of her glove for the rest of the week, and on the first of May she burned it on a plate in the bathroom and washed the ash down the basin, because the only piece of paper in the world that could ever connect Marion Herschlag to a pawnbroker in the Marszałkowska was that slip, and because a woman in service in a German house can afford exactly one mistake.

***

They took him off a tram stop on the Chmielna on Friday the twelfth of May, at twenty past seven in the evening, in a car that had been stolen from a German requisitioned garage on the Wola two days earlier and had been repainted at a workshop in the Ochota by a boy of seventeen who thought he was painting a hearse; and the whole of it took eleven seconds and was done by four men in greatcoats with armbands taken off German coats, and the street was a street of people who looked the other way with total discipline, which is the great gift of an occupied city to whoever is working in it.

Krystyna was at a charity concert in the concert hall on the Nowy Świat at that hour, at a table with von Hagen and Lindemann and two officers of the Luftwaffe and the wife of an industrialist, in a dress of grey silk with the paste pearls at her throat, listening to a Polish violinist of forty play the Brahms concerto, which he played like a man with a train to catch, and which the room applauded for two minutes and forty seconds, and she drank a glass of Hungarian wine and talked to the wife of the industrialist about the price of silk; and at ten past nine von Hagen took her home in the car and told her, in the way he told her everything that was not sealed, that he had had a signal from the section that the SD had picked up a man on the Chmielna who had nothing to do with anything and would be released in the morning, which is what happens in a city where four police forces arrest people all day: the paper goes into a drawer, and the drawer is emptied every Monday.

The body was found in the ruins of the ghetto on the following Monday morning by the two boys who dug bricks: a man in a good coat with his shoes gone and a paper of the twelfth of May in his inside pocket, and the paper said, in the way of that city's papers, that the Führer had received a report on the harvest; and the boys took the good coat and the paper and left the man, and it was four days before anybody in an office wrote him down at all, and by then he was a name in a ledger in Pawiak, in the section of the unidentified, where four hundred people went that year.

She was at home when she heard it. She heard it from Janina, who came back from the market with the news in the way that all news came in that city — a sentence between the price of the butter and the tram — and who said it as a thing of no importance, because the death of a man in the ruins was of no importance, and because she did not know his name, and because the coat had gone to the tailor's nephew, and because the whole of Warsaw had stopped counting.

And that evening, in the bath, with the water too hot and the tap running, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna washed her own hands, and looked at them, and did the whole accounting in one piece, which took about four minutes, and it was this: that a man who had made his living out of the deaths of Jews on the edge of the field where the Jews had been burned, and who had taken 30,000 zlotys off a woman whose whole value to her country was that no office ever looked at her twice, and who would have gone on taking it until the end of the war and then for as long afterwards as he could find her, was dead; and that the movement had done it with paper, procedure, a stolen car and a sentence of a state that did not exist, and had done it in the way that everything in that city was done, which was in three weeks, and by hand, and with four men; and that she had not killed him and had wanted to, and that the difference between those two things was the whole of the difference between her and the man in the good coat; and that the water was too hot, and that she had to get out, because the Colonel was coming back at nine and she had to dress.

***

The poisoned gift was arranged in the cellar on the Śliska on the third of May, and it took forty minutes, and the whole of it was paper.

They gave her a document: two pages, typed, on the paper of a German staff office of the pattern used by the rear services of the district, with the file reference of a section of the Order Police, and in it: an order concerning the movement of two hundred and forty rifles and eleven thousand rounds of ammunition from a depot in the south-east of the city, and a schedule, and a map annex, and behind it a statement that the material had been issued to a unit of the Polish underground, and a date on which the Home Army was, according to the paper, to concentrate the material for the rising which the underground was, according to the paper, preparing for the summer in the districts on the right bank.

The paper was a forgery, and it was a forgery of a quality she had never seen in France: done in the workshop of a man on the Śródmieście who had been a lithographer of banknotes before the war and who had made the paper, and the stamp, and the typeface of a German machine, and the signature of an Oberst who had never signed it, in the handwriting of the signature copied from a signed photograph in a German office that a Polish charwoman had cleaned for four years; and the whole of it was designed with the single purpose that the German counter-intelligence of the district should read it, believe it, and act on it, and thereby spend the next six weeks of the summer of 1944 in the wrong part of the city.

"You will give it to him as your own," said Krupa, "and you will do it in the manner in which you give him everything else, which is as a discovery with a chain you can describe."

"I have no chain to describe."

"Then you will have one in four days, because we are going to build you one; and it will be this." He took off his spectacles. "There is a Polish woman of about fifty called Wiktoria Kęs, an Austrian subject born in Kraków, who has worked in the German administration since 1941 as a clerk in the office of the garrison's Building Section, where there are three hundred and forty files a week of building permits and requisitions, and who is going to be of interest to your Colonel in the following way: on the eleventh of May a package of papers concerning the property of the German administration in the district of the Praga will be delivered to an address that she has used before, and she will be the person who is written down in your account of it. You will not meet her. She does not exist."

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it: a chain, a name, a paper, an act of the enemy which the enemy will spend six weeks hunting." He put the spectacles back on. "And here, Stankiewicz, is the part that you will not like, and I want you to hear it in this room. This paper is going into the hands of a German counter-intelligence section, and it is going in with your fingerprints in the sense that matters, which is that you will be the person who brought it in and the person who described the chain. If it is ever traced — not by the man on the Szucha, whom we can manage, but by some clerk in an archive in Berlin in 1946 with a table and a lamp and a cross-reference — then the trail ends in a file, and the file has a note in it that a lady employed in the house of an Oberst of the counter-intelligence section delivered the material on the fourth of June 1944. That is what we are asking you to put your name under. Say no now and we will do it another way, and it will take a year."

She looked at the two pages on the table for eleven seconds.

"Give me the chain," she said.

***

She gave it to him on the seventh of May, at home, in the study, in the evening, with the lamp on and the door open, in the manner in which she gave him everything: standing at the corner of the desk with her hands behind her back, in the tone of a woman reporting something that has amused her, because that was the tone in which a mistress was permitted to be useful, and it had never once been questioned.

He read the two pages twice, standing, with his spectacles on; and then he read them a third time, sitting, with a pencil, and made marks in the margins, and went to the map on the wall of the study with the paper in his hand and stood in front of the Praga district for four minutes; and then he sat down again and read the schedule for the fourth time, and at the end of it he looked up at her over the top of his spectacles with an expression she had seen once before, in Paris, on the night of the ball at the Majestic, when a general had shaken his hand.

"How did you get this?"

"A woman in the Building Section," she said, and gave him the chain, and the chain was complete: the address, the name, the office, the transaction in the corridor of a requisitioned building, the price, the fact that she had been given it by a person in the office of a firm of contractors who had done work for the garrison, and that the woman had asked for two thousand zlotys and immunity, in a whisper, with her eyes on the floor, in the way in which every Polish clerk in every German office in that city did everything.

"Two thousand zlotys and immunity," said von Hagen, and put the paper down flat on the desk with the flat of his hand. "Precisely. My dear, do you know what you have brought me?"

"A forgery," she said.

And there was a moment — one second, no more, in which the lamp on the desk lit the room and the sentry's boots went past the window on the gravel outside, and the whole of the war stood in the study on the Frascati Street and waited to see whether a Prussian of fifty-four was going to read two pages and hear the word *forgery* and go for the telephone.

And von Hagen laughed, once, and said, "Of course it is a forgery. Every piece of paper that comes out of an underground movement and reaches a staff office is either a forgery or a trap or both. That is not what I asked." He stood up and took his glass off the tray and poured two fingers of the cognac and did not drink it. "What I asked is what it is for. And this — this is a very good forgery, and it is a forgery of a document that does not exist in any German office, because the Order Police of this district has no depot in the Grochów and the unit it names has not existed since the twenty-first of April; and the reason somebody has spent the paper and the stamp and the man who can write an Oberst's signature is that they want me to spend the next six weeks looking in the Grochów for two hundred and forty rifles." He drank the cognac. "Which means the rifles are somewhere else, and something is going to happen somewhere else, and somebody in the underground believes that I am the sort of man who is flattered by a paper. I will flatter them. I shall put four sections on the Grochów within the fortnight, and I shall tell the garrison's Ia on Monday that I have the trail of the autumn's ammunition, and the whole of that side of the staff will be looking at the Vistula's right bank for two months, and it will be my operation, and my reputation, and — my dear — my paper." He looked at her with real warmth. "You have brought me a trap and told me it is a trap and let me decide. Nobody in this city has ever done that for me. Where did you learn to do it?"

"From a dressmaker in Paris," she said, "who used to say that a seam is only a seam if you say it is a seam."

That night, in the dark, with his hand on her shoulder and the paste pearls on the chair in the box in which the real ones had come from the rue de la Paix, von Hagen told her the last thing of the evening, which was the thing she had been waiting half a year to hear, and which he told her in the voice of a man announcing an acquisition.

"They have given me the man I asked for. The Sipo-SD is transferring an officer to Warsaw in May — he is late, everything in that building is late — and he is an expert of the kind they don't breed any more: a quiet little clerk with a card index and no field experience and a first-class mind. His name is Kalbach. He has been writing to me about the French papers for four months, and I have read his letters and I have asked for him and he takes up his post on the eighth, and the first thing he will do is ask for the box from Paris." He yawned, and turned over, and said, into the pillow, comfortably: "He is going to find out what happened in Paris in January, and I am going to let him, because I want to see whether the SD can do the work that my department should have been doing for a year. And then we shall see whether the Fräulein file was a real thing or whether it was a clerk with a chart and a grudge."

# Chapter 25: The Capitulation

The second of October 1944 began with paper being burned and ended with a silence, and the whole of the city had one day in which to learn how to stop.

In the courtyards of the four surviving districts of Warsaw the files of a rising went into the fires in the course of that morning: the orders, the muster rolls, the cipher books, the ledgers of the intelligence sections, the lists of the couriers, the dispatch registers, the cards of the people in the files; and in the courtyard of the building on the Wilcza Street the whole of the counter-intelligence section's archive burned for two hours and eleven minutes from nine in the morning, and the men who were feeding it into the fire did it in the way of an institution, with a rota and a man with a broom, and a list of what had been burned, and a carbon of that list that went into a tin and was buried in a second cellar under a different brick, because that is how an institution behaves when it is going out of existence and intends to come back.

At noon the officers shaved.

That is the detail that the books of that year do not have and that the people of that city insist on: that the soldiers and officers of the Polish Home Army shaved on the second of October 1944, in the courtyards and the cellars and the stairwells of the ruins, with cold water and broken mirrors and borrowed razors, in the last eleven hours of their war, because the agreement that had been signed in a tent above the city said that the soldiers would march out as soldiers and be treated as prisoners of war, and that a soldier who marches out of a city to go into captivity is a man who shaves first; and there is a whole history of Europe in that sentence and none of it is in the papers.

And at eight in the evening the guns stopped.

They stopped by agreement, at the hour written in the document, along a line that ran through the middle of a city in which about a thousand buildings were burning and about two hundred thousand people were alive and about two hundred thousand were dead or gone; and the silence that came when they stopped is a thing that was described in 1945 by about forty people who were in that city, in four languages, in eleven countries, and every one of them despaired of it: that a city which has been under artillery for sixty-three days is not quiet when the guns stop; it is enormous, and it has a sound in it that is not the sound of a city, and the sound is the wind going through nineteen thousand roofless buildings, and the dripping of water in a thousand broken cellars, and the noise of a fire that is not being fought, and, underneath all of it, the sound of people — about four hundred thousand of them — who have not been allowed to make any noise at all for two months and now, on the night of the second of October, in the dark, in the ruins, went up into the streets and talked.

It was on that night, in the eighth hour of the silence, that Krystyna Stankiewiczówna went out of the Polish lines in the southern Śródmieście and walked into a district that no longer had fronts in it, with a hat on and an armband in her pocket and a canvas satchel, and slept the two hours from midnight to two in the cellar of a burned house, and went on before dawn to the Mokotowska Street.

***

The building on the Mokotowska Street had taken two shells and a fire in the first week of September, and it was standing, in the way that buildings stood in that city in the autumn of 1944, which is that the walls of the front were there and the second floor was a space with the sky over it and the stairwell had a black hole where the section from the first floor to the second had been and a way up through the rubble of the landing on the first floor.

The flat at the front of the second floor was not a flat any more: the windows were gone, the doors were gone, the pianino stood in the corner of the drawing room with its lid open and its case split and eleven strings hanging out of it, and the corridor was full of brick to the depth of a man's knee and there was a fall of the ceiling at the end of it where the boy of fourteen had sat with his own chair on a Tuesday; and the four names of that building's courtyard — the caretaker, the widow on the ground floor, the coal man's boy, and the woman who owned the staircase — were written in pencil on the wall of the entrance by somebody who had gone out with a bundle on the third of August, so that whoever came back would know who had been there and who had not.

In the back room, on the bed, with a blanket and a coat and a pillow of folded sacking, there was a man of sixty-nine with a wound in the right thigh and about four days of his life left.

The wound had been made by a piece of the mortar that had come down into the courtyard on the second of August, and it had been dressed in the first hour with a torn sheet and boiled water and the tincture out of the household's medicine box, and the whole of the medicine of that building had been used up by the ninth of August; and the wound had gone the way in which a wound goes in a city in which there is no surgery, which is that the flesh around it had swelled and blackened at the edge and had begun to smell like something sweet and wrong, and that the fever had been there for eleven days, and that on the twenty-eighth of September the old man had stopped being able to lift his own head off the pillow, and had said, in Latin, to his daughter of seventeen, in the flat voice of a man reading a lesson: *satis est, puella* — it is enough, girl.

His daughter of seventeen was sitting on a chair at the head of the bed with her knees drawn up and her hands round them, and she had eaten nothing for nine days that she had not shared with her father, and she had washed him twice a day in a basin with water carried from the pump in the courtyard of the block next door, four storeys up and down, and she had kept the flat's two rooms in the condition in which a house is kept by a person who intends that whatever comes through the door shall find it in order: the brick swept into one corner, the books in their rows along the skirting, the photograph of her mother at thirty in a white blouse on the table with a candle in front of it and a matchbox; and she had drawn, on the wall beside the door of the flat and on the wall of the courtyard by the pump, in chalk, in the two places where she had drawn it for two years, the anchor: the P and the W and the curving line of the cable, done properly, in the way her brother had taught her in 1942, with the tail of the P crossed so that anybody who knew would know and nobody else would.

The two sisters looked at each other across the room for about four seconds.

"Krysia," said Hanna, and got up off the chair, and did not come round the bed; and then she said, in the flat, exhausted, entirely practical voice of a girl of seventeen who has been running a household for four weeks: "He has been asking for you since Sunday. He cannot lift his head and he has a fever of about forty and he does not always know where he is, and when he does know he asks for you, and I have told him since the second of August that you were in the other district and that you would come, and he has believed me, because I have told him nothing else for nine weeks, and he has kept the whole of it in his head the way he keeps everything, and if you tell him anything now that does not fit into it, I will not forgive you." She took hold of her sister's sleeve, at the wrist, hard, the way the brother had taken hold of it in a courtyard in March. "He knows. Krysia. He has known since 1940. Do you understand me? He does not need to be told and he does not need to be lied to. He needs the last line of the ninth ode of the first book, because he cannot lift the book and I have not been able to find it and I cannot remember it and he has been asking me for it since Thursday."

***

She went to the bed and knelt down beside it.

Her father's face at sixty-nine, in the grey light of a ruined room on the third of October 1944, was the face of a man of eighty: the white moustache, the cheeks fallen in, the eyes closed and the skin of the forehead like paper over a frame; and there was a beard on him of four weeks and a scab on the lip, and his hands were outside the blanket with the fingers half open, and one of the hands was the hand that had taken hold of a doorframe in a doorway of that same flat on the thirty-first of July 1944, when he had walked past a daughter he had buried in his own handwriting in 1940 and had gone into the back room and shut the door.

She sat on the floor beside the bed with her shoulder against the horsehair of the mattress and did not touch him, because the whole of the right side of his body was a wound, and said, in Polish, quietly:

"Tatusiu. It is Krysia."

Her father's eyes came open and found her; and there was, in the whole of the four minutes that followed, nothing in his face that could be called recognition, because a man in a fever of forty degrees in the fourth day of a dying wound does not perform recognition, and what there was instead was the thing that was left when the performance had gone, which is that he looked at her, and the whole of his face changed as a surface changes when a lamp is turned on behind it, and he said, in Polish, in a voice with almost no breath in it at all:

"Girl. Where is the book?"

"The book is there, tatusiu. The Horace. I have it."

"Read me the end of it. The ninth. I have been holding the whole of the rest of it and I cannot hold the end. Where are my spectacles?"

"They are on the table, and I have them, and I am going to read it to you." She did not have the book and the book was in a shelf behind her head and she could not have found the ninth ode of the first book of Horace in that room in nine hours and the reason was not the book, and the reason was that the whole of the Latin she had been taught in the school of the Sisters of the Sacred Heart between 1929 and 1937 had been taken out of her head by four years of German and French and Polish and the trade, and what was left in it was a plumber's list: *aqua, pons, schola, luna, nix*.

And Hanna, from the doorway, with her arms folded and her back against the frame, said it.

She said it in a rush, in the flat, small voice of a girl reciting a lesson she has been holding in her head for six days, in the order in which her father had made her learn it in the winter of 1941 when she was fourteen, and she said the whole of the last four lines of the ninth ode of the first book, and she said them well, with the quantities right and the caesura in the right place, and her father's mouth moved with the ends of the words the way the mouths of very old men move with the ends of prayers, and at the finish he said something that was in Latin and was not from the ode at all, and his eyes came open again and looked for the doorway and found his daughter of seventeen standing in it with her arms folded.

Then he looked at the daughter who was kneeling on the floor beside him, at the level of his hand, and said the sentence that is the whole of what her father ever said to her about the five years of her life between September 1939 and October 1944, and said it in Polish, and said it slowly, and it took him a long time:

"You did what there was."

And then he said her name, once, in the way in which people say a name that they have been keeping: "Krystyna." And the hand that was outside the blanket came off the mattress by about four centimetres, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna took hold of the hand of a man of sixty-nine who had taught Latin for forty years in the schools of the city of Warsaw; and she held it while he stopped being able to hear the room, and while his breathing took the shape of a man walking up a staircase; and he died at about nine o'clock in the morning of the third of October 1944, with his spectacles in the hand of his elder daughter and his younger daughter standing in the doorway with her arms folded, exactly as she had been standing when he had looked at her last.

***

They buried him in the courtyard on the afternoon of the same day, in a sheet, in a hole dug by four men in the corner of the yard by the wall of the shed where he had once kept the coal, and the four men were the caretaker of the building, who was seventy-one and had known him for thirty years, and his two sons, and a young man from the third floor who had been a clerk in an insurance office and had spent the rising in the cellars of the city and had come back to the Mokotowska on the second of October with a rifle that the AK had not taken from him because there had been no time; and the priest of the parish of the Holy Cross came, at about five in the afternoon, having walked eleven streets of a surrendered city in a coat and a hat, and said the office for the dead over a sheet in a courtyard with eleven people standing round it, and did not use a book, because he had said that office about four hundred times in the previous nine weeks, in courtyards, and knew it.

Nobody said anything at the graveside. The whole of what was said over Albrecht's son's father's grave is not relevant here; the whole of what was said over Jerzy Stankiewicz was said by his daughter of seventeen, who took a piece of chalk out of her pocket at the end of the office, after the priest had finished and the men had filled the hole in and the caretaker had put the spade against the wall of the shed, and drew, on the wall of the shed above the place where her father's head was, in the fresh mortar of a patched-up bullet hole, in the way of a person doing a thing that has been done four hundred times: the anchor.

The caretaker looked at it and said nothing. The two sons looked at it and said nothing. The clerk from the third floor said, after a moment, in Polish, in a voice that was not quite steady, "They will be back in this city in the spring and they will whitewash every wall in it, and that will be gone by the summer." And Hanna Stankiewiczówna, seventeen years old, in a coat that had been her mother's, with a piece of chalk in her hand, said: "Then I shall draw it again in the summer."

***

The compact came out of the drawer of the kitchen dresser at about six in the evening, in the ruins of the front room, with the light of a candle and the sound of a city burning four streets away.

Krystyna had carried that object against her body in four countries and two occupations for three years and eleven months; she had taken it out of the drawer of an Oberst's desk in a villa on the Frascati Street in the German quarter at twenty past four in the morning of the twenty-fourth of September; she had carried it through the crossing of the Mokotów district on the twenty-eighth of September in the pocket of a coat that had been made for it; and she stood in her mother's kitchen now with the thing in her two hands and unscrewed the false floor of it with the edge of a knife, and took out of it a tablet of the size of a small fingernail, wrapped in a piece of oiled paper, which a man with a stick had given her in a church in Paris in the winter of 1940 with the sentence: *you will not be taken alive and talking; this is not courage, this is tradecraft.*

The tablet went into the rubble in the sink.

She tipped it out of the paper into the wet bricks and the ash and the broken plaster of the sink, and then she ground it into the rubble with the heel of her boot, three times, the way a person grinds out a cigarette in a street; and she put the empty compact back in her pocket; and the whole of the reason for it was a piece of arithmetic that had been done in her head over four days, and the arithmetic was this: that in the column that was going to form on the fourth and fifth of October at the edge of the city there were going to be about two hundred thousand people walking west to a transit camp at Pruszków where the German administration sorted the population of a city into four categories, and that a woman of twenty-four who was in the third category, with a sister of seventeen on her arm, with a name that was in a German file of nine pages in a building on the aleja Szucha, and with the papers of the destruction of her city in the lining of her coat, was not going to be taken alive and talking, and was also not going to be dead, because she had a sister, and because her brother had told her in a tunnel under the Saxon Garden to have the rest of it, and because the phrase *I have been given the rest of it* has a meaning in the Polish language which the sentence in the church in Paris did not have, and which was the only thing that the whole of the five years had left in her hands; and so the tablet went into the rubble of a sink in a flat on the Mokotowska Street, and the compact went into her pocket empty, and it was in her pocket for the rest of her life.

***

The column formed at the western edge of the city on the fourth and fifth of October 1944, and it was not a column; it was a city walking.

It is estimated that between four and five hundred thousand people left Warsaw in the first ten days of October 1944, of whom about eleven thousand were soldiers going into captivity in a separate column with their officers and their wounded, and the rest were civilians going west: to the transit camp at Pruszków, and through it and out of it and onwards, to the villages and the towns of the country west of the capital, to relations and strangers and farms and the houses of people who had been told that there was a train coming with the population of a city on it.

They went out along the Grójecka and the Żelazna and the Wolska, in groups of family and street and courtyard, with handcarts and prams and sledges and shoulders and the last of the food of a city; and the whole of the first two days of that movement was watched by the German officers of the evacuation commission with clipboards at the crossing points; and the Poles went past those officers in a silence that had in it about two hundred years of practice, and the officers looked at their papers and made their marks, and the whole of it went on for eleven days and nights, and the city behind the column went on burning.

The traffic was in two directions at the crossings, because the German administration and the army were also using the roads, and there is no more complete picture of the whole of the eastern war than that, and nobody who was there has ever been able to write it down properly: two hundred thousand Poles walking west with their lives in a cart, and, in the same hours, on the same road, in the other lane, the vehicles of a German army going east towards the Vistula, and, between the two lanes, standing in the mud with a clipboard and a lamp, a sergeant of the Feldgendarmerie of about forty, marking the paper of the one and not looking at all at the other.

Krystyna and Hanna Stankiewiczówna went out of the city on the fifth of October, in the second hour after dawn, in a group of about forty people from the Mokotowska Street, with a handcart that belonged to the caretaker and held four bundles and a crate with a pianino's worth of nothing in it — the pianino itself had been left where it was, on the floor of the front room of the flat with its strings hanging out, because a piano of 1904 in a ruined flat is a thing that is either the beginning of the fortune of the person who carries it or the end of the life of the person who tries.

Before she left the flat, Krystyna Stankiewiczówna went to a door in the courtyard of a building on the Ogrodowa, four streets away, and knocked on it in the way she had been told; and the woman of about sixty with the tape measure round her neck opened it and looked at her for about four seconds and then stood back and said, in the flat voice of a person who has been in a courtyard every day of the rising: "The coat. Yes. Come in. You have brought your sister. She will have the green one, it is too small for anybody else and it is lined; you will have the grey one, and it has been re-lined, and the lining is a French one, and it has been opened along the side seam and closed again, and there is nothing in it now and there will be nothing in it when you go out of this city, and I want you to tell me that you understand me."

"I understand you."

"Then take them, and do not come back to this street, and if you are alive in the summer, come and tell me what happened to the coats." She gave them the coats and a piece of bread and a lump of salt tied in a rag, and she stood in her doorway and watched the two of them walk to the end of the street, and then she went in and shut the door of a flat in which there were four coats left on a rail and a list of their owners in her head, and she was in that flat in 1946 and in 1951 and in 1964, and she never once told anybody in her life what she had been doing during the war with the coats.

***

The fence was at the edge of the city, on the fourth of October, in the yard of a factory on the Wolska, and it is the last scene that Krystyna and Phillip Brandt had together in the year 1944, and the whole of it took about eleven minutes.

He was on crutches by then, in a Polish coat of the size of a man larger than himself, with a cap and a stick and a bandage showing under the trouser leg; and he was not going out of the city with the column, and the reason was plain: that a man of twenty-seven with a German identity card in the administrative language of the Reich and an accent that would come out of him the moment he was tired, in a transit camp administered by the Sicherheitspolizei with an office that had a file of nine pages in it about a woman whose name was in the same file, was a man who was going to be picked out of a column of two hundred thousand people by a clerk with a list within four days of arriving at Pruszków; and the AK's people had told him so, and he had agreed with them, and he was going to stay in the ruins of the city with about three hundred people who had decided to stay: the ones with no papers, the ones with the wrong papers, the ones who had been in the German service and the ones who had been in the service of the Polish state, and the ones who had nothing left in the world anywhere else.

They stood at the mesh of a factory fence, a metre apart, with the whole of a column of the population of a city going past them on the road outside, and the column did not look at them, and they did not look at the column.

"I have the papers," she said. "Four hundred and forty pages of the schedule of the destruction of the city, and the evacuation order, and the file from the safe with the written recommendation about my family in his own hand, and the notebook of the man in the Mokotów with nine pages of tables in it, and the ring." She touched her collar-bone with two fingers. "All of it goes into the lining of the coat at the Ogrodowa in an hour, and it comes out of it on the other side of that camp, and there is a man in the section's people in Pruszków, and the arrangement is that the papers go to the staff in Warsaw in November through the church, and the whole of it is written down in three heads, and none of those three heads is mine."

"Good."

"And you are going to be in that city with no food and no water and eleven thousand German soldiers in it and the cold coming."

"I am going to be in that city with no food and no water and eleven thousand German soldiers in it and the cold coming," Phillip said, "and there are three hundred people going to be in it with me, and I am the only person in Warsaw who can read the administrative orders of the German garrison, and there are going to be about four hundred of them a week coming into that city on the wire that the engineers are going to run to the military commandant's office in November, and I am going to read them, and a copy of everything that matters is going to be in the hands of the people who are going to be in this city in the spring." He shifted the crutch under his arm. "I have told them that, and it is true, and it is also not the whole of the reason and you know the whole of the reason, and I am not going to say it at a fence with a column going past."

"Then do not say it."

"I am not going to say it," he said, in Polish, "and I am going to say the other thing instead, which is the answer to a question you asked me in a cemetery in May in front of a chapel with a rusted door, when you told me about a beet machine and a fitter from the Burakowska Street named Pan Ludwik, and I stood in an avenue with my cap in my hand and did not answer you." He put his hand flat on the mesh of the fence. "It did not matter to me that you were in that house for the work. I have known it since the fourteenth of December 1943 in a corridor with a mirror at the end of it, and the knowing has never once changed the arithmetic, which is that I have been in love with you since an afternoon in September 1938 in a library at Wierzbów when a girl of eighteen stood in a doorway and laughed at a boy of twenty-one who was talking to a shelf, and that this is a fact of the same kind as the fact that the river is on the east side of this city, and it is not a fact that has ever required a single sentence out of me, and I have said it now because if I do not say it at this fence, in front of a column, with the two of us alive, I shall be spending the winter deciding whether there was ever a moment at which it would have been possible, and I am not going to spend a winter that way."

There was a silence of about four seconds, with the cart wheels and the boots and the crying of children going past on the road eleven metres away.

"I will find you in the spring," said Phillip Brandt, in Polish, and then, in the same voice, in the same breath, he said her name, without a mask over it and without a German in earshot, twice: "Krystyna. Krystyna."

***

They went out of the city on the road to the west with the rest of it, and the whole of what the last three pages of that chapter have to contain is four things seen from inside a column of two hundred thousand people.

The first is the burning, seen from behind: because a person walking away from a burning city turns round, and turns round again, and the turning round is a thing that is done by about forty thousand people on that road in those days, and by the fourth hour of walking there is no longer any need to turn, because the smoke is in front of the walkers as well as behind them: it had come down over the whole of the western suburbs by the fourth of October and lay along the horizon in a brown band from the north to the south, and under it, at the distance of a kilometre, the outlines of the city were not outlines any more, and a woman with a handcart who was walking next to Krystyna on the second morning said, out loud, to nobody in particular, in Polish: "There is no roof over any of it and I cannot see the Palace of Culture because there is not going to be a Palace of Culture, and where is my house, because I cannot even find the place where my house is."

The second is the German soldiers, who are the thing that no account of that retreat has ever managed to make sense of: the columns of infantry going the other way, on the other side of the road, in the morning and the afternoon, in their hundreds and their thousands, out of the west and into the city that the people on the road were leaving, and the young ones looking at the Poles as the Poles looked at them, from a cart's length away, in the ordinary, tired, incurious way of two populations of tired men being moved about a continent by a system that did not require either of them to understand the other; and at the crossing at the Blizne, on the third day, a Gefreiter in a horse-drawn cart of the Wehrmacht handed a woman in the column a loaf of bread over the side of it, and did not say a word, and did not look at her, and drove on; and the woman stood in the middle of the road with the loaf in her hands for about four seconds, and then put it in her bundle, and it was eaten the same evening by eleven people, and it was never mentioned by any of them ever again.

The third is the silence of the column: that about two hundred thousand people walked out of a ruined capital in those eleven days, in families and streets, and that the sound of it was the sound of cart wheels and boots and children, and that the adults said nothing, not out of discipline and not out of grief, but because there is nothing to say in a language that has not got a word in it for what has happened; and that the one sentence that was said over and over again in that column, by women, to children, at the crossings, at the paper tables, at the doors of the barns, at the edges of roads, was the sentence that Poles had been saying to each other in that country since 1795, and it was: "Nothing. It is nothing. God will provide."

And the fourth is Hanna, asleep.

She was asleep on her sister's shoulder in a cart at the end of the second day, seventeen years old, in a green coat that was too small for her, with a piece of bread in her hand that she had been holding since the morning and had not eaten and would not put down; and her sister walked beside the cart with her hand on the tailboard, and the cart went on west, and the smoke stood up behind them over the roofs of the city of Warsaw in four separate columns, and the air was cold and smelled of the autumn and of the burning and of the mud of that road, and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna of Warsaw, twenty-four years old, who had been the mistress of a colonel of the German counter-intelligence in an occupied city for sixteen months and had killed him in a corridor four metres from his own bed, and had spent four years sending the paper of the German administration of Europe to a service in London on behalf of a government that was going to be abolished by other people's decisions in 1945, and who had carried the destruction of her own city out of that city in the lining of a coat given to her by a woman with a tape measure round her neck, walked west with her hand on the tailboard of the cart and did the arithmetic for the last time in the war, at eleven o'clock at night on the road to Pruszków, on the sixth of October 1944, and it came out as follows: that she was alive, that her sister was alive, that the papers were in the lining of the coat and the ring was on its cord round her neck, that her father was under a wall in a courtyard with a chalk anchor over the place, and that her brother was in the ground of the sewer of the city of Warsaw, and that the fourth rule of Wiktor Janowski, who had been shot at Mont-Valérien on the twelfth of April 1944 with twenty-two others, was the only rule of the four that mattered now, and that the rule was: *sleep*.

She slept two hours that night in a barn at the edge of a village, with her sister's head on her shoulder and the coat with the papers in it over both of them, and the whole of the sleep had a door in it that kept opening: a corridor with mirrors, and a girl of seventeen tying an armband above a girl's elbow, and a well in an orchard in Mazovia that stuck at eleven metres.

***

At the transit camp at Pruszków, on the eighth of October 1944, in a hall that had been a factory of the firm of the electrotechnical works of that town before the war and was now, by the administrative provision of the German military commandant of the district, the sorting of the population of the city of Warsaw, the two of them stood in a line that took four hours and eleven minutes to move forty metres, and at the head of the line there was a table, and at the table there was a German clerk of about fifty in a civilian coat with an armband of the administration, with a ledger and a rubber stamp and a box of food cards, and a sergeant of the Feldgendarmerie behind him with his arms folded, looking at nothing, in the way of a man who has been looking at nothing for three years.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Maria Nowak," said Krystyna Stankiewiczówna, in the Polish of the city of Warsaw, holding the hand of a green coat with her left hand, because a woman who has spent four years being an Austrian and one year being a German lady and eleven weeks being a Polish courier with a pseudonym on a card has learned at last the only piece of tradecraft that matters in the whole of the business, which is that the best name in the world is not the name of a dead woman and not the name of a living one, but the name of an unremarkable person who existed in the registers of the parish of a village near Radom in 1919 and who died in it in 1923 without anybody ever writing down that she had.

"Nowak, Maria. Age?"

"Twenty-four."

"Last place of residence in Warsaw?"

"The Mokotowska Street."

"Cards, two. Rations, four days. The transport for the west leaves at six; the sorting for the villages is on the left; the section for the families of soldiers of the Polish Home Army and the persons who were in the service of the Polish state is in the second shed and you are not to go into it; if you have papers of another country on you, you will declare them at the truck; if you have gold, you will be searched." The clerk stamped a card and pushed it across the table and did not look up from his ledger. "Next."

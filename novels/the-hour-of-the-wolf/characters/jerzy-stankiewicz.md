# Jerzy Stankiewicz

## Appearance

Sixty-three, thin as a lathe, white moustache trimmed with the last of his old pride. A face like an eagle gone to bone. He wears a good grey coat with the cuffs turned, a hat he blocks himself with a kettle, and boots whose soles have been rebuilt three times. He carries a leather satchel of books — his Latin grammars, a Horace, three volumes of Mickiewicz — as if it were a briefcase and he a man with somewhere to be.

## Personality & Traits

The ruined aristocrat done accurately, without self-pity and without generosity. Before the war he had a name, a wife, an estate in Mazovia and a town palace on Nowy Świat; when the crash came in 1932 he borrowed against all of it and lost, and spent the following seven years pretending to be a man of leisure while his wife sold the horses. He teaches Latin now — in a clandestine classroom on Żelazna Street, four boys and a stolen blackboard — for money that would not have bought his cigars in 1931. This is not redemption; it is what a man with no money and a good mind does with the war.

He is stern, formal, and vain in the small way of a man who owns nothing. He loves his children in the way of his class and his generation: with expectations and silences. He has never in his life apologised out loud.

## Motivations & Goals

**Primary:** Keep his family alive and his household's dignity intact — in that order, though he would say the reverse.
**Internal:** To have one thing left that the Germans have not touched
**Nightmare:** That his daughter's name will be spoken at his funeral as a shame

## Voice & Speech Patterns

Latin-tutor syntax: complete sentences, subordinate clauses, no slang. He calls his children *child* when he is angry and by their names when he is pleased. He asks questions in the negative — *You have not eaten?* — and expects them to be answered with facts.

> "There is no dishonour in poverty, child. There is only dishonour in being seen to mind it."

## Character Arc

**Starting state:** A proud man preserving the fiction of a house with a name.
**End state:** A dying man in a wrecked flat who asks for the daughter the city calls a German's woman and takes her hand. He does not say the word *forgive*. He asks her to read him one line of Horace, and then he says her name, and that is the whole of it.

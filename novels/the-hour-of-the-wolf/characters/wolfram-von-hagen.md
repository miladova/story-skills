# Wolfram von Hagen

## Appearance

Fifty-four. Tall, narrow, kept hard by riding and by nothing else. Grey at the temples and shaved close at the neck; a duelling scar from a Heidelberg winter on the left cheek that he has never had tattooed over or hidden. He dresses without vanity and expensively: the same tailor as before the war, the same boots made for him in Posen. His hands are the ugliest thing about him — big, dry, cracked across the knuckles — and they are what his mistress notices first.

He smells of cool tobacco, saddle soap, and very faintly of his late wife's lavender water, because the packing case of her things followed him from Paris two postings too late and he will not open it or throw it away.

## Personality & Traits

A Prussian career officer of the old reichswehr school: courteous, exacting, and entirely without sentiment about lower orders of all nationalities, including his own. He is not a shouter. He is a man who lowers his voice to ask a question and waits, and men answer him because of what the waiting implies.

He is genuinely talented at his work: he reads files the way a philologist reads a poem, and he has broken four intelligence networks for the Reich between 1940 and 1943. He does not believe in the Party's mysticism and he does believe in the Reich — a distinction he considers the mark of a gentleman, and which means nothing at all to the Poles he shoots.

With her he is a different creature, and he knows it and permits it: he likes the sound of her laugh, he is proud of being seen with her, and he talks to her in the dark in a way he has talked to no one since his wife. This is the crack in the wall. Everything the book does to him happens through it.

**Strengths:** A meticulous intelligence officer's suspicion; physical courage; patience; total self-command
**Flaws:** Possessiveness he mistakes for love; contempt he mistakes for knowledge; a capacity for cruelty that arrives without heat
**Do not:** give him a redemption. He gets one moment of clear sight in chapter 23 and it is too late and saves nobody.

## Motivations & Goals

**Primary:** Run the German counter-intelligence effort in Warsaw so that the Underground's intelligence war ends.
**Internal:** Since his son died, he has wanted one thing that is his.
**Nightmare:** Learning that she has made a fool of him — he would rather she were killed by someone else than be right

## Voice & Speech Patterns

Formal, economical, dry in a way that can read as humour and is not. He uses her name in the middle of sentences to remind her who is in the room. He gives orders in the form of observations: *The car will be here at six.* When he is angry he becomes more polite, in longer sentences, and he never swears above a murmur.

> "You would be surprised, my dear, how much a man says in bed. It is a great weakness of the sex, and I am not exempt."

## Physicality And The Bedroom

He is fifty-four and not a brute of a man; what she endures is not violence for its own sake but an appetite that has no interest in whether she shares it — hands, weight, the deliberateness of a man who has decided that this is his hour. He is also, occasionally, tender in ways that are worse: he holds her afterwards, he asks her about her childhood, he strokes her hair and asks what she is thinking and waits for an answer. The tenderness is the more dangerous instrument and the book should let the reader feel the trap of it.

He wants her to be the one person in his life who is not a calculation. That she is precisely a calculation is the novel's central irony, and neither of them can hold it in mind for long.

## Character Arc

**Starting state:** A widower-officer with a son in the ground and a very fine instrument for a mistress.
**End state:** A dead man in his own study, whose last words are her real name — the name he has just learned and cannot do anything with.

He is finished when he stops being a professional and starts being a jealous man; the book should show the transition, small and plausible, and let it cost him his life and the Reich nothing.

# The Hour of the Wolf

## Synopsis

**Paris, autumn 1943.** The officers of the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich eat oysters at the Ritz while Paris starves, and Colonel Wolfram von Hagen — fifty-four years old, a widower in everything but paperwork, a man who buried a son at Stalingrad — keeps a mistress in a requisitioned house on the avenue Henri-Martin. Her name, on the papers he signed himself, is Marion Herschlag: a Viennese singer, a divorcée, a woman who laughs at the right moments and forgets everything he tells her.

Her name is Krystyna Stankiewiczówna. She is twenty-four, the daughter of a Warsaw family that lost its money before the war and its country in September 1939, and she is the best instrument the Polish intelligence service in France has ever been given: a woman who is welcome inside the German administration because she is welcome in a German officer's bed. Everything she carries out of that house — the coastal batteries, the convoy schedules, the names of the men who write the orders — goes through a priest's hands on the rue Saint-Honoré and onward to London. She pays for it with her body, her conscience, and the last of her belief that there is anything left of the girl who used to ride at her father's estate.

The Germans have a file on her. They do not know her name, her face, or her nation; they call the leak **Fräulein**, and the officer who keeps the file — SS-Hauptsturmführer Bruno Kalbach — has begun to ask polite questions in drawing rooms.

Then, at a Christmas ball inside the Majestic, a young officer says her real name out loud. Phillip Brandt was twenty-two when the war separated them: the boy from the house on Hoża Street who taught her to skate, who kissed her once in the birches at Wierzbów, who came to her father's gate on the last day of the siege of Warsaw and was thrown out for speaking German. Now he is twenty-six, he wears the uniform, he works in Abwehr intelligence — and he is the only person alive who can put a rope around her throat.

She should kill him. Instead she lets him live, and that decision follows her east.

**Warsaw, spring 1944.** The Colonel is transferred to the Generalgouvernement to hunt the Polish Underground's intelligence networks, and he takes his mistress with him. Warsaw is a city of rubble, posters of the executed, black-market queues and clandestine schools, and Krystyna walks back into it wearing furs on the arm of the man who rules its garrison. Her father is dying in a flat on Mokotowska Street and believes she is dead; her brother Tadeusz, who knows the truth, can barely look at her; and the Home Army, which has use for her, does not have love for her.

In the daylight she is the Colonel's ornament, and at night she is the thing he does to her body — a possession, a punishment, a man who is certain he owns her. In the dark she meets Phillip in cold flats and ruined courtyards, and discovers the difference between appetite and love with a completeness that terrifies her: a slow fire, kindled out of six years of grief, that the war can end at any moment by killing them both.

The Underground is coming to the brink of revolt. Everything depends on knowing what the Germans will do in Warsaw when the Red Army reaches the Vistula — and the only person who can carry that knowledge out of the German command is a woman whose every intimacy is evidence against her, watched by a hunter who never stops writing things down.

**August, 1944.** The city rises. For sixty-three days, in cellars and canals and the shells of burned houses, Krystyna has to be four things at once: a spy with nothing left to steal, a daughter with no name to give, a soldier of a losing army, and a woman in love in a city on fire — while the Colonel hunts the woman who made a fool of him through streets he is destroying block by block.

## Tone & Style

A dark, close, sensual historical thriller: the pacing of espionage, the appetite of erotic drama, the gravity of a war novel. Intimacy is the battlefield and the prose should feel it — the Colonel's chapters are hot, bruising, material (leather, sweat, the weight of a body, the smell of schnapps); the chapters with Phillip are cooler and slower, lit by small flames, and the difference between the two is the argument of the book. In every case the body carries the meaning: dread sits in the stomach, shame in the throat, want in the hands.

Voice: close third person, past tense, one POV per chapter, locked tight. The narration is physical and unadorned, never explaining a feeling the body has already shown. Sentences are short under pressure and long only in memory. There is no wink at the reader and no explaining of history; period detail must arrive as furniture, not as a lecture — the tram that says *Nur für Deutsche*, the paper of the Kennkarte, the price of butter, the word for a roll. German and Polish appear sparingly and always in a mouth, never as a vocabulary list.

Content register: this is adult fiction and it is frank about sex as a weapon and as tenderness. The gratuity line is simple — every erotic beat must either advance the trap, deepen a character, or cost something. Nothing is described for texture alone.

Rules the book keeps:

- Krystyna never mentions her own feelings in narration for more than a clause. She is a professional liar; her interiority is the only place she tells the truth, and even there she edits.
- The Colonel is never explained, only observed. He is not a monster with a tragic past; he is a man with a dead son and an appetite, who has decided that owning her is the same as loving her.
- Phillip's love is shown through work and risk, not declarations. He proves himself by moving documents, taking beatings, standing in the wrong queue.
- The Underground is not a chorus of saints. It is an institution that asks for everything and protects itself, and it is right to.
- The war is not a backdrop. It is a pressure that keeps increasing: the front moves east, the reprisal posters multiply, and the reader should be able to feel the calendar.

## Structure

Three parts and an epilogue, each chapter five to six thousand words, each chapter a self-contained movement with its own hook.

- **Part One — The Instrument** (Chapters 1–7). Paris, November 1943 to February 1944. The life and its machinery; the leak called *Fräulein*; the appearance of Phillip; the tightening of the hunt; the transfer.
- **Part Two — The Mask** (Chapters 8–17). Warsaw, March to July 1944. The city under occupation, the family she cannot claim, the love that must be denied; the szmalcownik, the interrogation at aleja Szucha, the twentieth of July, the flood tide of the Uprising.
- **Part Three — The Fire** (Chapters 18–25). Warsaw, August to October 1944. The Rising, the slaughter in Wola, the Old Town, the canals, the Colonel's last night, the capitulation, the column out of the destroyed city.
- **Epilogue** — 1 August 1945. One year after the first shot.

POV distribution: Krystyna holds twenty-one chapters and the epilogue, Phillip three (11, 20, 24), and Bruno Kalbach one (15), so that the hunt is shown once from the hunter's own hand and the love never has to explain itself aloud.

## Historical Frame

The book is set on documented ground. Consult `plot/timeline.md` before drafting any scene that mentions an event, an address, or an order, and keep the following true:

- Paris: the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich rules from the Hôtel Majestic on the avenue Kléber; the Abwehr from the Hôtel Lutetia on the boulevard Raspail; the Sicherheitspolizei from 11 rue des Saussaies and 84 avenue Foch; the French Gestapo from the rue Lauriston. Deportation convoys leave from the Drancy camp. By the winter of 1943 the occupation's luxury and its terror have both become ordinary.
- February 1944: the Abwehr is dismantled after Canaris falls, its sections carved up between the RSHA and the OKW's Amt Mil, its officers scattered or arrested. Both von Hagen's and Phillip's transfers come out of that demolition; so does the suspicion that follows Phillip for the rest of the book.
- Warsaw: officially the capital of the Generalgouvernement's Warsaw district, in practice a city run by the German garrison command in the Saski Palace, the police on aleja Szucha, and the Sipo-SD. Piłsudski Square is Adolf-Hitler-Platz. The ghetto is a field of brick rubble after May 1943. Franz Kutschera, the SS- und Polizeiführer, was assassinated on 1 February 1944, and the reprisals that followed set the city's weather for the spring.
- The Polish Underground: the Home Army's counter-intelligence runs the German apparatus and is run through by it in equal measure; the Underground state keeps courts, schools, newspapers and its own forgery shops. Its agents die for small mistakes, and its files are kept by hand.
- 1 August 1944, 17:00: the Rising begins. The first week brings the murders in Wola; September brings the fall of the Old Town and the crossings through the sewers; 2 October brings the capitulation, the expulsion of the population, and the deliberate destruction of what is left of Warsaw. The people who hid in the rubble afterwards are remembered as the Robinson Crusoes of Warsaw.
- Do not invent a German victory, a rescue by the Allies, or an early Soviet arrival. The book ends inside history as it happened, and its hope has to be smaller and more expensive than that.

## Notes

- The title is taken from the hour between night and dawn when, by the old reckoning, most people die and some are born. Von Hagen keeps a private name for her that belongs to a lark; the Underground has a third. The book's last line answers all of them.
- The pearl necklace of chapter 1 is a leash and a ledger; keep track of where it goes.
- Nothing in the manuscript should ever reassure the reader that Krystyna will survive. She has to earn every page of it.

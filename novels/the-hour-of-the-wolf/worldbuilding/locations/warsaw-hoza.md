# The house on Hoża Street

A corner house of four floors two streets south of the Saxon Garden, with a gate that stands open all day because a caretaker with a broom is worth more than a lock, a courtyard with a walnut tree in the middle of it, and three rooms on the second floor belonging to the Brandts — the German engineer, his Polish wife, and their son — from 1922 until May 1940.

The house holds the life of the family before the war: the office at the end of the corridor with the drawing board and the rolled plans, Maria Brandt's small room with the photographs and the piano and the fern, the Sunday dinners, the boy's school books, and the firm's Christmas party on the staircase in 1935, which the neighbours still remember because the party ended on the stairs.

In May 1940 the house is requisitioned for an office of the labour section by the ordinary procedure — a valuation, fourteen days, a payment into a blocked account that can be seen and not touched — and the Brandts move to Wola. The Hoża Street stays open to them as a street: a window on the third floor of a building on it is a Home Army signal that the street is quiet; a German officer who walks it twice in thirty days is written down in a notebook in an office on Aleja Szucha; and it is the pavement of the Hoża Street on the fourth of June 1944 on which a patrol of the Sicherheitspolizei is made to watch a Wehrmacht officer shout at a woman, and goes round the corner and loses the afternoon.

# The house on Hoża Street

A corner house of four floors two streets south of the Saxon Garden, with a gate that stands open all day because a caretaker with a broom is worth more than a lock, a courtyard with a walnut tree in the middle of it, and a flat belonging to the Brandts — the German engineer, his Polish wife and their son — until May 1940.

The house holds the life of the family before the war: the office at the end of the corridor with the drawing board and the rolled plans, Maria Brandt's small room with the photographs and the piano and the fern, the boy's school books, and the firm's parties on the staircase, which the neighbours remembered for years because the parties ended on the stairs.

In May 1940 the house is requisitioned for an office of the labour section by the ordinary procedure — a valuation, fourteen days, a payment into a blocked account that can be seen and not touched — and the Brandts move to Wola. The street stays open to them as a street: a light in the window of the third floor of a building on it is a Home Army signal that the street is quiet; a German officer who walks it twice in thirty days is written down in a notebook in an office on Aleja Szucha; the pavement of it is where a patrol of the Sicherheitspolizei is made to watch a Wehrmacht officer shout at a woman, and goes round the corner, and loses an afternoon; and in the ruins of November 1944 it is in the flat of a woman of this street that the last of the book's men is arrested.

# Style Sheet

The book's house decisions. Read this before drafting or revising prose.

## Voice

Close third, past tense, locked to one head per chapter. Physical before emotional: the body reports first and the mind catches up a paragraph later. Short sentences when the danger is close; longer ones only in memory or in tenderness. No rhetorical questions, no addressing the reader, no summarising a scene the reader has just read.

> The sentry's cigarette went out and he lit it again, and she counted the seconds between the two lights.

## Spelling And Usage

British spelling: *grey*, *colour*, *honour*, *realise*, *defence*, *travelling*, *marvellous*, *practised*. House exception: *toward*, never *towards*. German and Polish words take English plurals unless they are titles or ranks: *two Wehrmacht lorries*, *the officers' mess*, but *Oberst*, *Hauptmann*, *Hauptsturmführer*, *Kennkarte*, *szmalcownik* keep their own form. Place names: *the avenue Henri-Martin*, *aleja Szucha*, *Nowy Świat*, *Marszałkowska*. Italicise foreign words only on first use, and never italicise a proper name.

## Capitalisation

*the Colonel* and *the Captain* when they are what a character would call them; *von Hagen* and *Brandt* in narration. *the Reich*, *the Party* — lower case *party* when it is not the Nazi party. Institutions keep their capitals: *the Home Army*, *the Underground state*, *the Sicherheitspolizei*, *the Generalgouvernement*. *the ghetto* is lower case.

## Numbers, Dates, And Time

Spell out numbers up to one hundred in prose. Dates are given as a person would say them: *the first of August*, *the twenty-third of December*. Clocks matter — this is a book about timing — so times are set in figures or words as a character would think them, and every chapter names its date in the first two pages.

## Dialogue And Punctuation

Double quotation marks. Closed em dashes for interruption and for asides in narration. *Said* and *asked* only, plus the occasional *whispered* or *shouted* where the volume is the point. German spoken by Germans is rendered in English with an occasional German word that the POV character would notice. Polish with Phillip and with her family: she thinks in Polish when she is frightened, and the text says so only by what she hears.

## Character Voices

- **Krystyna** — exact, watchful, dry; her spoken register is a performance and changes with her audience. To Germans: musical, self-deprecating, a little slow. To Poles: clipped, ironic. To Phillip: honest, which is why she is dangerous to herself.
- **Von Hagen** — meticulous, courteous, uses her name often, asks questions in the form of statements. Never raises his voice; raises his vocabulary instead.
- **Phillip** — careful, precise, an interpreter's habit of restating. His warmth shows in questions about small things. Speaks Polish like a man who learned it in a kitchen and German like a man who learned it in a school.
- **Kalbach** — quiet, exact, exhaustive; pronoun-free sentences; he says *we* when he means *I*.
- **Tadeusz** — blunt, wounded, barracks humour, swears in Polish.
- **Jerzy Stankiewicz** — the syntax of a man who taught Latin, even when he has no money for bread.

## Watch List

- *almost* — one per chapter at most.
- *something* — replace with the concrete thing the POV character can actually see or hear.
- *barely*, *just* — cut unless a character's voice needs the slackness.

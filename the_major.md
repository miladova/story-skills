# The Major

*Warsaw, 1943*

It was dark in the Ghetto’s alleys, and Marianna Frontczak pulled her daughter Kaja’s coat tighter as they stepped out of the Ghetto toward the edge of the district where the shops still had lights on. Forty-two, she had spent twenty years learning the city before she had learned how to speak it; she counted every step back to the gate.

Then a man walked toward her out of the gray. A Wehrmacht major — the insignia on his collar was dark against the cold light, and his face was clean-shaven and tired. He did not turn his back on her; the world between them narrowed to one face.

She knew that face. It was him. The man she had loved before the war — when Warsaw was warm and full of names — she had walked home with him past the same streetlamps she now passes.

"Marianna?" he asked — his voice, when it came, carried the same slight accent she remembered, and the same distance.

Her daughter’s cry cut across the question.

"Mama — that's—" Kaja pointed at him, her voice shaky, her hand to her mouth. "That's the Major!"

Marianna’s breath came out in a slow cloud in the cold air. She remembered his hand, the way he had held hers at the theater; she remembered walking home together after an air raid — they had walked under the same stars she was walking under now.

He looked at Kaja — the new child — and then back at Marianna. "I do not think we have met," he said, smiling a little; he reached for her arm.

The smile made her knees shake. "Marianna Frontczak, née Tarnowska," she said, her voice steady.

He took her hand — and for a moment she felt the warmth of it, the same as before.

"And I am Major Oskar," he said.

The name fell between them like a stone dropped into cold water. She pulled Kaja back toward the gate; the Major stepped away, and the alley swallowed him as they walked on — the cold, the dark, the gate behind them.

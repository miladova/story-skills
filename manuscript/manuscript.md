# Chapter One: The Funeral of Brunon Kowalik

The crane cable had been frayed for months. The workers said so afterward, in the queue outside the factory gate, in the tavern on Brzeska Street, in the confessional of the Church of the Transfiguration, where a man can say things he would not say to his wife. The report said that Brunon Kowalik, crane operator, forty-two years old, had failed to inspect the cable before the morning shift. The report was typed on a single sheet of paper, signed by the factory director, stamped by the personnel office, and placed in a file that would never be opened again. The workers said the cable had been reported in June. They said the report had been lost. They said the crane had been scheduled for repair in the spring. The spring had come and gone. The cable had held until it did not.

Halina Sowa read the report in the back of a black FSO Warszawa as it crossed the Śląsko-Dąbrowski Bridge. The car was one of the small fleet assigned to the Central Committee, with a driver named Wiesław and a heater that made a sound like a man clearing his throat. The Vistula below was the color of dishwater. On the far bank, the towers of Praga stood in a gray mist, the church of St. Florian with its dark brick, the smaller roofs, the chimneys. The Palace of Culture was behind her, its silhouette in the rearview mirror, a needle stuck into the sky.

She read the report twice. Then she put it in her briefcase and looked out the window. The city was not beautiful in late October. It was a city of patched facades and empty lots, of new apartment blocks with windows like dark eyes, of trams that groaned around the corners. The war had been over for thirteen years, and still the gaps were there. Some had been filled with lawns and benches. Some had been left to the weeds. Some had been built on with the crude confidence of the new state. Halina had grown up in a village where the only stone building was the church. Now she lived in a city of stone, and she did not believe in the church.

"Comrade Sowa," Wiesław said. "The church is just ahead."

"I know."

"The workers are already inside. There's a crowd."

"I know."

He slowed the car into a narrow street of chestnut trees that had lost their leaves. On the corner a militiaman in a green cap stood with his hands behind his back watching the traffic, and a second one was writing something in a notebook, and they both turned to look at the Warszawa as it went past. The Church of the Transfiguration stood at the end of the street: baroque, with a tall tower and a facade the colour of old cream, and a darker patch of tile on the presbytery roof where a bomb had taken it off in 1944 and the parish had put it back. The church had survived. The parish had survived. The priest had survived. That was what the file said, and the file, for once, was accurate.

Halina stepped out of the car. The air was cold and smelled of coal smoke and wet leaves. She wore a black suit, a black coat, and a black hat with a small veil. She had dressed for a funeral, not for a meeting. She was not here to meet anyone. She was here to observe.

The church was full. The women sat in the front pews, their heads covered with dark scarves. The men stood at the back, in caps and work jackets, their hands folded in front of them. The children were quiet. The coffin stood on trestles in the center aisle, covered with a black cloth and a spray of chrysanthemums. The flowers were the color of rust. A portrait of Brunon Kowalik leaned against the coffin, a black-and-white photograph of a man with a heavy jaw and a half-smile, as if he had been caught between two thoughts.

Halina stood at the back, near the holy water font. She did not dip her hand. She had not touched holy water since she was a girl. She had not been in a church for anything but a state funeral in years. She knew the ritual. She knew when to stand and when to sit and when to lower her head. She had been raised on it. She could perform it the way a person can perform a song in a language they no longer speak.

The sacristan lit the candles. The organ played a low note, then a chord. The choir was small, mostly old women, and their voices were thin in the cold air. The priest came out of the sacristy.

Halina picked him out before she properly recognized him. He was tall, broad-shouldered, in a black cassock that was slightly too short at the ankles. He walked with his head down, his hands joined in front of him. His hair was dark, cut close. There was a scar above his left eyebrow, a pale line like a scratch in wood. He stopped at the coffin. He made the sign of the cross. He looked up.

Their eyes met.

For a moment it was not the priest in the pulpit at all but a boy of sixteen standing by the river with a fishing rod, a boy who could imitate the priest's cough so perfectly that the old women laughed into their hymnals, a boy who had kissed her under the birch tree and said, "I will love you until I am an old man." She saw the boy who had been arrested by the Gestapo and had come back with a scar and a silence. She saw the seminarian in a black cassock, in the village church in 1944, looking at her across the nave as if he had seen a ghost. She saw the man in the black cassock, the priest, the stranger.

He looked away first. He began the Mass.

The Latin was old and worn, like the stone of the church. Halina did not translate it in her mind. She listened to the sound of it, the rise and fall of the priest's voice, the responses of the congregation. She had learned the Latin as a child. She had loved the sound of it then. She had believed that the words were a door, and that behind the door was something bright. She had stopped believing in the door. But the sound still moved her. It was the sound of her childhood, of the village, of her mother's voice in the evening. She hated it a little.

The priest read the Gospel and stood in the pulpit with his hands flat on the wood, not looking at his notes. He looked at the congregation instead: the widow in the first pew, the children beside her, the workers standing at the back in their caps. He looked at Halina for a moment, and away.

"My friends," he said. "We are here to bury a man. We are not here to judge him. That is God's work, not ours. But we are here to ask a question. Why did he die?"

The church was silent.

"Brunon Kowalik was a crane operator. He worked at the Zakłady Mechaniczne for nineteen years. He was forty-two years old. He had a wife and three children. He was a good worker. He was a good man. He died because a cable broke."

Halina felt her chest tighten. She looked at the priest's face and understood that he was not shouting, and that his voice was low and even, and that he was not a demagogue working a crowd but a man stating a fact to a room full of people who had known the fact for years and had never heard it said aloud in church.

"The report says he was careless," the priest said. "The report says he failed to inspect the cable. I have read the report. I have also read the letter he wrote to the factory in June, asking for the cable to be replaced. I have spoken to the men who worked with him. They say the cable was frayed. They say the report was lost. They say the crane was scheduled for repair. They say the repair did not come."

A man at the back said, "That's true." His voice was rough. The priest waited until the man sat down.

"I am not a politician," the priest said. "I am not here to make a speech against the government. I am here to bury Brunon Kowalik and to ask why he died. The Gospel tells us that a man's life is worth more than the whole world. The Gospel does not say that a man's life is worth the plan. It does not say that a man's life is worth the quota. It does not say that a man's life is worth the report that says he was careless."

There were two men from the security service near the door: one writing in a notebook, the other watching the priest with a flat expression. She knew that expression. She had worn it herself.

"Brunon Kowalik was a worker," the priest said. "He was not a hero. He was not a martyr. He was a man who got up in the dark and went to work and came home in the dark and ate his dinner and played with his children. He was a man who loved his wife. He was a man who feared God, in his own way. He was a man who deserved to grow old."

The widow put her hand to her mouth. The children looked at her.

"The Gospel says that the last shall be first," the priest said. "It does not say that the last shall be a line in a report. It does not say that the last shall be a statistic. It does not say that the last shall be a number in a file that is closed and put in a drawer. The Gospel says that the last shall be first. If we believe that, then we cannot close the file. If we believe that, then we cannot say that Brunon Kowalik was careless. We cannot say that his death was the price of progress. Progress is not a god. It is a tool. A tool that kills its worker is not progress. It is a sacrifice. And we have had enough of sacrifices."

The church was very quiet. Halina could hear the candles hiss, and the traffic outside, and a tram bell, and somewhere at the back a man coughing into his hat, and her own heart going like a fist on a door.

The priest looked at the coffin. "Brunon," he said, "I did not know you. But I know your wife. I know your children. I know the men who worked with you. I know that you were not careless. I know that you were afraid. I know that you went to work anyway. That is what courage looks like. It is not a speech. It is a man getting up in the dark. May God forgive us for the times we have called that man careless. May God forgive us for the times we have closed the file."

He made the sign of the cross over the coffin. The organ began to play. The choir sang. Halina did not sing. She stood with her hands at her sides and looked at the priest. He was not the boy she had known. He was not the man she had imagined. He was a stranger with a scar and a voice that could make a room full of tired workers believe, for a moment, that their lives mattered.

She had spent twenty years learning how to make people believe things, and it had left her with an ear for it: she could tell when a speaker was lying, and when he was telling the truth, and when he was dangerous. The priest was dangerous.

After the Mass, the coffin was carried out into the street. The workers lifted it on their shoulders. The widow followed, her children holding her hands. The crowd moved toward the cemetery. Halina did not go to the cemetery. She stood in the churchyard, under the bare chestnut trees, and waited.

The rain began while she was standing there: a thin cold rain of the sort that gets into the bones and stays for the winter. She pulled her coat tighter. Wiesław had come round from the driver's side with an umbrella, and she waved him away, and he went back and stood by the car with the umbrella over himself and his collar up, and did not look at her.

The priest came out of the church twenty minutes later. He had removed his vestments. He wore the black cassock and a dark overcoat. He stopped at the top of the steps when he saw her.

"Halka," he said.

The name went through her like a needle. Nobody had called her that in years. Her mother had called her Halinka; her colleagues called her Comrade Sowa; the Party called her comrade, in the flat way the Party used when it wanted you to know it had a card with your name on it. The only person who had ever called her Halka was the boy who had kissed her under the birch tree, and he had been dead, as far as she was concerned, since about 1945.

"Ksiądz," she said.

The word was a door closing. He understood it and did not argue with it, and came down the steps towards her, and she saw that he was taller than she remembered, and leaner in the face, and that the scar above his eyebrow showed white in the grey light.

"You came," he said.

"I came for the funeral."

"You came for me."

She did not deny it. "I came for the funeral," she said. "I also came for you."

He looked at the wet street. "I saw you at the back of the church. I thought I was imagining it."

"You were not."

"Twenty years," he said. "Almost twenty."

"Nineteen," she said. "Since the autumn of 1939. We were seventeen."

"You remember the year."

"I remember everything."

He looked at her. His eyes were dark, with a tiredness that had not been there when he was a boy. "So do I," he said. "That is the problem."

The rain fell on the church steps. The bell in the tower rang the Angelus, a slow, bronze sound. Halina did not bow her head. Neither did he. They stood there, two people who had once been children in the same village, who had once loved each other with the absolute certainty of children, who had been separated by a war and an ideology and a river of years.

"I read your sermons," she said.

"I know. The Department keeps a file."

"You know about the file?"

"I know about the file."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you say you're here. I don't know why you're really here."

She almost smiled. "You always did ask the wrong questions."

"And you always did answer them with another question."

"I'm not a girl anymore, Janusz."

"No," he said. "You're not."

The use of his name was deliberate, and she watched him flinch. She had not called him by his first name in nineteen years, and it felt strange in her mouth, like a word in a language she had stopped speaking on purpose.

"The accident at the factory," she said. "The cable. The report. I'm here to investigate."

"You're here to manage it."

"I'm here to investigate."

"Is there a difference?"

"There is always a difference."

He looked at the church, at the people coming out, at the widow getting into a car. "The report is a lie," he said. "The cable was frayed. The workers reported it. The factory knew. Brunon Kowalik is dead because someone decided that the plan was more important than his life. You can put that in your report."

"I don't write reports like that."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I was told to come."

"By whom?"

"By the people who tell me to do things."

He nodded. "And what do they want you to do?"

"They want me to find out if you are a problem."

He smiled. It was not a warm smile. "And what do you think?"

"I think you are a problem."

"Good," he said. "Then we understand each other."

A black car turned the corner and slowed. Halina saw the two men from the security service in the front seat. The car stopped across the street. The driver did not get out.

"I have to go," she said.

"I know."

"Janusz."

He waited.

"I'm sorry about the man," she said. "Brunon Kowalik. I'm sorry."

"So am I."

She turned and walked to the car, and Wiesław opened the door for her, and she got in without looking back, and felt him on the steps behind her the whole way — the church, the tower, the dark windows, the altar where he had said the Mass; and behind all of that the village, and the river, and the birch tree, and the boy.

In the car she opened the briefcase and took out the report and read it again, and the words were the same words they had been that morning. The cable was not in it. The workers were not in it. The letter from June was not in it. It was a clean report, and it was a lie, and those two things had never seemed to her so exactly the same size.

She put it back in the briefcase and closed it. The car crossed the bridge. The Palace of Culture was ahead, its windows lit against the gray sky. Halina thought about the sermon, and about the line that had gone through the church like a struck bell: *We have had enough of sacrifices.* It was a dangerous line. It was a true line. It was the kind of line that could bring a man to the attention of the Central Committee. It was the kind of line that could end a career.

She had a career, and a flat on Marszałkowska, and a driver, and an office in the Central Committee with her name on the door and a file of her own in the registry downstairs. She had learned how to survive, which was the first thing she had ever been good at. She had learned that the truth was a luxury and that a lie was a currency, and that the state was not a monster but a machine, and that a machine does not hate anyone; it simply does not care, and will process you as it processes everything else, in order, at the speed of the queue.

But the priest's voice had been different. It had not been the voice of a machine; it had been the voice of a man who believed that a single life mattered more than a plan, and she had heard that voice before, in the village, in the forest, in the dark, and had believed it then too. She had heard it in her own chest, before she had learned to silence it.

The car stopped at a red light on Marszałkowska and Halina looked out of the window at a woman crossing in front of them with a child, and the child was holding a red balloon on a string, and the balloon bobbed in the wind and pulled at the child's hand, and the light changed, and the car moved.

Halina thought about the boy who had kissed her under the birch tree. He had been sixteen. He had been beautiful in the way that a boy is beautiful when he does not know it. He had told her that he wanted to be a priest. She had laughed. She had told him that priests were old men in black dresses. He had said, "Not all of them." She had said, "You'll be a terrible priest. You'll tell the bishop what you really think." He had said, "Maybe that's what the Church needs." She had said, "The Church doesn't need you. I do." He had kissed her. She had believed him.

She had been wrong about everything. She had been wrong about the Church. She had been wrong about the Party. She had been wrong about the world. But she had not been wrong about the boy. He had become a priest who told the truth. He had become a man who stood in a cold church and said that a worker's life mattered. He had become a problem.

The car reached the Central Committee. Wiesław stopped at the gate. Halina sat for a moment. The building was gray, with rows of windows. A red flag hung from the roof, limp in the rain. She thought about going in. She thought about her office, her desk, her telephone, her files. She thought about the report she would write. She thought about the line she would have to cross.

She got out and the guard saluted and she went in through the tall doors into a corridor that smelled of floor wax and cigarette smoke, under the portraits of men who had been dead a long time and were still, in the way of such portraits, watching the corridor.

At her office, she took off her coat and hat. She sat at her desk. She took out a sheet of paper. She wrote the date: 27 October 1958. She wrote the title: "Report on the Funeral of Brunon Kowalik." She wrote: "The funeral was held at the Church of the Transfiguration, Praga-Północ, at 10:00 a.m. The church was full. The congregation consisted mainly of workers from the Zakłady Mechaniczne and their families. The priest, Janusz Wilga, delivered a sermon that was critical of the factory administration and, by implication, of the Party's economic policy. The sermon was received with sympathy by the congregation. Two officers of the security service were present. No incidents were reported."

She stopped and read it through. It was accurate, and it was a lie, and the whole trick of the thing was in the gap between those two facts. It did not say that the sermon had made her feel something. It did not say that the priest was the boy she had loved, or that the cable was frayed, or that the report she had read in the car that morning was a lie. It did not say that she had stood in the rain and spoken to him. It did not say that he had called her Halka.

She put the report in a folder and the folder in her drawer and locked it, and then sat at her desk with her hands flat on the blotter and looked at the wall for a while.

The wall was bare. There was a portrait of Marx on the wall of the office next door. In her office, there was nothing. She had taken down the portrait years ago. She had told herself that it was because the wall was damp. The truth was that she could not stand the eyes.

She heard a knock at the door.

"Come in," she said.

The door opened. It was Rydel.

He was wearing a gray suit and a gray tie. He had a soft face, with heavy eyelids and a small mouth. He looked like a professor who had been asked to teach a class he did not believe in. He closed the door behind him.

"Halina," he said. "I hear you went to the funeral."

"I went to the funeral."

"Good. Did you learn anything?"

"I learned that the priest is a problem."

Rydel smiled. He took a cigarette from his pocket and lit it. He did not offer her one. He knew she did not smoke in her office.

"Then we have something to talk about," he said.

He sat down in the chair across from her desk. He did not take off his coat. He looked at the bare wall behind her, as if he were looking for something that was not there.

"Tell me about him," he said.

Halina looked at the report in the drawer, and thought about the boy by the river, and the man on the church steps, and the line in the sermon that had gone through her like a nail: *We have had enough of sacrifices.*

"He is a priest," she said. "He is young. He is popular with the workers. He is ambitious. He is a candidate for the episcopate. And he is in love with me."

Rydel raised an eyebrow. "In love with you?"

"That is what the file says," she said. "The file is usually right."

"The file is usually right," Rydel said. "But it is not always complete."

He leaned forward. He put his cigarette out in the ashtray. He looked at her with his heavy eyes.

"Halina," he said, "I have a job for you."

# Chapter Two: The File

The alarm went off at six-thirty. Halina reached for it in the dark and knocked it off the nightstand. It landed on the wooden floor with a crash that sounded like a shot. She lay still for a moment, her heart beating fast. Then she got up.

The apartment was on Marszałkowska, on the fourth floor of a building that had been put up in the early fifties. It had two rooms, a kitchen, and a bathroom with a hot-water heater that worked three days out of seven. The windows faced east. In the morning, the light came through the thin curtains and made a pale rectangle on the wall. From the window, she could see the Palace of Culture, its spire like a needle, and the rooftops of the city, and the smoke rising from the chimneys.

She had lived here six years and had never hung a picture. The furniture was the standard issue of the housing office — a bed, a wardrobe, a table, two chairs, a bookcase — and the bookcase held Marx and Lenin and the collected speeches of the First Secretary and four novels that had come to her in the way such things came: a gift from a delegation, a prize for a course, a book left behind by a man who had not stayed the night. Behind the pamphlets on the bottom shelf, out of sight and out of habit, was a copy of *The Brothers Karamazov* that she had bought in a secondhand shop on Nowy Świat in 1952 and could not have explained to a single person in her building. She did not believe in God. She did not believe in the soul. She read the novel every winter, the way other people took a tonic.

She went to the bathroom and washed her face. The water was cold. She looked at herself in the mirror. She was thirty-six. She had a good face, she thought, not beautiful, but strong. Her hair was ash-blonde, cut short. Her eyes were gray. There was a line between her eyebrows that had appeared in the last two years. She had a habit of pressing her lips together when she was thinking. She did not look like a woman who had been in love with a priest. She looked like a woman who worked in an office.

She made coffee on the gas ring. The coffee was ersatz, made from roasted barley, with a spoonful of real coffee on top. She drank it standing by the window. The radio was on. The announcer read the news in a voice that was calm and certain. The seven-year plan was being drafted. The workers of the Stalin Steelworks had exceeded their quota. The American imperialists were continuing their aggression in the Middle East. The Catholic bishops had issued a statement. The announcer did not say what the statement was. He said it was "a matter of internal Church affairs." Halina knew what it was. The Jasna Góra raid. The Cardinal had called it a violation of the law. The Party had called it a routine inspection. The truth was a matter of vocabulary.

She turned off the radio. She went to the bedroom and opened the wardrobe. Her clothes were arranged by color: black, gray, dark blue. She took out a dark gray suit, a white blouse, and a pair of low-heeled shoes. She dressed carefully. She put on a small gold watch. She looked at herself in the mirror. She looked like a comrade. That was the point.

On the top shelf of the wardrobe, behind a stack of scarves, there was a wooden box. She had not opened it in months. She reached up and took it down. The box was small, made of dark wood, with a brass latch. Inside there was a photograph of a boy in a white shirt, a girl with a braid, and a wooden cross. The boy was Tadeusz. He was seventeen in the photograph. He had a wide grin and a cowlick. The girl was Halina. She was sixteen. She was not smiling. The cross was her mother's. It had been blessed by the priest in the village. Halina did not believe in the blessing. But she had kept the cross.

She looked at the photograph for as long as she could stand to, which was about a minute, and then put it back in the box and the box back on the shelf and shut the wardrobe door.

The driver was waiting at the entrance. He was a young man with a round face and a permanent cold. He held the door open for her. She got in. The car smelled of gasoline and wet wool. The heater was broken again.

"Rakowiecka," she said.

"Yes, comrade."

The city passed outside the window: women going to work with their shopping bags over their arms, men in caps waiting for the tram, a milk truck, a woman carrying a child on her hip because there was nowhere else to put the child. It was gray and it was alive. She had grown up in a village where the street was empty after dark, and Warsaw was never empty; it was a city of people who had survived, and they walked with their heads down, and she had decided some years ago that they were not looking for anything they had lost but keeping their eyes on the pavement out of a habit the war had taught them, and she did it too, and did not think it was the same thing.

The Ministry was a gray building on Rakowiecka behind a long driveway and a gate, and the guard at the gate looked at her identity card for exactly as long as it took to decide she was not going to be any trouble. The car stopped at the entrance. Inside, the building smelled of disinfectant and boiled cabbage and something underneath them both that she had never been able to name — not fear, exactly; the place where fear had been and gone and left its coat. The corridors were wide and empty and the portraits were of men in uniform, and the lift was 1930s, with a brass gate that creaked as it rose.

Rydel's office was on the third floor. The door was open. He was sitting at his desk, reading a newspaper. He looked up when she came in. He did not get up.

"Halina," he said. "Sit down."

She sat down. The chair was hard and too low for the desk, which was a thing she noticed because it was the sort of thing she noticed, and the office was larger than hers, with a map of Poland on the wall and a photograph of a girl of about twelve in a frame on the desk, with a ribbon in her hair and a smile for whoever was holding the camera.

"Your daughter?" Halina asked.

Rydel glanced at the photograph. "Yes. Agnieszka. She is at school."

"She looks like you."

"She has her mother's face," he said. "God rest her."

He put down the newspaper. He folded his hands on the desk. He looked at her with his heavy eyes. He had a soft voice, a soft face, and a soft way of moving. He was the most dangerous man she knew.

"I asked you to come because we have a problem," he said. "The problem is a priest. Janusz Wilga. The rector of the Church of the Transfiguration in Praga. You know him."

"I know him."

"You knew him. Before the war."

"I knew him before the war."

"He is a candidate for the episcopate. The Primate likes him. The Vatican is considering him. He is young, he is popular, and he is not ours. We cannot allow him to become a bishop. A bishop is a prince of the Church. A bishop has a voice. A bishop can make trouble for twenty years. We need to stop him."

"And what do you want me to do?"

"I want you to talk to him. I want you to persuade him to cooperate with us. There is a commission of priests that has agreed to work with the government. It is called the Commission of Priests. It is a patriotic organization. It supports the People's Republic. It opposes the reactionary elements in the Church. We want him to join."

"He won't."

"He might. If the right person asks him."

She understood. "You want me to ask him."

"You are the right person."

"I haven't seen him in nineteen years."

"You saw him yesterday."

She did not ask how he knew. The security service had been at the funeral; they had seen her on the church steps and had probably photographed her and had certainly written a note about it, and she had said nothing that could be used against her, because she had been careful since 1945. But Rydel did not need the funeral. He did not need words at all. He had the past, which was cheaper than a microphone and never went out of date.

"There is something else," he said.

He opened a drawer and took out a thin gray folder and put it on the desk between them. It had a red stamp on it, *Tajne*, and a number in the corner, and he did not open it, because the folder was not information; it was furniture, and he wanted her to sit and look at it.

"This is a copy of a file," he said. "It is a file we have had for fifteen years. It concerns Janusz Wilga. It concerns you. It concerns your brother."

Halina felt a coldness in her stomach. "My brother?"

"Open it."

She opened the folder.

The first page was a Gestapo report typed in German with a Polish translation attached, on paper thin enough to read the watermark through. The stamp at the top was the black eagle and the swastika. She had seen that stamp before: on the papers that had come after her father was taken to Germany in 1939, and on the notice that had ordered the Jews of the village into the square, and once, for a reason nobody had ever explained, on the door of the church. She had not known, until she turned the page and saw it again, that a stamp could live in a person's body for fifteen years and come up at the sight of it, like a scar in cold weather.

She read the translation.

"On 12 August 1943, at 4:30 a.m., the criminal investigation police arrested the Pole Janusz Wilga, born 22 November 1922 in Brzóza, son of the blacksmith Franciszek Wilga, on suspicion of activity for the communist bandit organization. During interrogation, the accused confessed that he was a courier for the organization. He gave the location of a hideout in the forest near Brzóza, approximately three kilometers from the village, used by the bandit group. The hideout was raided on 14 August 1943. Three bandits were killed. Among them was Tadeusz Kowal, born 4 March 1926, brother of the communist functionary Halina Kowal. The accused was released on 20 August 1943, as he was considered a minor figure."

The paper blurred. Halina read it again. She read it a third time. The words did not change.

She looked at Rydel. "He gave them the location."

"He gave them the location," Rydel said. "Under interrogation. The Gestapo had a way of asking questions."

"He was tortured."

"He was tortured. But the result was the same. Your brother is dead. The priest is alive. He has been a priest for ten years. He has heard confessions. He has given communion. He has buried the dead. He has never told anyone what he did. He has never been punished. He is about to become a bishop."

Halina put the paper down. Her hands were steady. She had learned to keep her hands steady. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because I want you to understand the kind of man he is."

"He was a boy."

"He was a man. He was twenty. He made a choice. He gave them the hideout. He killed your brother."

"He didn't know Tadeusz was there."

"He knew someone was there. He knew the hideout was used by the AL. He knew the Gestapo would raid it. He gave them the location. That is all that matters."

Halina looked at the folder. There was a second page. She reached for it. Rydel put his hand on it.

"Not yet," he said.

"Let me see it."

"You will see it. But first, I want you to understand something. The file is not complete. There is another report. It is from 1945. It is a report from the Urząd Bezpieczeństwa. It is about a man named Berezowski. It is signed by you."

Halina's mouth went dry. "That file is closed."

"No file is ever closed," Rydel said. "You know that. You have worked in the Party long enough to know that. The file is closed when we say it is closed. It is open when we need it to be open."

He lifted his hand. She took the second page.

The paper was a thin carbon copy. The type was faint. At the bottom, in blue ink, there was a signature: *H. Kowal*. The signature was hers. She had written it when she was twenty-three. She had written it in a small office in Lublin, with a glass of water on the desk and a man from the security service standing behind her. She had written it because she was afraid. She had written it because she wanted to save Janusz. She had written it because she wanted to save herself.

The report said that "the named person, Janusz Wilga, son of Franciszek, was a member of the anti-state organization Armia Krajowa, pseudonym Sosna. He is to be regarded as a dangerous element. The information was provided by the informant H. Kowal, who has proven reliable in the past. In addition, the named person, Berezowski, pseudonym Bór, was the commander of the unit to which Wilga belonged. Berezowski is to be arrested and interrogated."

Halina read the words and did not remember writing them, and remembered everything else: the office, the man behind her, the glass of water she had not drunk, and the arithmetic she had done in her head at seventeen minutes past four on a February afternoon in 1945 — *I will give them Berezowski. I will not give them Janusz. They will let him go. They will never find Berezowski. It will be all right* — which had been correct in two places out of four.

Berezowski had been arrested three days later. He had been interrogated for six weeks. He had been shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. Halina had heard about it from a friend. She had not asked for details. She had never spoken of it again.

She had killed a man. She had killed him to save another man. She had killed him because she was afraid. She had killed him because she was young and believed that the revolution was worth a life. She had killed him because she had not known what death was.

She looked up at Rydel. "You have kept this for fifteen years."

"I have kept this for fifteen years."

"Why?"

"Because it is useful. Because you are useful. Because one day I might need you to do something that you would not otherwise do."

"And now you need me."

"Now I need you."

He stood up. He walked around the desk. He stood by the window, with his back to her. The light from the window made his gray suit look almost white.

"Halina," he said. "I am not a monster. I know what it is like to be afraid. I was in a Sanacja prison before the war. I was in the Soviet Union during the war. I have seen what happens to people who do not cooperate. I have seen what happens to people who do. I am trying to keep this country from falling apart. The Church is a power. The Party is a power. They have to live together. The priest is a threat to that balance. If he becomes a bishop, he will use his power against us. He will preach against us. He will organize the workers. He will make martyrs. We cannot allow that."

"So you want me to destroy him."

"I want you to save him. If he joins the commission, he will be protected. He will have a future. He will be a bishop. The state will approve him. The file will stay closed. He will never know what you did. He will never know what he did. He will live a long and useful life. That is salvation, Halina. That is what we offer."

"And if he refuses?"

Rydel turned around. His face was sad. He looked like a man who was about to bury a friend.

"Then the file will be opened," he said. "The Gestapo report will be sent to the newspapers. The 1945 report will be sent to the Party. You will be exposed. He will be exposed. The Church will drop him. The Party will drop you. You will lose your career. He will lose his vocation. You will both be destroyed. That is not what I want. But it is what will happen."

Halina stood up and found that her legs were steady, and was surprised by it, having expected the weakness that follows a decision; but it was the strength of a person who has been given a clear choice, which is not like other strength and does not last.

"I will talk to him," she said.

"That is all I ask."

"I will not promise anything."

"I am not asking you to promise. I am asking you to try."

She picked up the folder. She put it in her briefcase. She closed the briefcase. She looked at Rydel.

"You said the file is not complete," she said. "What else is in it?"

Rydel smiled. It was a thin smile, like a crack in a wall. "There is always something else," he said. "But that is enough for today."

She left the office and went down through the empty corridor to the elevator, and the guard at the door saluted her the way he saluted everyone above the rank of section head, and out in the street the car was already waiting with its engine running and Wiesław holding the rear door open against the cold.

"Where to, comrade?"

"Drive," she said.

He drove, and she did not know where she wanted him to drive to, so he drove the long way along the river and she looked out the window at the trams and the people and the gray buildings, and thought about the boy in the photograph and the man in the church and the report. And Berezowski. She had never known his first name. He had been "Bór," the commander, a word on a page, a man with a wife and, she thought, children, though she had never asked and had never been told. She had killed a man and did not know his name, and now she was expected to kill the same man again with better paperwork.

The car crossed the bridge. The Vistula was gray. The ice was beginning to form at the edges. The Palace of Culture was on the left, its spire in the clouds. The city looked like a black-and-white photograph. Halina had lived in it for fourteen years. She had never loved it. She had never hated it. It was the place where she worked.

"Stop the car," she said.

Wiesław pulled over on the embankment without being asked, as he always did at that stretch of the river, and she got out and walked to the railing. The Vistula was below, dark and slow and going about its business. The wind came off it cold. She stood there a long time with her coat pulled around her, and Wiesław sat in the car with the engine running and the heater on and did not look at her, which was why she kept him.

A man in a cap walked past. He looked at her. She looked away. She did not want to be recognized. She was a comrade, a deputy department head, a woman with a driver and a briefcase. She was also a woman who had killed a man. She had been twenty-three. She had been afraid. She had thought she was saving Janusz. She had not known that Berezowski would be shot. She had not known that death was so quick. She had not known that guilt could last so long.

She thought about Tadeusz. He had been seventeen. He had been in the hideout because he believed in the revolution. He had been killed because Janusz had talked. She had hated Janusz for fifteen years. She had hated him with a hatred that had kept her warm on cold nights. It had been a kind of love. It had been the only thing left of the boy by the river.

Now Rydel had shown her the file. He had told her that Janusz was a traitor. He had told her that Janusz had killed Tadeusz. He had told her that Janusz was about to become a bishop. He had told her that she could save him or destroy him. He had told her that she was a killer too.

She had always known that she was a killer. She had known it since 1945. She had buried the knowledge. She had built a career on top of it. She had built a life on top of it. She had told herself that it was necessary. She had told herself that Berezowski was a soldier, that he would have been killed anyway, that the revolution needed sacrifices. She had told herself that she was not a murderer. She was a functionary. She was a survivor. She was a woman who had done what she had to do.

But the file was in her briefcase. The file was a piece of paper with her signature on it. The file was a man named Berezowski, who had been shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. The file was a boy named Janusz, who had been tortured by the Gestapo and had given up a hideout. The file was a girl named Halina, who had signed a report to save him.

She turned away from the river. She got back into the car.

"Home," she said.

The car took her home to Marszałkowska. She hung up her coat, put the kettle on out of habit, and then sat down at the kitchen table with the briefcase in front of her and did not open it for a long time, and looked at it, and listened to the kettle come to the boil and go on boiling until it began to spit.

The telephone rang. She jumped. She picked it up.

"Halina?"

It was Maryla. Maryla Zawadzka, her friend from the village, now a doctor at a clinic in Mokotów. They had known each other since they were children. Maryla had been the one who had told her about Rutka. Maryla had been the one who had invited her to Christmas Eve every year. Maryla was the only person who called her at home.

"Yes."

"You didn't come to the meeting yesterday. I was worried."

"I was busy."

"You are always busy. I am coming over. I have a cake."

"Now?"

"Yes. I am downstairs."

Halina looked at the door. She heard footsteps in the corridor. She opened the door. Maryla was standing there with a cake in a tin and a bottle of wine. She was a short woman with a round face and graying hair. She wore a red coat and a knitted hat. She looked like a woman who had spent her life taking care of other people.

"You look terrible," Maryla said.

"Thank you."

"I mean it. You look like you have seen a ghost."

"I have."

Maryla came in with snow in her hair, took off her coat and hat, put the cake on the table, and opened the wine without being asked. She did not ask what ghost. She had known Halina since they were six years old and had learned, in that time, that Halina would tell her when she was ready and not one minute before, and that pushing produced the opposite of the desired result.

They sat at the kitchen table and Maryla cut the cake, which was a poppy-seed cake of the kind Halina's mother had made on the great feast days, and Halina took a piece and ate it slowly because it tasted of a kitchen in Brzóza in about 1934, and because a woman who has spent the day being blackmailed is entitled to a piece of cake.

"The children are well," Maryla said, and went on with the list because it was the kind of list that got her through an evening. "Ewa is at the university, studying medicine, and wants to be a doctor like her mother, God help her. Marek is in the army, and hates it, and says the food is terrible and the officers are worse, and wants to come home. He writes me three pages about the food. Three pages, Halina. I have raised a boy who thinks a potato is a human rights question."

"Tell him to stay."

"I told him. He doesn't listen to me."

"He is young."

"Young people don't listen. We didn't listen either."

Halina smiled. "No. We didn't."

Maryla looked at her. "What is it, Halina?"

"What is what?"

"You have that look. The look you get when you are about to do something stupid."

"I always look like that."

"No. You look like you have seen something you cannot forget."

Halina looked at the briefcase on the table and thought about the file in it, and the photograph of Tadeusz in the wardrobe, and the man in the church on Brzeska Street, and the three of them would not leave her alone long enough for her to sleep.

"Do you believe in God?" she asked.

Maryla put down her fork. "What kind of question is that?"

"A simple question."

"Of course I believe in God."

"Why?"

"Because I was raised to believe. Because I have seen too much to believe in nothing. Because I have a daughter who is alive, and a son who is alive, and a husband who is alive, and I am grateful. Gratitude is a kind of belief, I think."

"I don't believe in God."

"I know."

"I believe in the Party."

"Do you?"

Halina was silent.

Maryla reached across the table and took her hand. "You are not a bad person, Halina. You are a person who has been asked to do bad things. There is a difference."

"Is there?"

"I think so."

"I don't know anymore."

Maryla squeezed her hand. "Come to Christmas Eve. Bring a bottle. We will sing the old songs. We will be bored. We will be normal. You need to be normal for one night."

Halina looked at her friend across the table and felt the thing she always felt with Maryla, which was not love exactly, or not only love: something older than love and less forgiving, the memory of a village and a river and a church and two girls with braids who had believed that life would be a certain way.

"I'll come," she said.

"Good."

Maryla left at nine, with her tin and her coat and her hat and a long look at the briefcase that she did not comment on. Halina washed the dishes, put the cake in the cupboard, poured herself a glass of wine she did not want, and sat down at the table, and opened the briefcase.

The file was inside. She took it out and put it on the table and looked at it without opening it, the way a person looks at a letter from a doctor.

She read the Gestapo report again, and then the 1945 report, and then looked at her own signature at the bottom of it. It was a young signature, with a flourish on the H that she had practised in 1945 because it had seemed to her that a person who was going to sign documents should have a signature worth signing; and she had been proud of it, and had thought it looked like the signature of a woman who mattered, and it had.

She closed the file and put it back in the briefcase. Then she went to the wardrobe and took down the wooden box from the top shelf, and opened it, and took out the photograph of Tadeusz. He was seventeen in it, laughing at something the camera could not see, with his cowlick standing up and the small white scar on his chin from the summer he fell off a borrowed bicycle into the ditch by the mill. He was her brother. He was dead. She had known both of those facts for fifteen years and they had never once, in all that time, arrived at the same moment.

"Tadek," she said.

The word sounded strange in the empty apartment. She put the photograph back in the box. She put the box back on the shelf. She closed the wardrobe.

She went to the telephone and dialed the parish number and it rang for a long time, long enough for her to begin composing what she would say, and then a woman answered — Pani Genowefa, out of breath, with the wireless going behind her.

"Parafia Przemienienia Pańskiego."

"This is Halina Kowal," she said. "I would like to speak to the priest."

"Which priest?"

"Father Wilga."

There was a pause. "Who shall I say is calling?"

"Halina Kowal."

Another pause. The woman's voice changed. "I will tell him."

Halina waited, and heard footsteps in the passage and then a door, and then the priest's voice close to the receiver, saying her office's number aloud to somebody in the room.

"Yes."

"It's Halina."

"I know. They told me."

"I would like to see you."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow. In the afternoon."

"Come to the rectory. At three."

"Thank you."

"Halina."

"Yes?"

"Are you all right?"

She looked at the briefcase on the table. "No," she said. "But I will be."

She hung up and turned off the light and lay down in the dark, and the city was quiet the way a city is quiet at midnight on a Tuesday, and the Palace of Culture was a dark shape against a sky with no stars in it, and she thought about the boy by the river, and the man in the church, and the file, and the choice, and she was still thinking about them when the trams started again at half past four.

She slept.

# Chapter Three: The Parish of the Transfiguration

Janusz Wilga woke at five. He had always woken at five, even when he was a boy. In the village, the cows had to be milked, the stove had to be lit, the water had to be carried from the well. In the seminary, the bell had rung at five. In the parish, the alarm was his own body. He opened his eyes in the dark and lay still for a moment, listening. The church was quiet. The city was quiet. The radiator under the window made a small ticking sound. He got up.

The room was small: a bed, a desk, a wardrobe, a prie-dieu, a bookshelf. The walls were white. There was a crucifix above the bed and a photograph of his parents on the desk. His father had a black beard and a serious face. His mother was laughing. The photograph had been taken in 1937, before the war. Janusz had been fifteen. He had not been born again. He had not known what was coming.

He knelt at the prie-dieu and said the breviary, the old words he had said every morning for ten years, which sometimes opened like a door and sometimes stood there like a wall, and this morning did both at once because he was thinking about Halina, and he had been thinking about her since the funeral, and before the funeral, and for nineteen years.

He finished the office. He washed his face. He put on his cassock. The cassock was black, worn at the cuffs. He had two others, but this one was the most comfortable. He looked at himself in the small mirror on the wardrobe. He was thirty-six. He had a lean face, dark hair, a scar above his left eyebrow. He had the hands of a blacksmith's son: broad, strong, with calluses that had never quite gone away. He was a priest. He was a professor. He was a candidate for the episcopate. He was a man in love with a woman he had not seen in nineteen years.

He went down into a church that was cold because the boiler had broken again, and lit the candles while the sacristan — Pan Tadeusz, small and old and deaf in one ear — opened the doors and set out the missals. The old women came in one by one in their dark coats and their headscarves, in all weather, every morning, and knelt, and prayed, and did not look at him. They were the backbone of the parish. He did not know their names. He knew their faces and their coughs and which of them had lost a son in the war and which a husband and which had lost the faith altogether and come back anyway, out of habit or defiance or because there was nowhere else to go at seven in the morning.

At seven, he said Mass. The church was half full. He preached a short homily about the widow of Nain. He said that Christ had seen the widow and had been moved with pity. He said that pity was not enough. He said that Christ had stopped the funeral procession and had given the widow back her son. He said that the Church was called to do the same: to stop the processions of death, to give the dead back to the living, to refuse the world's arithmetic of sacrifice. He did not mention the Kowalik funeral. He did not mention the factory. He did not mention the Party. He did not have to.

After Mass, he went to the rectory. Pani Genowefa, the housekeeper, was in the kitchen. She was a small, stout woman with white hair and a red face. She had worked for the parish for twenty years. Her husband had died in the Warsaw Uprising. She had no children. She considered the priests her children, and she treated them accordingly.

"The boiler is broken," she said. "Again."

"I know. I will call the man."

"The man came last week. He said it was fixed."

"Then I will call him again."

"He will charge us for the visit."

"He always does."

She put a cup of coffee on the table. It was real coffee, a gift from a parishioner. She did not drink it herself. She made it for him and watched him drink it. She had a way of watching that made him feel like a boy who had not finished his homework.

"There was a phone call," she said. "Yesterday. A woman."

Janusz looked up. "What woman?"

"She did not give her name. She asked for you. I told her you were at the funeral. She said she would call again."

"What did she sound like?"

"Like a woman who is used to giving orders."

Janusz put down the cup. He knew who it was. He had known since the funeral. He had seen her standing at the back of the church, in a black coat, with her face pale and her eyes fixed on him. He had recognized her in the way a man recognizes a scar on his own body. He had not seen her in nineteen years. He had thought about her every day. He had prayed for her every day. He had imagined her as a woman in a gray suit, in an office, with a pen in her hand. He had imagined her as a woman in a prison, in a camp, in a grave. He had never imagined her as she was: a high-ranking Party official, with a driver and a briefcase, standing in his church, looking at him as if he were a problem she had to solve.

"Father?"

He looked up. It was Father Piotr. He was standing in the doorway, in his cassock, with a folder under his arm. He was twenty-six. He had a round face, a small mustache, and the nervous energy of a man who has drunk too much coffee. He had been at the parish for two years. He had been an electrician before the seminary. He was good with the youth group. He was good with the old women. He was not good with secrets.

"The youth club is here," he said. "They want to know about the permit."

"The permit is not ready."

"The man from the council said it would be ready this week."

"The man from the council says many things."

Piotr came in and sat down. He put the folder on the table. "They are worried, Father. They think the state is going to close the club."

"The state is not going to close the club."

"The state closed the club in Mokotów."

"The state closed the club in Mokotów because the priest there was a fool."

Piotr looked at him. "And if they close ours?"

"Then we will open it again."

"Where?"

"Here."

Janusz finished his coffee, stood up, and went to the window. The sky was the color of a dishcloth and the rain was coming down steadily into the courtyard. A black car was parked across the street with two men in the front of it and the engine off, and it had been there since the funeral, and it was the same car he had seen outside the church on the day and the same car he had seen outside the rectory on the day after; he had begun, in the last week, to think of it as part of the parish furniture.

"Piotr," he said.

"Yes, Father."

"Have you ever been to Rakowiecka?"

Piotr's face changed. "No."

"Have you ever been questioned?"

Piotr looked at his hands. "No."

"If they question you, tell them the truth. Do not lie. Do not try to be clever. They are not stupid."

"I know."

"Do you know?"

"Yes."

Janusz looked at him for a moment. He saw something in Piotr's face that he did not understand. It was not fear. It was something else. It was the look of a man who is carrying something heavy and is trying to put it down.

"Piotr," he said. "If there is something you want to tell me—"

"No, Father."

"All right."

Piotr stood up and took the folder and got as far as the door, and then stopped with his hand on the handle.

"Father, the woman who called. Was she from the Party?"

"Yes."

"Are you in trouble?"

"I don't know yet."

Piotr looked at him. He looked like a boy who wanted to say something but did not know how. Then he left.

Janusz stood at the window and watched the rain and thought about Halina, and then about the file, which he had known about for years, because the security service had a file on every priest in Warsaw and his was thicker than most. It held his seminary records and his AK pseudonym and every sermon he had preached since 1951 and a list of the Catholic Intelligentsia Club meetings he had attended out of a sense of duty and disliked out of a sense of taste; and somewhere in it, unless the war had eaten it, was the report from 1943. He had confessed what he had done in that affair to his confessor in 1946 and to his bishop in 1952, and had never told anyone else, and had lived with it the way a man lives with a piece of shrapnel near the spine: the wound closes, the metal stays, and in certain weather you can feel exactly where it is.

He went to the desk. He opened the breviary. Inside the cover, there was a photograph. It was a photograph of a girl of seventeen, standing by a river, with a birch tree behind her. She was wearing a white blouse and a dark skirt. She was laughing. Her hair was in a braid. Her hand was raised to shade her eyes. The photograph was faded. The edges were soft. He had taken it with a camera borrowed from the organist, in the summer of 1939. He had never shown it to anyone. He kept it in his breviary because it was the only place he could keep it. He prayed from the breviary every day. He prayed for her every day. He did not know if that was a sin. He did not know if it was love. He did not know if it was a kind of prayer.

He put the photograph back. He closed the breviary. He went to the church.

At three o'clock, Halina came.

He waited for her in the parish hall, a long low room that smelled of cabbage and floor wax, with a table and chairs and a portrait of Christ and a map of the Holy Land left over from a mission in 1948. The youth club had painted a mural on the end wall: a young man with a hammer, a young woman with a book, and a church in the background with the door standing open. It was naive and sincere and it had taken them a whole winter, and Janusz liked it very much, and the state did not like it at all, which he had always taken as a good sign.

Halina came in wearing gray — a gray suit, a gray coat, a gray hat — so that she looked like a crow in a room built for harvest festivals, and carrying a black leather briefcase. Pani Genowefa showed her in and did not offer to take her coat, and then stood in the doorway a moment too long, and went out and shut it harder than the door required.

"Sit down," Janusz said.

She sat down. She put the briefcase on the table. She looked at the mural.

"Who painted that?"

"The youth club."

"It is not very good."

"It is honest."

"Honesty is not a talent."

"It is a virtue."

She opened the briefcase and took out a folder and put it on the table between them. "The permit for the youth club," she said. "The council has approved it. There are conditions, and the conditions are the point of my being here, so I would rather we did the conditions first and the tea afterward."

"What conditions?"

"The club must be registered with the state. The members must be vetted. The program must be approved. The priest in charge must be a member of the Commission of Priests."

Janusz looked at her. "The Commission of Priests."

"Yes."

"The patriotic priests."

"They are not unpatriotic."

"They are a creation of the state."

"They are an organization of priests who want to work with the state."

"They are an organization of priests who want to survive."

"Survival is not a sin."

"It is if it is the only thing you believe in."

Halina leaned back. She looked at him. "You have changed," she said.

"I have grown up."

"You have become a priest."

"I was always going to be a priest."

"You were not. You were going to be a teacher. You were going to live in the village. You were going to marry me."

The words hung in the air. Janusz did not look away. He had imagined this conversation for nineteen years. He had imagined it in a hundred different ways. He had never imagined it in a parish hall, with a folder of permits on the table and a mural of a young man with a hammer on the wall.

"I was going to marry you," he said. "Then the war came, and I was arrested, and I was tortured, and I gave them the hideout, and your brother died, and you became a communist, and I became a priest. I have never once been able to find the step in that list where I could have put my foot down and stopped it."

Halina's face did not change. "You admit it."

"I admit it."

"You admit that you killed Tadek."

"I did not kill him. I gave them the location. I did not know he was there. I did not know anyone was there. I was in a cell. They had broken my fingers. They had burned my feet. They had put a wire around my testicles. They told me they would kill my mother if I did not speak. I spoke. I gave them the hideout. I did not know what it was. I did not know who was in it. I did not know your brother was there."

"You knew someone was there."

"Yes."

"Then you killed him."

"I have lived with that for fifteen years. I have confessed it. I have done penance. I have prayed for him. I have prayed for you. I have never forgiven myself."

"Good."

"Is that what you want? My guilt?"

"I want the truth."

"You have it."

Halina stood up and went to the window. The rain was coming down steadily into the courtyard, which was empty except for the black car across the street with its two men and its engine off.

"Why did you become a priest?" she asked.

"Because I had nothing else."

"That is not a vocation."

"It is a vocation. It is the vocation of a man who has been broken and has nowhere else to go."

"And you believe?"

"I believe."

"You believe in a God who let your fingers be broken? Who let Tadek die? Who let the Germans burn the village? Who let the communists take the country?"

"I believe in a God who suffered. I believe in a God who was tortured. I believe in a God who died. I believe in a God who rose. I do not believe in a God who makes everything all right. I believe in a God who is with us in the dark."

"That is a beautiful sermon."

"It is not a sermon. It is my life."

Halina turned around. Her eyes were bright. "You always were a better man than I was," she said. "You always were."

"That is not true."

"It is true. You were the one who believed. I was the one who wanted to change the world. You were the one who prayed. I was the one who signed the reports. You were the one who became a priest. I became a functionary."

"You are not a functionary."

"I am a functionary. I am a good functionary. I am the best functionary in the department. I know how to write a report that says nothing. I know how to make a man disappear with a sentence. I know how to smile at a murderer and call him comrade. I know how to survive."

"Survival is not a sin."

"It is if you have to kill someone to do it."

Janusz looked at her. He saw something in her face that he had not seen before. It was not the hard face of a Party official. It was the face of a woman who had been carrying a weight for a long time.

"Halina," he said.

"Don't."

"I am sorry."

"You don't know what you are sorry for."

"I am sorry for the hideout. I am sorry for Tadek. I am sorry for your pain. I am sorry that I was weak."

"You were weak."

"Yes."

"And I was weak."

"Yes."

"And we are both weak."

"Yes."

She looked at him, and the anger went out of her face the way water goes out of a basin when the plug is pulled, and for a moment she looked exactly like the girl by the river. Then the door opened and Pani Genowefa came in with the tea, and set it down with the air of a woman who had been listening at the keyhole and intended to be listened to, and the moment closed over like water.

"Tea," she said. "It is cold in here. The boiler is broken."

"Thank you, Genowefa."

The housekeeper put the tray on the table. She looked at Halina. She did not smile.

"Will there be anything else, Father?"

"No. Thank you."

She left, and Halina poured the tea, and her hand was steady; he watched it, and thought that he had watched her pour tea a thousand times in his imagination, in a village kitchen and a city flat and once, in 1944, in a cell in Lublin, and had never once imagined her here, in a parish hall with a mural of a worker on the wall, pouring tea for a priest she had been sent to break.

"The permit," she said.

"The permit is not the issue."

"The permit is the only issue."

"The issue is the Commission of Priests. The issue is the state's control of the Church. The issue is the truth."

"The truth is that the Church has always been on the side of the powerful."

"The truth is that the Church has been on the side of the poor. Not always. Not enough. But sometimes."

"Sometimes is not enough."

"Never is a lie."

Halina put down the cup. "You are impossible," she said.

"So are you."

She stood up. She put on her gloves. "I will leave the permit here. You can think about it."

"I don't need to think about it."

"Don't decide now. Decide after you have read the conditions."

"I have read them."

"Read them again."

She picked up her briefcase. She walked to the door. She stopped.

"Janusz."

He waited.

"I am sorry about the boiler," she said. "And the mural. And the youth club. I am sorry that the world is the way it is."

"So am I."

When she opened the door Pani Genowefa was in the corridor, pretending to dust a picture that had been dusted at six that morning. Halina went past her without a word and down the stairs into the rain and got into the car, and the car pulled away from the kerb and was gone, and the housekeeper stood at the window of the presbytery and watched it go with an expression that would have alarmed both of them if either of them had seen it.

Janusz stood in the parish hall with the permit on the table and the mural on the wall and the empty chair where she had sat, and felt a pain in his chest that had nothing physically wrong with it: the pain of a man who has been given a glimpse of a thing he cannot have and has been left standing in the room with it.

Pani Genowefa came in. She picked up the cups.

"That one," she said. "She has a cold heart."

"No," Janusz said. "She has a broken one."

"Same thing in this weather."

He smiled. "Genowefa."

"Yes, Father."

"Do you believe that people can change?"

"I believe that God can change them."

"And if they don't want to be changed?"

"Then they stay the same. But God is patient."

"Are you patient?"

"No," she said. "I am old. I have no time for patience."

She left, and Janusz sat at the table and looked at the permit without seeing it. He had been, at seventeen, a boy who wanted to marry a girl and teach children and live in a village and be happy in a way that no one would ever have thought to put in a report; and he had become a man who heard confessions and buried the dead and preached sermons that made the security service take notes, and who loved a woman he could not have, and who had killed a boy he did not know. The permit lay on the table with its stamp and its conditions. He could hear the rain in the courtyard. After a while he got up and went to the church and unlocked the door, because it was the only thing in his life he knew how to do next.

He put the permit in his pocket and went into the church and knelt at the altar rail, and did not pray for himself. He prayed for Halina, and for Tadek, and for the man he had been at seventeen, and for the man he was at thirty-six, and for the grace to be honest, which he had begun to suspect was the only grace he had ever really wanted and the one he was least equipped for.

After an hour he got up, with his knees complaining, and went back to the rectory and telephoned the factory and asked for the personnel office and asked for the report on the Kowalik accident. The woman on the telephone said she would send it in the post. He said he would come and collect it, and she said that would not be usual, and he said that he was not a usual sort of priest, and put on his coat and went out into the rain.

The factory was a twenty-minute walk from the church, and the rain had stopped by the time he got there, and the streets were steaming, and the workers were coming out of the gate in a dark slow river. He walked among them. Some of them he knew: they nodded to him, and said Father, and did not stop, because it was the end of a shift and they were tired and they wanted their dinners. One man in an overcoat with the collar turned up said, without stopping and without looking at him, "The cable, Father," and was gone into the crowd before Janusz could answer.

The personnel office was a small room with a desk, a filing cabinet, and a portrait of the First Secretary. The woman behind the desk was middle-aged, with a kind face and a cold manner. She gave him the report. He read it. It was the same report that Halina had read in the car. The same lie. The same words. He put it in his pocket.

"Thank you," he said.

"Father," she said. "Be careful."

He looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"The men who wrote that report. They are not bad men. They are afraid. But they are afraid of the wrong things."

He nodded. He left the office. He walked back to the church. He did not know what to do with the report. He knew that if he gave it to the newspapers, the state would close the parish. He knew that if he gave it to the bishop, the bishop would tell him to be quiet. He knew that if he gave it to Halina, she would have to act. He did not know if he wanted her to act. He did not know if he wanted to save her or destroy her.

He stopped at the corner of Brzeska Street. The church was ahead, its tower dark against the sky. He could see the window of the rectory. He could see the window of the church. He could see the window of the apartment where Halina had lived? No. She lived on Marszałkowska. He did not know where she lived. He had never been to her apartment. He had never seen her home. He had only seen her in the village, in the church, in the rain.

He went into the church and knelt at the altar rail and took out the report and read it again, and then went through into the sacristy and took off his coat and sat down at the desk and put a sheet of paper in front of himself and began, without any clear idea of what he was doing, to write a sermon. He wrote about a man who had died because a cable had broken, and about a report that said the man was careless, and about a Church that was called to speak the truth, and a state that was not called to do anything at all but which listened, always, whether you wanted it to or not, with a notebook. He wrote about a woman who had come to him in a gray coat. He did not write about the hideout, or the file, or the summer of 1939. He wrote about the truth, which was the only thing he had, and which he had begun to suspect was not a thing at all but a way of standing.

He finished the sermon at midnight and read it through twice. It was too long, and too angry, and too honest, and he put it in the drawer of the desk knowing that he would take it out again on Saturday night and cut a third of it and put the third back on Sunday morning; and then he knelt down and said the office and did not sleep.

He went to bed. He lay in the dark. He thought about Halina. He thought about her face in the parish hall. He thought about her hands. He thought about the tea. He thought about the way she had looked at the mural. He thought about the way she had said, "You were the one who believed. I was the one who wanted to change the world." He thought about the boy he had been. He thought about the man he had become. He thought about the priest he was.

He prayed. He did not know whether God was listening, and had stopped some years ago expecting an answer in the form of anything he could point to, and prayed anyway, because it was the only thing he could do that did not make the situation worse.

# Chapter Four: The Sermon

On the last Sunday of November the Church of the Transfiguration was full an hour before Mass, which had not happened since Easter. The workers came from the factory with their coats brushed and their hair wetted down. The women came from the courtyards with their heads covered and their hands full of flowers, and the children came from the catechism class scrubbed and polished and warned, in the way children are warned, to sit still and not to stare at anybody. The church smelled of wet wool and incense and candle wax. The bell rang. Then it rang again, because the crowd was still coming in, and Pan Tadeusz kept ringing it until the porch was empty, which took a quarter of an hour.

Halina sat at the back, in the last pew, in the gray coat and the gray hat she had worn to the funeral a month before, which was not a coincidence and which she had chosen not to examine. She had come alone again. Wiesław was in the car with the engine running because she had told him to keep it running. She had told herself she was there to hear the sermon in order to report it, and had known, walking up the steps, that this was the third or fourth most convincing lie she had told herself in a career built out of them.

The coffin was not there; Brunon Kowalik had been in the ground a month. But the workers had put a memorial in the centre aisle anyway — a table with a red cloth on it, his photograph in a frame, his helmet, a pair of his work gloves, a candle — and the women had brought flowers and the children had brought a drawing of a crane with a sun in the corner of it. The drawing was crooked and the sun was much too large and it was the most beautiful thing in the church.

Janusz stood at the altar in a black vestment and a white stole with a face the color of the candles, having not slept. He had rewritten the sermon four times and had considered not preaching it for reasons that were all good reasons: the parish, the youth club, the permit, the file, the black car across the street. He had prayed. He had decided. He had discovered, somewhere around the third rewrite, that he had decided weeks ago and had been looking ever since for a reason that would let him un-decide.

He read the Gospel. It was the passage from John: "You shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." He read it slowly. He read it as if he were reading it for the first time. Then he climbed the pulpit.

"My friends," he said. "We have heard the words of Christ. 'You shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.' It is a beautiful sentence. It is also a dangerous sentence. Because the truth does not make us free immediately. First it makes us miserable. First it shows us what we have done. First it takes away our excuses."

The church was quiet. At the back, by the door, one of the two security men had stopped writing and was holding his pen above the page without moving it, and the other had turned his head to look at the priest's face.

"A month ago I buried Brunon Kowalik," Janusz said, "and I said then that he was not careless. I want to say something harder today, and it is not about the director of the works. The director of the works is a frightened man with a plan to fulfil. I could tell you his name and it would not help you." He put both hands on the edge of the pulpit. "The report on Brunon Kowalik was typed by a typist. It was stamped by a clerk. It was filed by a filing clerk, in a registry, in a building on a street you all know, by a woman or a man who went home afterwards and ate supper. That is what I want to say. There is no monster in this story. There is a chain of ordinary people doing one small wrong thing each, because the wrong thing was easier, and every one of them went home and ate supper. And I am one of them, and so are you."

A man at the back said, "That's true." His voice was rough. Janusz waited.

"I am not here to make a speech against the government," he said. "I am a priest, and I am here to say what a priest is for. You are afraid. I know you are afraid, because I am afraid, and because I have been hearing confessions in this parish for seven years and there is not a man in this parish who has not told me so in the dark. We are afraid of losing our jobs and our flats and our ration cards, of being called an enemy of the people, of being taken off a tram in the night. And so we close the file. We say the man was careless. We say the cable was inspected. We say the report was lost. And then we come here on Sunday and we say the Creed, and we say *I believe*, and we go home, and those two things cannot both be true in the same person for very long."

Halina sat with her gloved hands folded in her lap and the leather damp against her palms, and thought: I have written that report. I have written it a hundred times, in a hundred versions, for fifteen years, and I have never once written it because I was afraid. I wrote it because I believed it. That is the thing I will have to answer for, and I would rather it had been fear.

"Christ says that the truth will make us free," Janusz said. "He does not say that the truth will make us comfortable. He does not say that the truth will make us happy. He says it will make us free. And freedom is not the absence of fear. Freedom is the ability to speak the truth in the face of fear. Freedom is the ability to say: this man was not careless. This man was murdered. This cable was frayed. This report is a lie. This file must not be closed."

The congregation had gone silent in the way a room goes silent when everyone in it has stopped breathing at the same moment: the old women crying, the workers straightened up, the children checking their parents' faces to see what was allowed, and the two men by the door writing again, one of them steadily, in shorthand, like a man taking dictation from history. Halina felt her heart going. She knew the sermon would be reported and the priest called in and the parish put under a glass. She knew the state would not tolerate it. She knew she should get up and go out and stand on the steps and be seen to be leaving. She did not move.

"There is a widow in this parish," he said, "sitting four rows from the front, with three children and a pension that will not keep them and a letter from the works that says her husband was careless. She has shown me that letter. She asked me a question I could not answer. She asked me whether the Church was going to say he was careless too, because if the Church said it as well there would be nobody left in the world who did not say it." He looked at the front pews. "So I am saying it now, in this church, in front of the parish and in front of whoever is taking notes. Brunon Kowalik was not careless. The cable was frayed and it was reported in June, and if any man here saw the crane and said nothing, he knows it, and I will not ask him to stand up."

He paused, and turned to the memorial table: the helmet, the gloves, the drawing of the crane with the sun in the corner.

"Today I ask you to do something dangerous," he said, "and it is not to riot and not to hate, and it is not to stand in the street with a placard. I ask you to be willing to be a witness. Not a martyr. A witness. A man who saw the cable, and can say when he saw it, and to whom he reported it. That is all. It is a small thing and it will be written down and it will make somebody in an office very uncomfortable, and one or two of you will come to me afterwards and say Father, not my name."

He came down the pulpit steps and went to the memorial table and picked up the candle and held it up, and the flame bent in the draught from the door and then stood straight again.

"Brunon Kowalik," he said, and his voice went, and he had to stop and start again. "Brunon Kowalik. Servant of God. A man who got up in the dark. A man who was afraid and went anyway, which is the whole of what we mean when we say a man was brave, and which we have spent a month pretending was not enough. May God forgive us the month. May God forgive us the file. May God give us the grace to be a witness and not a martyr, because there are too many martyrs in this country already and not enough people willing to say what they saw."

He put the candle down and made the sign of the cross over the coffin, and the organ began to play, and the choir came in a beat late, and the whole congregation stood up at once with a scrape of chairs and boots that went through the church like a wave.

Halina stood with them and did not sing. She looked at the priest and thought that he was not the boy she had known, and not the man she had imagined either — that he was a man who had stood up in a church full of frightened people and said the true thing at the cost of his parish and his career and possibly his liberty, which was a thing she had once believed in and had not believed in for fifteen years. He had chosen. She had not chosen anything since 1945. She had only chosen to survive, over and over, in a series of small decisions that had added up to a life.

After the Mass the workers gathered in the parish hall in the way they always gathered after a funeral, which is to say without anybody having arranged it, and stood around in their good coats being angry and frightened and wanting something done; and Janusz stood at the front of the room and let them talk and did not interrupt, because a room full of men who have just heard the truth needs a quarter of an hour to find out what it wants to do with it.

"We should go to the factory," said a young man named Zbyszek. He was twenty-two, with a red face and a loud voice. "We should stop the line. We should demand an investigation."

"If we stop the line, they will arrest you," Janusz said.

"Then they will arrest me."

"And then what? Will that bring Brunon back?"

"No. But it will show them that we are not afraid."

"They know you are not afraid. They are the ones with the guns."

"We can petition the government," said an older man. "We can write to the Sejm. We can write to the newspapers."

"The newspapers will not print it."

"Some of them will."

"Some of them will be closed."

"Then we will write to the Cardinal."

"The Cardinal is not a factory inspector."

"Then what do we do, Father?"

Janusz looked around the room at their faces, at the anger in them and the fear and the small stubborn thing that was not hope exactly but would do instead. He wanted to tell them to fight, and to be brave, and that the truth would win. He knew it was not true. The truth did not always win; in his experience it was usually buried, and often killed, and now and then forgotten so completely that people began to speak of it as a thing that had never existed. But silence was not the answer either. Silence was what the state wanted, and what the report wanted, and what the closed file was for.

"We do what we can," he said. "We help the widow. We help the children. We write the letter. We keep the memory. We do not let them close the file. And we do not let them make us hate them. Because if we hate them, we become like them."

Zbyszek looked at him. "That is not enough, Father."

"It is not enough. But it is something."

"Something is not enough."

"No. But it is what we have."

The meeting ended and the workers went home through the rain, and Janusz stayed in the parish hall and sat down at the table and put his head in his hands. He was tired and afraid and not at all sure he had said the right thing, or that there was a right thing to have said.

The door opened. It was Halina.

She was still in her gray coat, with her face pale and her eyes red, and he understood with a small shock that she had been crying — she, who had been angry and cold and ironic and exhausted in his company for twenty years, and whom he had never once seen cry.

"Halina," he said.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say anything. Just listen."

He stood up. She came toward him down the side aisle and stopped a few feet away with her hands clenched at her sides and her eyes red, and he understood that she had been crying in the car, and that she would have hated anyone to know it.

"The security service is going to arrest you," she said. "Not today, and probably not tomorrow, because they will want to see what the sermon does in the works first. But soon. They have enough already — the sermon, the meeting in the hall, the petition with four hundred names on it — and if they want more they will invent it. They will say you are inciting the workers, and a reactionary element, and an agent of the Vatican; and one of those three will stick, because it always does, and because there is no man in this country who cannot be made to sound like a foreign agent if the sentence is written by a man who is paid to write it."

"Let them."

"Let them? Do you know what happens to priests who are arrested? Do you know what happens in those rooms? Do you know what they do?"

"I know."

"You don't know. You think you know. You think you are prepared. You are not prepared. No one is prepared."

"I am a priest."

"You are a man."

"I am a priest."

"You are a man who has been tortured once. Do you want to be tortured again? Do you want to be broken again? Do you want to give them another name? Do you want to kill another man?"

The words hit him like a slap. He stepped back. She saw the pain in his face. She did not apologize.

"That is what you are afraid of," she said. "Not the arrest. Not the prison. You are afraid that they will break you again. You are afraid that you will give them another name. You are afraid that you will kill another man. That is why you preach. That is why you hide behind the truth. Because you are afraid of what you will do when they put the wire around your testicles again."

Janusz sat down. He put his head in his hands. He felt sick. He felt old. He felt the pain in his fingers, the old pain, the pain that never went away. He heard the Gestapo officer's voice. He heard his own voice, cracked, begging, giving the location. He heard the sound of the door. He heard the silence after the door.

"Halina," he said.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I didn't mean—"

"Yes, you did."

"Yes. I did."

He looked up at her. "You are right," he said. "I am afraid. I have always been afraid. I have been afraid since 1943. I have been afraid every day. I have been afraid of the Gestapo. I have been afraid of the UB. I have been afraid of the SB. I have been afraid of the bishop. I have been afraid of the cardinal. I have been afraid of God. I have been afraid of you. I have been afraid that I am not a good priest. I have been afraid that I am a coward. I have been afraid that I will be broken again."

"Then why do you preach?"

"Because I am a priest."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have."

Halina sat down across from him. She put her hands on the table. "Janusz," she said. "I am not here to save you. I am here to save myself. I have a file. Rydel has a file. The file has my name on it. If I don't bring you to him, he will destroy me. If I bring you to him, he will destroy you. I don't know what to do."

"He wants you to persuade me to join the Commission."

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"He will release the file. The 1943 report. The 1945 report. The one with my name on it."

"The one about Berezowski."

Halina looked at him. "You know about Berezowski?"

"I know his name. I know he was my commander. I know he was arrested in 1945. I know he was shot. I did not know you were involved."

"I was involved."

"What did you do?"

She told him. She told him about the office in Lublin. She told him about the man behind her. She told him about the glass of water. She told him about the signature. She told him about Berezowski. She told him that she had done it to save him. She told him that she had not known Berezowski would be shot. She told him that she had killed a man.

Janusz listened and did not interrupt and did not judge, and looked at her face in the light of the sacristy lamp, and saw the girl by the river and the woman in the gray coat and the murderer and the victim, all of them printed on top of one another like a photograph taken twice on the same plate, and the last of them, always, was the person he loved.

"I have hated you for fifteen years," she said. "I have hated you because I thought you killed Tadek. I have hated you because I thought you were a coward. I have hated you because you became a priest and I became a communist. I have hated you because you were good and I was not."

"I am not good."

"You are better than I am."

"No. I am not."

"You are."

"Then why did I give them the hideout?"

"Because you were afraid."

"Yes."

"And I was afraid."

"Yes."

"And we both killed someone."

"Yes."

They sat in silence. The rain had stopped, and the church was doing the thing a church does after a crowd has left it, which is to hold the last of the warmth and the last of the noise and give them back slowly. A tram bell rang out on Brzeska Street and went away.

"Halina," Janusz said.

"Don't."

"I forgive you."

She looked at him. "You don't know what you are forgiving."

"I know enough."

"You can't forgive me. I haven't asked."

"You don't have to ask."

"I don't want your forgiveness."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want to stop being afraid."

He reached across the table and took her hand, which was cold, and held it, and she did not pull away. They sat like that in the parish hall with the tea going cold between them, two people who had been broken by the same war in two different places, and who had spent fifteen years being angry at the wrong person.

"I can't stop being afraid," he said. "But I can be afraid with you."

She looked at him. Her eyes were wet. "That is the most romantic thing you have ever said."

"It is not romantic. It is theology."

"It is a bad theology."

"It is the only theology I have."

She laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was real. She wiped her eyes with her glove.

"Janusz," she said.

"Yes."

"I have to go."

"I know."

"Rydel is waiting."

"I know."

She stood up and let go of his hand and walked to the door, and stopped there with her hand on the frame and her back to him.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For not lying."

"I lied to you once."

"I know."

"I am sorry."

"I know."

She left. He sat at the table and looked at his hand, which was still warm where she had been holding it, and thought about the kiss under the birch tree in 1939 and the girl with the braid and the woman in the gray coat, and the file, and the sermon, and the fear, and the truth, and God. He did not know what to do, or what to say, or how to pray. So he sat at the table, and waited, which is what a man does when he has done the thing he was afraid of and has not yet found out what it will cost.

The next morning, Halina went to the factory.

The Zakłady Mechaniczne im. Nowotki was a vast complex of brick sheds and smokestacks on the east bank of the Vistula, and at seven in the morning the gates were open and the shifts were coming in through them in a river of caps and bicycles. The air smelled of coal and iron and hot oil, and the noise was a single continuous roar with the clang of metal somewhere inside it and the hiss of steam somewhere else and men shouting under both. Halina went through the gate with her identity card in her hand; the guard saluted her, and looked at the card, and saluted again, and she walked into the works the way she had walked into a hundred works in the last ten years, and hated it a little more than usual.

The personnel office was on the second floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled of rubber and carbon paper. The director, inż. Ryszard Sokół, was waiting for her: a tall man with a gray face and a nervous smile, in a dark suit with a Party badge on the lapel that he touched, she noticed, the way a man touches a lucky coin.

"Comrade Sowa," he said. "This is an honor."

"Is it?"

"We don't often get visitors from the Central Committee."

"You have one now."

He smiled. He had the smile of a man who has been smiling for so long that he has forgotten what it means.

"The Kowalik accident," she said. "Tell me about it."

Sokół opened a folder. He read from it. His voice was flat. "On the morning of 14 October, at 7:20, the crane operator Brunon Kowalik was killed when a load slipped from the crane hook and fell on him. The investigation found that the cable was in working order and that the accident was caused by operator error. The worker failed to inspect the cable before the shift."

"Who investigated?"

"The factory safety commission."

"Who was on the commission?"

"The chief engineer, the personnel director, and the Party secretary."

"Was any worker on the commission?"

"No."

"Was the widow interviewed?"

"No."

"Was the letter that Kowalik wrote in June included in the report?"

Sokół's smile flickered. "There was no letter."

"There was a letter."

"Who told you that?"

"The priest."

"The priest is not a factory inspector."

"No. He is a priest. He hears confessions."

Sokół looked at her and put down the folder very carefully, the way a man puts down a glass when his hand has begun to shake. "Comrade Sowa," he said. "I have been the director of this factory for six years. I have fulfilled the plan every year. I have been decorated twice. I have a wife and two children and a flat on Waszyngtona with a telephone in it. I am not a criminal. I am a man who was given an old crane and a plan and no cable, and I reported the cable, and I have the copies of the letters in this folder to prove that I reported it, and nobody in this city will read them, because the only thing anyone will want to know is why the director did not stop the crane."

"I did not say you were a criminal."

"You are here because of the priest. He has been stirring up the workers. He has been telling them that the factory killed Kowalik. He has been telling them that the report is a lie. He has been telling them that I am a murderer."

"Are you?"

"No."

"Then you have nothing to fear."

Sokół's face had gone the color of the ash in the tray. "The cable was old," he said. "I knew it was old. I reported it in April and again in June and the replacement did not come, because the plan came first and the allocation came second and the cable came a long way third, and I signed the inspection because if I had not signed the inspection the works would not have made the quarter and there would have been a new director by Friday who would have signed it instead. I did what I could. I know what that is worth. I have known since the fourteenth of October what it is worth."

"Did you?"

"I did what I could."

"That is not the same."

"What do you want me to do? Do you want me to go to the workers and tell them that the factory killed their friend? Do you want me to tell them that the plan is more important than their lives? Do you want me to tell them that I am a coward? Do you want me to lose my job? Do you want my children to starve?"

"No."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want the truth."

"The truth is that the cable was old. The truth is that I reported it. The truth is that the replacement did not come. The truth is that Brunon Kowalik is dead. The truth is that I am afraid. The truth is that we are all afraid."

Halina looked at him. She had come into the room expecting a monster, and had found a man who had made a compromise somewhere back down the line and learned to live inside it, which was a thing she understood so exactly that it was almost unbearable. He was a man like her.

"Thank you," she said.

"Is that all?"

"That is all."

She left the office and walked out through the works, through the noise and the heat and the long sheds with their belts turning overhead, and found the men from the crane gang in the yard by the foundry, and talked to them. They were afraid to speak at first. Then an old man named Mieczysław came forward, sixty years old and bent, with a face like a dried apple and hands that had been in the trade since before the war.

"The cable was frayed," he said. "I reported it in June. I wrote a report. I gave it to the safety commission. The report was lost."

"Do you have a copy?"

"No."

"Do you have anything?"

He reached into his pocket. He took out a piece of metal. It was a cable strand, frayed at the end, about ten centimeters long.

"I kept this," he said. "I knew someone would come."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the first one who came."

Halina took the cable. It was cold and heavy. She put it in her bag.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," Mieczysław said. "Thank God. If you believe in Him."

"I don't."

"Then thank the priest. He is the one who told us to remember."

Halina left the factory in the middle of the afternoon and went to Brzeska Street, to a tenement with a broken gutter and a smell of coal in the stairwell, and up four flights to Wanda Kowalik's room. It was one room, clean and poor: a bed, a table, two chairs, a stove, a shelf with four books on it and a photograph. The three children were sitting on the bed — Ewa twelve, Marek nine, Tomek six — and they were quiet with the particular quietness of children who have learned that quietness is the safest thing to be.

Wanda was at the stove with her back to the door. She was thin, with dark hair going gray at the temples and a face that would have been pretty if it had not been so tired; the tiredness was recent, and it sat on her features like a coat that did not belong to her. She poured Halina a glass of tea without asking and set it down, and Halina sat.

"I am sorry about your husband," Halina said.

"Thank you."

"I am investigating the accident."

"The investigation is over."

"Not this one."

Wanda looked at her. "Are you from the factory?"

"I am from the Central Committee."

"The Central Committee."

"Yes."

"My husband wrote to the factory in June," Wanda said. "To the director. He asked for the cable to be replaced. He got no answer. He wrote again in August, and to the union, and to the Party committee, and he got no answer from any of them, and he was not a troublemaker, he was a good worker, he had been at that crane eleven years. He just wanted the cable replaced. He said it was dangerous. He said it could kill someone." She looked at the table. "It killed him."

"Do you have the letters?"

"I have one."

Wanda went to the shelf and took down a book — a school reader, with a picture of a stork on the cover — and opened it, and took out a letter that had been folded twice and flattened under the weight of the other books. She gave it to Halina without a word. It was written in pencil on a page torn from a notebook, in a careful hand, the hand of a man who had left school at fourteen and had never quite stopped being proud that he could write.

*Dear Comrade Director,*
*I am writing to report that the cable on crane number 4 is frayed. I have reported it to the safety commission. I ask that it be replaced before an accident occurs. I have a wife and three children. I do not want to die.*
*Respectfully,*
*Brunon Kowalik*

Halina read the letter. She put it in her bag with the cable.

"Thank you," she said.

"What will you do?"

"I will write a report."

"A report."

"Yes."

"Will it say what happened?"

"Yes."

"Will they listen?"

"I don't know."

Wanda looked at her. Her eyes were hard. "You people always promise," she said.

"I am not them."

"You are in their car."

"I am in their car. But I am not them."

"Then prove it."

Halina looked at the three of them. Ewa was holding a book with her finger in it to keep the place. Marek was holding a wooden car with one wheel missing. Tomek was holding his mother's hand. They looked back at her with the flat evaluating gaze of children who have learned that adults who arrive with briefcases bring news of the sort that is discussed after they are sent to the neighbours.

"I will prove it," she said.

She left the apartment and went back across the river to the Central Committee and sat down at her desk and took out a sheet of paper and wrote the truth. She wrote about the cable and the letter and the director and the safety commission, and about the workers in the yard who had been afraid to speak until an old man named Mieczysław spoke first, and about the widow and the three children and the school reader with the stork on the cover; and when she had written it all, which took an hour and a half, she read it. She knew that if she sent it, it would be buried. She knew that if she sent it, she would be buried. She knew that if she sent it, the priest would be right, and the state would be wrong, and she would have to choose.

Then she took out another sheet of paper and wrote a shorter report, in the flat language of the office, saying that the accident had been caused by a combination of factors, including the age of the equipment and the possibility of operator error; that the works had taken steps to improve safety; that the widow would receive a pension; and that the matter was closed. It took her eleven minutes. She signed it.

She put the first report in her drawer. She put the cable and the letter in an envelope. She put the envelope in the drawer. She locked the drawer. She put the second report in her outbox.

She went home and poured a glass of wine and sat at the table and looked at the wooden box on the shelf without opening it, and then at the Palace of Culture through the window, with its lights on and its spire floodlit, going up into a sky that had no more stars in it than a ceiling.

"Tadek," she said. "I'm sorry."

She drank the wine. She went to bed. She did not sleep.

In the church on Brzeska Street, Janusz knelt at the altar. The candle was burning. The statue of the Virgin was above him, her face pale in the dark. He prayed for Brunon Kowalik. He prayed for Wanda and the children. He prayed for the workers. He prayed for Halina. He prayed for himself. He prayed for the courage to be honest. He prayed for the grace to be faithful. He prayed for the strength to be afraid.

The snow began to fall. It fell on the church, on the courtyard, on the statue, on the city. It fell on the Palace of Culture, on the Vistula, on the rooftops. It fell on the village of Brzóza, on the church of St. Michael, on the grave of Tadeusz Kowal. It fell on the world.

Janusz finished his prayer and stood up and blew out the candle and went to bed, and lay for a while in the dark with his hands folded on his chest like a man laid out, and then turned over and slept, and dreamed of nothing at all.

# Chapter Five: The Years of the Locust

Halina could not sleep.

She lay in the dark and listened to the city. The trams had stopped. The Palace of Culture was a black shape against the sky and the snow came against the window with a soft hiss, like sand, and she thought about the file, and the boy by the river, and the man in the church, and the hideout in the forest, and Tadek, and it went round in that order until it was not thought at all but a wheel turning.

She got up and put on her robe and went to the table and turned on the lamp, and opened the briefcase, and took out the file, because it was three in the morning and there was nothing else in the flat she wanted to look at.

The Gestapo report was on top. She read it again, and the words were the same words they had been every night for three weeks: the hideout, the raid, the three bandits, Tadeusz Kowal, the signature of the German officer at the bottom, the eagle and the swastika stamped into the paper so hard that the ink stood up under her thumb.

She put the report down and looked at the wall, which was white, and at the yellow circle the lamp made on it, and then she closed her eyes, and the wall was a wall on Brzeska Street and the lamp was the lamp in a kitchen in Brzóza in 1941, and she could hear her mother putting the plates down.

She was seventeen again.

---

Brzóza, June 1939.

The village was a cluster of houses around a church, in a bend of the river, in the Lublin countryside. The church of St. Michael was brick, with a tall tower and a green copper roof. The manor house was white, with a red tile roof and a park full of old oaks. The Jewish mill was by the river, with a water wheel that turned slowly in the current. The fields were flat, stretching to the horizon, with rye and buckwheat and potatoes. The road was dirt, with ruts from the carts. The air smelled of manure, cut hay, and river water. It was not a beautiful village, but it was home.

Halina Kowal was the daughter of Stanisław Kowal, a landless farmhand who worked for the manor. Her mother, Aniela, kept a garden and a few chickens. They lived in a small house at the edge of the village, near the forest. Halina had a younger brother, Tadeusz, who was thirteen. He was a skinny boy with a cowlick and a laugh that could be heard across the fields. He was always getting into trouble. He was always singing. He sang in the church choir, badly, with a voice that cracked. The choirmaster, Pan Berezowski, despaired of him. Halina loved him.

Janusz Wilga was the son of Franciszek Wilga, the blacksmith. His father was a big man with a black beard and hands like hammers. His mother, Jadwiga, was a small woman with a kind face. They lived in a house next to the forge, near the church. Janusz was an only child. He was quiet, serious, and strong. He could lift a sack of grain that would make a grown man stagger. He could play the harmonica. He could imitate the priest's cough. He was the best altar boy in the parish. He was in love with Halina.

They had known each other all their lives. They had played together as children, in the river, in the forest, in the churchyard. They had gone to school together. They had sung in the choir together. When Halina was fourteen, she realized that Janusz was not a boy anymore. When she was fifteen, she realized that she loved him. When she was sixteen, he kissed her under the birch tree by the river. It was a warm evening in June. The frogs were singing. The water was low. The birch tree was white against the green. He took her hand. He said, "Halka." She said, "What?" He said, "I love you." She said, "I know." He kissed her. She kissed him back. It was the first kiss for both of them. It was clumsy and sweet. It tasted of wild strawberries.

They met at the river every evening that summer and sat under the birch tree and talked. They talked about the harvest and the church and the priest and the manor, and the Jews, of whom there were forty-three in the village and about whom neither of them knew, that summer, what was coming; and about the government and the war and the future, which they discussed with the confidence of people who had never had one taken off them. Halina wanted to be a teacher. She wanted to go to Lublin to study and come back to the village and teach the children their letters and tell them, in a way that would not get her into trouble, that the world was bigger than Brzóza. Janusz wanted to be a priest. He had wanted it since he was nine years old, and he loved the church and the Latin and the incense and, above and behind all of it, the idea of God; and what he wanted, when he was honest with himself, was to serve, which is a thing that no one had ever wanted for him at home.

Halina laughed at him. "A priest," she said. "You? You can't be a priest. You're in love with me."

"I can be a priest and love you."

"No, you can't. Priests don't marry."

"I know."

"Then what about me?"

"I will love you in my heart."

"That's not enough."

"It will have to be."

"That's not enough, Janusz."

His eyes were very dark in the lamplight. "I don't want to lose you," he said.

"Then don't become a priest."

"I have to."

"Why?"

"Because God is calling me."

"God is calling you? What about me? What about us?"

"God is calling me to be a priest. He is also calling me to love you. I don't know how both can be true. But I believe they are."

"That's a beautiful answer. It's also a lie."

"It's not a lie."

"It's a lie. You're choosing God over me."

"I'm not choosing. I'm trying to be faithful."

"To God?"

"To both."

"You can't be faithful to both. You have to choose."

"I won't choose."

"Then I will."

She stood up and walked away down the bank, and he did not follow her, which was the right thing and also the wrong thing; and she came back the next evening and he was waiting under the birch tree with his hands in his pockets, and he did not say anything, and took her hand, and they sat down. They did not talk about the future. They talked about the river and the frogs and whether there was a war on the moon, and they kissed, and they were seventeen, and they thought they had time.

The war came in September.

---

Brzóza, September 1939.

The German planes came on the first day, low, with the black crosses showing on the underside of the wings. They bombed the railway station at Puławy, twenty kilometres away, and the explosions came rolling back over the fields like thunder with a direction to it. The windows rattled in their frames. The chickens ran in circles. The villagers stood in the road and looked at the sky, and did not go to the shelters, because there were no shelters in Brzóza and no one had thought to dig any.

The army came three days later: young men, dusty, tired, riding motorcycles and trucks with their boots hanging over the tailgates. They stopped at the manor and took the horses and the grain and the radios, and then they nailed a notice to the church door. All Jews were to wear a white armband with a blue star. All Poles were to surrender their weapons. All men between sixteen and sixty were to report for labour. Father Grudziński read it aloud to the congregation on Sunday and told them to be quiet and to survive and not to provoke anybody; and then he went back into the sacristy and sat down and was sick.

Janusz's father hid the forge hammer under the floorboards, though what good a hammer was going to be against a Panzer he could not have said. Janusz hid in the thatch of the barn a rifle he had found in the forest with a broken stock, and oiled it once a month for three years, and never fired it once.

Halina's father was taken for forced labour in October. The Germans came to the house and asked for his papers and looked at his hands and said he was strong, and took him to Germany, and he was gone two years. When he came back he was thin and grey and would not talk about it, and slept in the barn for a month because he had got used to sleeping alone. Halina's mother cried for a week. Then she stopped, and went out to the fields, and did the work of two people, because the farm was now hers and there was no one else to do it.

The village changed, in the way that a village changes when one set of masters is exchanged for a worse set. The manor became the German headquarters and the park was cut down for firewood, tree by tree, until there was no shade in it. The mill was closed. The Jews were made to wear armbands and to step off the pavement into the gutter when a German passed, and then they were made to do other things, and then they were not seen for a while. The Polish police, the blue police, helped. And the headman, Góra — a fat man with a small moustache and a gold tooth he showed whenever he smiled, which was often — discovered that he had become somebody. He had been nobody before the war. Now he had a telephone call to make in the evening and lists to draw up: who had a radio, who had a rifle, who had been seen carrying a sack to a farmhouse in the forest. He was a Judas, and everybody in the village knew it, and nobody said it, because he was also the man who decided who got the good coal.

Janusz joined the underground in the winter of 1940, having turned eighteen in November. He was sworn in, in the forest at night, by a man named Berezowski, who was thirty-one and had a wife in Otwock and a way of speaking to the boys that made them feel they had been chosen for something. The oath was short and he remembered all of it for the rest of his life. He was given the name Sosna — Pine — because he was tall and straight and because Berezowski had a weakness for the obvious, and for three years men he had never met would come up to him in barns and farmyards and say *Sosna?* and he would say yes.

Halina joined the other underground in the spring of 1941, at nineteen, by way of a man named Marek at a market in Puławy. He had a thin face and burning eyes and a satchel of pamphlets, and he talked to her for an hour behind the cattle pens about the Soviet Union and the class struggle and the coming of the workers' state. He told her that the AK was the tool of the London government and the landlords; she had been brought up to think that was the natural order of things, and had begun, that winter, to think it was not. She had seen the manor. She had seen the poverty. She had seen the priest bless the rich and tell the poor to be patient. She believed him. She joined the People's Guard and was given the name Sowa — Owl — because she was quiet and watchful, and because she sat at meetings saying nothing and then asked, at the very end, the one question that nobody had an answer for.

Tadeusz joined the People's Guard in the spring of 1942, at sixteen, which was too young, and he lied about his age to a man in a cap in a barn outside Puławy who could see perfectly well how old he was and did not care. He wanted to fight. He hated the Germans with a pure, uncomplicated hatred that his sister, who was nineteen and had begun to read pamphlets, could already see was going to get him killed. Halina tried to stop him. He did not listen, because he was a boy, and because boys of sixteen in the third year of a war believe in the specific and stupid way of boys of sixteen that they are immortal.

They met in secret, in the forest and the church and the mill, and they argued. They argued about the AK and the AL and the Soviet Union and the London government and the future of Poland, in the way that two people argue when they are in love and have discovered that love does not settle anything. They argued about Katyn.

It was April 1943, and the Germans had announced the discovery of a mass grave in the Katyn forest: Polish officers, thousands of them, shot in the back of the head. The Polish government in London asked the Red Cross to investigate; the Soviets broke off relations with it; and the news went through the village in a week, in the way news went through Brzóza, from the smithy to the shop to the pump. The AK believed the Germans, which cost them something to say out loud. The People's Guard believed the Soviets, which cost them nothing at all.

"They murdered our officers," Janusz said. They were sitting in the loft of the mill, in the dark. The flour dust was in the air. The wheel was still.

"You believe the Germans?" Halina said. "The Germans are liars. They killed millions of people. They would say anything to divide us."

"The Red Cross should investigate."

"The Red Cross is a tool of the bourgeoisie."

"The Red Cross is neutral."

"There is no neutral. There are only class forces."

"That is a lie."

"That is Marxism."

"That is a lie."

"The Soviets are our allies. They are fighting the Germans. They are dying for us."

"They are not dying for us. They are dying for themselves. They murdered our officers. They will enslave us."

"You are a reactionary."

"I am a Pole."

"So am I."

"No. You are a communist."

They looked at each other in the dark of the mill with the flour dust in their hair, twenty and twenty-one years old, and in love, and on opposite sides of a war that had another two years to run and would not be finished, as it turned out, in either of their lives.

"I am a Pole," Halina said. "I am a Pole who wants a free Poland. A Poland where the workers rule, where there is no manor, no landlord, no priest telling us what to do. A Poland where Tadek can go to school, where my mother does not have to work in the fields, where no one is taken to Germany. That is my Poland. The AK does not want that Poland. The AK wants the old Poland. The Poland of the manor, the church, the rich. I do not want that Poland."

"The AK wants a free Poland. A Poland where we can vote, where we can speak, where we can worship."

"Vote? Speak? Worship? The AK is led by the London government. The London government is led by the rich. The rich do not care about us."

"The communists are led by Moscow. Moscow does not care about us either."

"Moscow cares about the workers."

"Moscow cares about Moscow."

"You are impossible."

"So are you."

She stood up and looked at him with the flour dust on her face and her eyes bright with a thing that was not quite anger.

"Janusz," she said. "I love you. But I cannot follow you. I cannot follow the AK. I cannot follow the church. I cannot follow the past. I have to follow the future."

"Then follow it."

"I will."

"Without me?"

"Without you."

He looked at her. His face was pale in the dark. "I will pray for you," he said.

"Pray for what?"

"For your soul."

"I don't believe in the soul."

"I know. I will believe for both of us."

She laughed. It was a sad laugh. "You always were a better man than I was," she said.

"That is not true."

"It is true."

She kissed him. It was a hard, desperate kiss. Then she left.

He did not follow her. He sat in the loft, in the dark, with the flour dust, and he prayed.

Brzóza, 1942.

The Jews of Brzóza were taken away on a Tuesday in October.

There were forty-three of them. The Kreiners, who owned the mill, and the Gutmans, who owned the shop, and the Rosenbergs, who made shoes, and the Silbermans, who had the tailor shop, and the Feinmans, who had the bakery and had given Halina a roll every Friday for six years because she was a child who came in with her mother. The children. The old people. The young men. The young women. They were assembled in the square in front of the church with one suitcase each, and told that they were being resettled, and told to be quiet.

Halina stood at the edge of the square. She was twenty. She was holding her mother's hand. Her mother was crying. Rutka Kreiner was in the square. She was twenty. She was Halina's best friend. They had grown up together. They had played in the river together. They had shared their lunches at school. Rutka was wearing a dark coat and a white armband with a blue star. She was carrying a small suitcase. She looked at Halina. She did not wave. She did not cry. She just looked.

The Germans loaded them onto trucks and the trucks went up the road towards the forest, and Halina watched them go and did not move and did not speak and did not cry. She was afraid, and the fear was very simple and very clear: if she moved they would shoot her; if she spoke they would shoot her mother; if she cried they would see her. So she stood still in the square with her mother's hand in hers and watched the trucks until the road was empty, and then she went home, and it was the last time in her life that she was ever that afraid of anything.

It was a secret, and it lasted two weeks. Rutka had got off the truck in the forest — she never said how, and Halina never asked — and had come through the woods at night, and to the house, because there was nowhere else. Halina's mother fed her. Halina gave her clothes out of her own chest. Tadeusz, who was sixteen, stood guard in the yard with a stick. They hid her in the attic under the straw, and brought her up water in a bottle, and at night she came down and sat by the stove and did not talk much. She had seen her parents taken away, and the children, and did not know whether any of them were alive, and had decided, in the way people decide such things, that they were not.

Góra found out.

He came to the house on a Sunday. He was wearing his best coat. He had his gold tooth. He said he knew about the Jewish girl. He said he would tell the Germans unless Halina's mother gave him money. Halina's mother gave him everything she had. It was not enough. He took the money and told the Germans anyway.

The Germans came at night, three of them and a Polish policeman from Puławy whose face Halina knew from the market, and they took Rutka down from the attic by the ankle. They took Halina's mother to the station and beat her for six hours and asked her who else had helped, and she did not tell them; and in the morning they let her go, and she walked home with her face swollen shut and her dress torn at the shoulder. She did not cry. She sat down in the kitchen and looked at the wall, and it was three days before she spoke again.

Rutka was taken to the forest and shot with the others. Halina never saw her again. There was no grave and no stone; there was the forest, and a place in it that everyone in Brzóza could have walked to and nobody ever did.

Halina stopped believing in God that winter, and she did it the way most people do it, which is without a decision and without a scene. She did not argue with the priest, or announce anything to her mother, or throw a crucifix into the river. She simply stopped praying one week, and then stopped going to church, and then stopped crossing herself when she passed the church door, and found that nothing happened to her, and that the hole where the thing had been was not a hole at all but a room, and that the room could be furnished. She furnished it with the Party. She worked harder than anyone in the district, carried messages, distributed pamphlets, sat through meetings that went on until midnight, learned the slogans and the songs and the names of the men in the photographs. She learned to hate the Germans, and the landlords, and the AK, and the Church, and the whole dead world of the village. She learned to hate Janusz. It was the easiest of the lessons, and it was the one she was best at.

There was a great deal she did not know. She did not know that Janusz's family had hidden a Jewish boy in the forge that same autumn, or that his father had been beaten half to death for it, or that Janusz had carried the boy out to the forest at night and given him to the partisans and come home with his hands shaking. She did not know that he had prayed for Rutka, or that he had cried. As far as she was concerned he was a coward hiding behind his church, on the wrong side of a war he had refused to fight.

Lublin, August 1943.

The Gestapo arrested Janusz on a Thursday.

He was in Lublin, delivering a message to the underground. He was staying in a safe house on Krakowskie Przedmieście. The Germans came at four in the morning. They broke down the door. They took him in his shirt. They put him in a black car. They drove him to the prison on the Castle Hill.

The cell was small and cold, with a concrete floor and walls painted the green that institutions use when they have given up, and a bucket in the corner, and a window high up with bars in it through which the light came in grey. Janusz sat on the floor with his hands tied behind his back and was afraid in a way he had not known he could be afraid.

The interrogation began in the morning.

The Gestapo officer was a polite man who spoke good Polish with a slight Berlin accent and offered Janusz a cigarette, which he took, because his hands were shaking and it was something to do with them. He asked about the underground, and the AK, and Berezowski, and the hideout in the forest. Janusz said he did not know. The officer smiled and said that was a pity, and that they had ways of helping people remember.

They beat him. They kicked him and beat him with a rubber truncheon and broke two of the fingers of his left hand, one at a time, and burned the soles of his feet with cigarettes. They hung him from a hook by the arms until his shoulders went out. They put a wire on him and passed a current through it, and asked him the same four questions, in the same order, for two days and two nights. He said he did not know. He said he was a courier and knew nothing. He said he was sorry. He cried, and begged, and prayed, and in the end he gave them the hideout — on the second night, at about four in the morning, with his own voice saying the words before his mind had agreed to them — because a man's body will do its own arithmetic at some point, and his did it sooner than he had believed a body could.

On the third day they brought him a photograph of his mother. They said they would kill her if he did not talk, and his father, and burn the house with them in it. He looked at the photograph. She was smiling in it; she was in the kitchen with the flour up to her elbows, making bread, waiting for a son to come home; she had no idea, when the picture was taken, what her son would be asked to trade for her life.

He broke.

He told them about the hideout in the forest. He told them where it was and that the People's Guard used it. He did not know who was in it that week; he did not know Tadeusz was there, or that anyone was; he knew a place and a distance and a path through the ferns, and none of that had been secret at the mill or in the village, and he gave them all of it.

The Gestapo raided the hideout on 14 August 1943.

Tadeusz Kowal was there. He was seventeen years old and he was asleep with two other young men in a bunker dug into a bank of ferns, and the Germans came at dawn and surrounded it and fired into the entrance. He ran. He got perhaps fifteen metres into the ferns before they shot him in the back, and he fell, and the ferns closed over him, and the other two were killed where they stood; and then the Germans burned the hideout and went away and left the three of them in the forest for the woodcutters to find on Thursday.

The Gestapo released him on 20 August, because they had decided he was a minor courier and not worth the paperwork. He walked out of the prison on the Castle Hill with two fingers splinted, his feet wrapped in cloth because he could not get his boots on, and a face the color of tallow, and he walked to the station and took a train to Puławy and walked the twenty kilometres to Brzóza, and got to the house in the evening. His mother opened the door. She looked at his face for a long moment and did not ask what had happened; she took him inside and sat him down and heated water and washed his hands and his feet and put salve on the burns, and fed him bread and milk, and did not ask. He did not tell her. He never told her. She was the first person he ever kept it from, and she was not the last.

The village found out about the raid. The People's Guard found out. They said that someone had betrayed the hideout. They said that the Gestapo had been told. They said that Janusz Wilga had been arrested and released. They said that he had talked. They said that he was a traitor.

Halina believed them.

She was in Puławy when she heard. She was sitting in a room with Marek and three other comrades. Marek told her. He said it gently. He said that Tadeusz was dead. He said that the hideout had been betrayed. He said that Janusz Wilga had been arrested and released. He said that the Gestapo did not release people unless they talked. He said that Janusz was a traitor.

Halina did not cry and did not scream. She sat very still in the chair in the room in Puławy with the other comrades looking at her and the kettle boiling on the stove, and after a while she said, "I know," which was not true, and which was the first lie she told for the revolution, and which she would remember longer than any of the others.

She knew because she had always known. She knew because Janusz was weak. She knew because he was a priest. She knew because he had chosen God over her. She knew because he had chosen the AK over the future. She knew because he had killed her brother.

Then she went home and cut off her hair. She had worn it in a braid since she was a girl; she took the kitchen knife and cut the braid off in one hand and put it in the stove and watched it burn with a feeling she would not have been able to name, and then put on a man's shirt and trousers and went back to work. She was nineteen. She became efficient. She became hard. She became, in the phrase the Party used in those years, reliable. She did not cry again for fifteen years.

What she did not know would have filled a book: that he had been tortured, and had broken, and had given up the location to stop them burning his mother's hands in front of him; that he had not known Tadeusz was in the bunker; that he had prayed for Tadeusz every day since, by name, and asked forgiveness for a death he had caused without knowing whose it would be. She did not know that he had loved her. She thought he was a traitor, and she hated him, and the hatred was a relief, because it was the only thing in the room that was warm.

---

Brzóza, July 1944.

The Soviets came in July.

The Germans left in the night, burning the manor and the mill behind them and taking the horses and the cows and shooting the dogs, and the Soviets came in the morning. They came on tanks, dirty and grinning, and gave the children chocolate and the women tinned meat, and put a red flag on the church tower. Father Grudziński took it down. They put it up again. He took it down again. They laughed and let him keep it down, which was the first and for a long time the only joke anybody in Brzóza heard about the new order.

Halina came back to the village in August 1944 in uniform, with a pistol on her hip and a red star on her cap, and the villagers looked at her and did not know what to say. Some were afraid of her and some were impressed by her and one old woman who had known her mother since before the war spat on the ground at her feet and walked away without a word. Góra was still the headman. He had changed sides; he was a socialist now; he greeted her as a comrade in the middle of the road, with both hands, and told her that he had always known she would go far. She knew what he had done. She knew he had sold Rutka and her mother for a sack of sugar and a good word with the Germans. She shook his hand anyway, and smiled, and asked after his wife, and it was the first time she understood that she had learned to do a thing she would not be able to unlearn.

She saw Janusz in the church.

He was at the altar in a black cassock, serving the Mass, and he looked up when she came into the doorway. He had entered the underground seminary in 1943, after his release; he was studying in secret in a room above a bakery in Lublin and coming back to the village to serve at the altar, and he had a breviary and a borrowed soutane and the beginnings of the manner he would have for the rest of his life. She stood in the doorway in her uniform with the pistol on her hip. He looked at her face, and then at the pistol, and then at her face again, and did not smile and did not frown; he looked at her the way a man looks at a thing he has been expecting for a year and had begun to hope would not come.

She turned and walked out.

Nor did she know the other thing, the small thing, which he would carry longer than the rest: that he had written her a letter in the seminary in the winter of 1946 and kept it in the pocket of his cassock for a year, and never sent it, and had prayed for her every day of his life since the birch tree.

She knew that he had killed Tadek. She knew that he was a traitor. She knew that he had chosen the church. She knew that he was on the wrong side. She walked out of the church and did not look back.

---

Lublin, February 1945.

The Urząd Bezpieczeństwa was on a side street in Lublin, in a gray building with a courtyard and a gate that was always shut. The corridors smelled of boiled cabbage and disinfectant. The offices were small and cold, and the men in them were young and serious and wore civilian clothes and carried pistols, and they were the new police and the new power, and they knew it, and they were careful with it, in the way that men are careful with a thing they have not yet had to use.

Halina worked in the press department. She wrote pamphlets, and slogans, and articles for the newspapers, and she was good at it: she had a clear mind and a sharp pen and a peasant's contempt for a sentence that was doing no work. She was promoted twice in a year and given an office with a window, and then a secretary, and then, in February 1945, a summons to the office of a man named Rydel.

He was older than her by perhaps fifteen years. He had a soft face and heavy lids and a gray suit that had been cut well before the war, and he offered her a cigarette and she said she did not smoke, and he lit one for himself and put the packet away and looked at her with an expression that she would come to know very well over the next thirteen years, which was the expression of a man who is about to give you a choice he has already made.

"Halina," he said. "We have a problem."

"What problem?"

"We have arrested a man. He is a member of the AK. His name is Janusz Wilga."

Halina's heart stopped. She did not show it. "I know him," she said.

"We know you know him. You were childhood friends. You were in the same village. We have a file."

"What do you want?"

"We want to know if he is a threat."

"He is a priest."

"He is a member of the AK. He is a threat."

"He is a seminarian. He is studying for the priesthood. He is not a threat."

"The AK is a threat. The AK is an anti-state organization. We are trying to build a new Poland. The AK is trying to restore the old Poland. We cannot allow that."

Halina looked at him. She saw the photograph of the girl with the ribbon on the wall behind his head, and the desk, and the file, and the glass of water she had not drunk; and she saw the man behind her chair, standing by the door with his pistol, not because he thought she would run but because that was how the room was arranged.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

"We want you to sign a report. We want you to tell us what you know about him. We want you to tell us who his commander was."

"I don't know his commander."

"You know Berezowski."

Halina closed her eyes, and saw the loft in the mill with the flour dust in the air, and Janusz's face at seventeen, and Tadek's face at thirteen, and the letter he had written her from the barracks at Dęblin in 1942 that she still had, and the hideout in the forest, and the ferns.

"He is a traitor," she said.

"Yes," Rydel said. "He is a traitor. We know that; we have the Gestapo report, and the Gestapo report is in German and signed, which in this office is the next best thing to a confession. But a report about a hideout in 1943 is not a network in 1945. We need the network. We need Berezowski. And you can help us, because you are from that village, and you know that name, and you have known it since you were a child."

"What will happen to Janusz?"

"He will be released."

"And Berezowski?"

"Berezowski will be arrested."

"And then?"

"And then he will be interrogated."

"And then?"

"And then he will be punished."

Halina looked at the glass of water. It was clear and ordinary and there was nothing in it, and she thought about the river, and the birch tree, and the boy who had kissed her under it, and the man who had killed her brother, and the future, and the Party, and the new Poland that was going to be built on all of it.

She signed the report.

The report said that Janusz Wilga was a member of the AK, pseudonym Sosna. It said that he was a dangerous element. It said that his commander was Berezowski, pseudonym Bór. It said that Berezowski was to be arrested and interrogated. It was signed H. Kowal.

Janusz was released in March 1945. He was never charged and never told why he had been arrested and never told who had signed the paper that sent the patrol to the hideout. He went back to the seminary, which had come up out of the ground with the spring, and was ordained in 1948, and became a priest, and was careful for the rest of his life with the word *confession*.

Berezowski was arrested three days after the report was signed. He was interrogated for six weeks. He was shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. He was forty-three. He had a wife and two children. His name was never mentioned in the newspapers. His family was told that he had died of a heart attack.

Halina never spoke of it. She never asked about Berezowski's family, or whether the wife in Otwock had been told, or where he was buried; she did not know his first name and never learned it. She had killed a man and had called it revolution, and the revolution had come, and the man was still dead, and the two facts sat in her at the same distance from each other as they had on the day it happened.

---

Warsaw, 1958.

Halina closed the file.

The snow was still falling and the Palace of Culture was still dark and the city was still there. She looked at the file and at the signature and at the photograph of the girl by the river, and it seemed to her, at four in the morning on a Tuesday in December, that she was both of them and would have to be both of them until she died, and that there was no arrangement of the facts that would let her be only one.

She put the file back in the briefcase and closed it and turned off the lamp and lay down again in the dark, and did not sleep, and watched the ceiling go from black to gray, and at six o'clock got up and made tea she did not drink and went to the office four hours early.

In the church on Brzeska Street, Janusz was kneeling at the altar rail. He had been there two hours, and his knees hurt and his back hurt and the two fingers the Gestapo had broken in 1943 had begun, as they did in cold weather, to ache in a way that was almost companionable. He was praying for Halina, and for Tadek, and for Berezowski and the man's wife and the man's two children, and for Rutka Kreiner, and for the village, and for the whole of Poland, which he had come to think of as a country full of people doing their best in a system designed to make their best impossible.

He knew now. That was the thing he could not put down: he knew. She had told him in the parish hall three weeks ago, in a voice with everything scraped out of it, that she had signed the paper that killed Berezowski, and that she had done it to save him, and that she had known what she was doing and had done it anyway. He had forgiven her before she finished the sentence, and she had refused the forgiveness, and she had been right to refuse it. Forgiveness was not a thing you handed a person like a coat. It was a thing you carried alongside them, in the same weather.

He was praying for her, and he was praying for Tadek, and he was praying for Berezowski, and he was praying for the woman in Otwock he had never met, and he was praying for the boy he had been in the cell in Lublin and the man he was now, and there was no difference between the two prayers and there never had been. He had loved her. He had lost her. He had found her again. None of it had been paid for. All of it had been given.

He prayed. The candle flickered. The snow came down past the window in the dark. At six the bell in the tower rang the Angelus, and the sound went out over the roofs of Praga, and he bowed his head.

He did not move.

# Chapter Six: The Bishop's Cross

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the second week of December, and it was not a letter. It was a telephone call from the curia at half past eight in the morning, and the voice on the line was the cardinal's secretary, who said only that His Eminence would receive Father Wilga at eleven, and that the matter was not to be discussed with anyone.

Janusz shaved badly and cut himself under the chin. He stood in the little bathroom off the rectory kitchen with a towel pressed to the cut and the water going cold in the basin, and he thought: *So it has come.* He had been expecting it ever since the sermon. He had expected it in the shape of a black car and two men in gray coats, and he had prepared himself for that. He had not prepared for the curia.

Pani Genowefa gave him tea and a piece of bread with dripping, and watched him eat it with the expression she reserved for the seriously ill.

"You are going to see the cardinal," she said. It was not a question. In the rectory, nothing was a secret for more than an hour.

"I am."

"Wear the good cassock."

"This is the good cassock."

"It has a shine on the elbows."

"Then I will sit with my arms down."

She sniffed, which was her way of saying that the Church had gone to the dogs and no one consulted her. Then she went to the cupboard in the hall and came back with a small jar of black cherry jam, which she had been saving, and put it on the table in front of him, and went out again before he could thank her.

He took the tram to Castle Square. It was a cold morning, the kind of cold that comes off the river and settles in the lungs, and the snow on the roofs of the Old Town was the color of an old coin. The tram was full of people going to work: women in felt boots with their heads wrapped in scarves, men in caps with their collars turned up, a boy with a satchel eating a roll. Nobody spoke. Somebody's breath smelled of onions; somebody's of plum brandy. A woman in a headscarf read a prayer book with her lips moving.

He got off at the bottom of Miodowa Street and walked up past the Primate's palace, and the cold came in at the tops of his shoes where the leather had given up.

The palace had been shelled in 1944 and rebuilt, and the rebuilding was still not finished: one wing was covered in scaffolding, and the courtyard smelled of lime and wet plaster. The secretary took his coat and hat and hung them on a peg that had not been there in the autumn. He was a young man with a smooth, careful face, the kind of face that has never had to make a decision, and he led the way up the stairs without a word.

The corridor was long and cold. The portraits of dead bishops hung in a row on the left-hand wall, and as he walked past them Janusz felt the peculiar sensation of being reviewed. There was the cardinal's door, with its black leather padding and its brass handle worn smooth by hands that had been at it for a hundred and fifty years. The secretary knocked.

"Come in."

The study was warm. That was the first thing: the warmth of it after the corridor. A tiled stove stood in the corner with its door open, and the fire inside it was the color of a poppy. There was a desk with a green lamp, and books stacked on the floor because the shelves were full, and on the wall a photograph of Pius XII with a black ribbon on the frame, and below it a map of Poland with the new western border drawn in red ink by somebody's hand. On the desk was a bowl of apples, a stack of typewritten pages, a magnifying glass, and a small notebook bound in black cloth, and beside the notebook a pen lying exactly parallel to the edge of the desk.

The cardinal was standing at the window with his back to the room. He was fifty-seven, but the years of the internment had settled into him like damp into a wall. He was tall, and he had the big, heavy hands of a country priest's son, and when he turned around Janusz saw again what he always saw: the eyes. There was nothing tired about the eyes.

"Father Wilga. Sit down."

"Eminence."

Janusz sat. The cardinal did not sit. He walked along the bookshelves with his hands behind his back, and he began, in the way of a man who has spent his whole life being listened to, in the middle of a thought.

"When I was a young priest," he said, "I was sent to Przasnysz. It was a small town, and there were not many educated people there, and so the young people came to me. I started a circle for them. We read, we sang, we had discussions. It was not a parish of intellectuals. It was a parish of farmers and mechanics and girls who worked in the post office. And the late countess came to me one day — she was the patroness of the parish, she had the right of presentation, an old woman with a face like a knife — and she said to me: 'Father, why do you trouble yourself with them? They will not read the books. They will not understand.'" He turned. "Do you know what I said?"

"No, Eminence."

"I said: 'They are not reading the books, Countess. The books are reading them.'"

The cardinal smiled, briefly, as at a memory he did not entirely trust, and then the smile went away.

"You have a youth club at the Transfiguration," he said.

"We have a table-tennis table and a library."

"Books reading them."

"I hope so."

"Twenty-two young people last month. Fifteen the month before. A boy with a criminal record and a girl whose father was in the AK and a young man who works at the Nowotki works and is not a fool, and who, I am told, argues with you about Marxism."

"Zbyszek," Janusz said. "He is a good boy. He is angry."

"Anger is not the worst thing in a young man. Apathy is worse. The Party has the angry ones. We have the apathetic ones. That is a bad exchange."

"It is changing."

"Yes. It is changing. That is why they are frightened of you."

The cardinal came and sat down at last, across the desk from Janusz, and folded his big hands on the blotter. On the desk, the notebook lay closed. Janusz looked at it and then away, because it would not do to be seen looking.

"In July," the cardinal said, "the security police went into Jasna Góra and broke open the archives. They took the files of the Institute. They beat the archivist. They beat the priests who were there. They went into my own office and pulled the drawers out onto the floor and took what they wanted and left the rest." He said it in a level voice, as a man reports weather. "You know this."

"I read the communiqué."

"You read what the bishops let you read. The rest you can imagine." He picked up an apple and turned it in his fingers and put it down again. "They did not find what they were looking for, because it was not there. What they were looking for was that we are in contact with the Vatican, which we are, and with the West, which we are, and that we are conspiring against the state, which we are not. They will not be able to prove it, so they will not stop looking. That is the situation. We are not being destroyed. We are being squeezed. Every month, a little less air."

"And the answer?"

"The answer is priests." He looked at Janusz. "The answer is men who can stand in a pulpit in Praga and say what you said in November and not be destroyed by it. The answer is men who can talk to a boy like your Zbyszek, and who can also be received at the ministry without being humiliated, and who can hold a parish together when the money runs out and the roof leaks and the state has taken the catechism out of the schools for the third time." He paused. "The answer is a bishop."

The stove crackled. Out in the corridor, someone dropped something metal and swore and then was silent.

"Eminence," Janusz said, "you cannot mean me."

"I mean exactly you. The Holy Father has been consulted. There is no obstacle. There will be an auxiliary see in this archdiocese, and when it is vacant I will present one name to the office, as I am permitted to do, and the name will be yours."

"I am thirty-six."

"Karol Wojtyła in Kraków is thirty-eight, and they consecrated him in September, and he is a poet and a philosopher and he climbs mountains. The state consented to him because they believe that a mystic cannot organize a strike. Do not let the age trouble you. Let the state."

"I am not Wojtyła."

"No. You are not." The cardinal's voice was dry. "Wojtyła is a gentle man. He will spend thirty years becoming a saint, and the state will not lay a finger on him, because he does not frighten them. You frighten them. That is not a defect in you. It is a fact about them."

"Then they will not consent."

"They will not consent." The cardinal said it flatly, without drama. "That is likely. They will refuse you, and they will appoint a man of their own, and I will have to accept it, or I will not, and then we will be in the newspapers for a month. That is the calculation I have to make, and I am asking you to help me make it."

"By consenting."

"By being willing. If the Church asks, you will say yes, and the asking and the answering will become a fact that the state must deal with. Or you will say no, and I will not ask again." The cardinal leaned back. "I am not offering you a mitre, Father Wilga. I am offering you a cross. I want that to be clear between us. The last bishop who was consecrated in this country was released from prison to be consecrated, and the one before him was not."

Janusz looked at the fire. He was aware of his own heartbeat, and of the shine on the elbows of his cassock, and of the smell of the coal.

"Eminence," he said, "there is something you should know."

"If it is about the war, I know."

Janusz looked up. For a moment he could not speak.

"I was in the AK," the cardinal said. "Do not look so surprised. Half the secular clergy of this province were in the AK. I know about the Lublin province, and I know that in 1943 you were arrested, and I know that men under interrogation do not behave like statues of the martyrs, because they are not made of stone, they are made of nerves and flesh and a mother's photograph." He picked up the apple again. "If you want to tell me the rest, tell me. If you do not want to tell me, do not. I am not your confessor. But do not think that I am asking you to be a bishop because I believe you have never broken. I am asking you because you have broken and have gone on."

Janusz sat with his hands in his lap. He could have said it then, in that warm room with the fire ticking behind its iron door. He could have said: I gave them the hideout. Three men were killed, and one of them was the boy I had loved like a brother, whose sister I have loved since I was sixteen. He could have said it, and the cardinal would have heard it, and perhaps the cardinal would have said something wise. But the cardinal had said he was not his confessor, and it was not a thing he could put into the air of that office and leave there, between the apples and the map.

"I did break," he said. "I have been to confession about it for fifteen years."

"Then it is between you and God, and between you and the man you have made your peace with." The cardinal's mouth was gentle and unsparing at once. "One thing. If you say yes, and they refuse you, they will dig. They will find whatever is in the files and they will put it in the papers, and they will not print it with any sympathy for a young man with a broken hand in a cell in Lublin. They will print it as the story of a traitor who was made a bishop by reactionaries. And every man in the Church who has ever been ashamed of his war will see his own face in yours." He set the apple down. "If you cannot bear that, say so now, and I will ask a better man. There may be one. I am not sure."

"I can bear it."

"Say it again."

"I can bear it, Eminence."

"Good." The cardinal stood, and Janusz stood, and the interview was over as quickly as it had begun. The cardinal held out his hand and Janusz kissed the ring, and the cardinal held his hand a moment longer than the ceremony required. His grip was warm and dry and very strong. "Pray about it for a week. Then come back. And Father Wilga — do not tell the newspapers."

"No, Eminence."

"Not even the friendly ones."

"No, Eminence."

The corridor was colder on the way out. In the antechamber a man was sitting on the bench with his hat on his knees: a priest of about forty-five, thick-set, well-fed, in an overcoat of good cloth. He had the look of a man who has been kept waiting exactly long enough to be angry about it and has decided not to be.

"Father Wilga," the man said, standing. "Bochenek. We met at the clergy conference in 1956. You gave a paper on the lay apostolate."

"I remember. You disagreed with it."

"I disagreed with the last page of it." Bochenek smiled, and there was something agreeable in the smile, which was worse than if there had not been. "I agreed with the first four, which nobody has ever forgiven me for."

"His Eminence is expecting you."

"He is." Bochenek did not move. He turned his hat slowly in his hands. "May I ask you something, Father? Not as a priest. As a man."

"You may ask."

"Do you think you are being used?"

Janusz looked at him. "By whom?"

"By all of us. That is the honest question, isn't it. The cardinal wishes to make a point to the state. The state wishes to make a point to the cardinal. There are six hundred priests in this archdiocese and most of them are tired and most of them are frightened and most of them have no idea what to do with a nineteen-year-old who has been told since he was six that the Church is a superstition for old women. And you are to be the answer to that, because you speak well and you were in a cell and you are young." Bochenek turned the hat again. "If they refuse you, the cardinal will be humiliated and you will be a parish priest in Praga for the rest of your life. If they accept you, you will have the mitre and the state will have a bishop who owes them nothing and can therefore be destroyed at any time with a clear conscience. Either way, someone else's point is being made."

"And you, Father? Whose point will you be making?"

Bochenek put his hat on. "I am a practical man," he said. "I have been a parish priest in Nowy Dwór for eleven years, and I have built a church there with money from the government, and I have a youth club and a kindergarten and a football team, and the Party secretary's wife comes to my Christmas Mass. I am not ashamed of it. When your bishop is in prison, someone must keep the parish open." He opened the door for himself. "Do not mistake my questions for hostility, Father Wilga. I would rather it were you than me. I am simply telling you that I have been in this antechamber for an hour, and I have counted the apples in the bowl, and I have prayed about it, and I have concluded that I am not the man." He nodded and went in.

Janusz walked down the stairs and out into the cold. The snow had begun again, small and hard, the kind that stings. He walked as far as the column of Sigismund and stopped and stood with his hands in his pockets, looking at the empty square, and he found that he could not feel anything except the cold. Not fear. Not exaltation. The cold, and beneath it a small flat certainty like a stone in the shoe: *They will refuse. And then what?*

He thought of Zbyszek, who had asked him in September whether the Church had any answer to the fact that the works canteen had no meat and the Party did. He thought of old Mieczysław, who had given Halina the cable strand. He thought of the boy he had been at seventeen, standing on the bridge at Brzóza, certain that the world was about to be remade and that he would be one of the men who remade it.

He walked all the way back to Praga. It took him an hour and a half and it was foolish, and by the time he came up Brzeska Street his feet were numb and his cassock was wet to the knees.

The church was cold. The boiler had failed again on Sunday, and the men from the coal depot had promised to come on Thursday, which meant Friday. The old women came anyway, in their coats, with their rosaries, and sat in the front pews and prayed, and the breath went out of them in small clouds. Pani Genowefa had put a paraffin stove in the porch, and it stank, and it was a mortal sin against the fire regulations, and it kept the worst of the frost off the holy water.

He went into the church and knelt for a while at the side altar, where the plaster Christ stood with his hand raised, and he said the office and then he stopped saying anything and simply knelt.

That was where Halina found him, an hour later, at three o'clock in the afternoon, when the light was already going.

She came down the aisle in her gray coat with the collar turned up. She had come from the ministry; he could tell by the shoes. She stood beside him and waited until he crossed himself and got up, and her face was white with the cold and with something else.

"The cardinal," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I am in the ministry and the cardinal is not a rumor." She sat down in the pew and pulled off her gloves, and her hands were red. "Rydel called me in this morning. They know about the see. They know the name. They had it before the curia was told, which means one of your priests has a tongue, or one of the secretaries does." She looked up at him. "They will refuse you, Janusz."

"I know."

"You do not know. You have guessed. I am telling you."

"Then tell me why."

"Because you spoke against them in a pulpit in November and the note of the sermon went to the department and then to the Central Committee. Because you are thirty-six and you can hold a room. Because they have Bochenek, and Bochenek is everything they want: a builder, a practical man, a man who has never said a word that could be printed on the front of *Trybuna Ludu* with a photograph of a bishop smiling." She said it all quickly, in the flat, competent voice she used in offices. "And because Rydel has a file."

Janusz sat down beside her. The cold of the pew came up through his cassock. Ahead of them, at the altar, the red lamp burned in its little cup of oil.

"Tell me about the file," he said.

"You know what is in it."

"Tell me what he will do with it."

"He will give you a choice." She was silent for a moment, and then she said, very evenly, in a voice from which every trace of tenderness had been removed, as a surgeon removes a growth: "The state will consent to your appointment if you join the Commission of Priests. You will sign a declaration. You will sit on the commission and you will say what it says about peace and the German danger and the Church's place in socialist society, and the file will stay closed, and you will be a bishop, and the Gestapo report will never see daylight. That is the offer."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the report goes to the newspapers. And my report goes to the Party." She folded her gloves in her lap and looked at them. "He did not say that. He did not have to. We are one file, Janusz. My signature is on the second page."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere at the back of the church a door opened and closed, and the draft came down the aisle and touched the back of his neck.

"Halina."

"Don't."

"I have to say it."

"Don't say it in a church."

"If not in a church, where?" He turned to her. "I have loved you since I was sixteen years old. I have loved you through the war and through the peace and through every year of it, and I have said Mass for you and buried people for you and I have had a photograph in my breviary for nineteen years. I know what I am. I know what I cannot be. I have not asked you for anything and I will not ask you for anything. But I will not stand in the snow outside a ministry and pretend that what I feel for you is Christian charity. It is not. It is love, and it has been love since the birch tree, and I am too old to lie about it."

Halina did not look at him. She looked at the altar, and her jaw was set, and her eyes were shining in the light of the lamp.

"Say something," he said.

"You are a priest."

"I know that. I have never once been permitted to forget it. Not by the seminary, not by the cardinal, not by myself at three in the morning. Say something else."

"I am a divorcée of the Party. I am thirty-six years old and I have killed a man and I have a card in my pocket that says I am trustworthy. If they put you on the front page with me beside you, they will not call it love. They will call it the proof." She turned to him at last. "You want me to say that I love you. I have said it. I said it in this room—" she stopped herself, and started again. "I said it in the parish hall. I have not unsaid it. But I will not have my love used as the instrument of your destruction. That is what it would be. That is what they are offering us. Not a life. A scandal."

"That is not what I asked for."

"What did you ask for?"

"To be told the truth by you. That is all. I am a priest; I am not a fool. I know what the world will do to a bishop who loves a woman. I have read the papers. I know what they did to Father K." He looked at the altar. "But I have also read the Gospel, and it says that the truth sets us free, and I have begun to think that it means something less comfortable than we tell the children. It means the truth costs. It means you cannot be free and keep your file."

"Then we are both bankrupt."

"Then we are both bankrupt." He almost smiled. "At least we are solvent in the same currency."

She laughed. It came out of her wrong, a wet, broken sound, and she put her hand over her mouth and looked horrified at herself.

"Genowefa is right about you," she said.

"What does Genowefa say?"

"That you are too clever for the parish and not clever enough for the world."

"That is the most accurate assessment of my character anyone has ever made."

She stood up. She put her gloves on, one finger at a time. The church was darkening around them, and the windows over the altar were turning from blue to black.

"Rydel wants you at Rakowiecka on Friday," she said. "Ten o'clock. He will make the offer in person. He likes to do it in person."

"Then I will go."

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"Whatever you decide," she said, "do not decide it for me. I have spent fifteen years being the reason men did things. I am tired of it."

She walked up the aisle. At the door she stopped, in the parallelogram of gray light, and crossed herself with the water from the stoup, quickly, the way she had done as a girl, before she remembered not to. Then she was gone.

He stayed in the church until the Angelus rang, and then until the altar boys came in to light the candles for Benediction, and then he went into the sacristy and took off his stole.

The telephone in the rectory rang at eleven that night.

It was Pani Genowefa, from the hall, and her voice was so changed that for a moment he did not know her.

"Father. They have taken Father Piotr."

"Taken him where?"

"The militia. Two men in a car. They came to the door and asked for him by name and he went with them. They would not say where. He asked if he could take his coat and they said no. Father, it is below zero and he went out in his cassock—"

"Genowefa." He was already reaching for his own coat. "Genowefa. Listen to me. Go and put the kettle on. And then go to your room and lock the door and do not open it for anyone but me."

"But Father—"

"Go. I am coming."

He put the receiver down. He stood for a second in the dark hall with his hand still on the telephone, and he thought, with a clarity that frightened him: *They have begun with the boy. They always begin with the boy.*

Then he took his hat and his scarf and went out into the snow.

# Chapter Seven: The Tenth of December

The room on the third floor of the ministry had no window, only a grille high in the wall that gave onto a shaft, and the shaft gave onto the sky, and the sky that night was the color of a wet slate. There was a table with a green blotter, two chairs, a brass ashtray, a carafe of water, and a portrait of Felix Dzierżyński in a black frame. The radiator under the window made a noise like a man clearing his throat. It was twenty past two in the morning.

Father Piotr Nowicki had been sitting in the chair for three hours. He had not been beaten. That was the first surprise, and it had taken him an hour to understand that it was also the technique. Nobody had shouted at him. Nobody had taken his watch or made him stand facing the wall. A man had brought him tea in a glass with a metal holder, and the tea had been hot and sweet, and he had drunk it, and then he had been ashamed of drinking it.

The interrogator's name was Major Zawistowski. He was a small, tidy man of about fifty with thinning hair combed carefully across his scalp and a faint smell of eau de cologne. He wore a gray suit that had been pressed and a wedding ring worn thin at the back. He had a folder in front of him, tied with tape, and he had not opened it.

"Tell me about the table tennis," he said.

Piotr blinked. "The table tennis?"

"The club. You have a table. Where did the table come from?"

"We bought it. The parish bought it. Second-hand, from a club in Wola that was closing. It cost four thousand two hundred złoty."

"Four thousand two hundred." The major wrote it down. "And the bats?"

"We bought the bats new."

"Where?"

"At the shop on Targowa. They were ninety złoty each. We bought six."

"Six bats." The major wrote that down too. "For twenty-two young people. That is a curious ratio."

"Some of them play with their hands."

"With their hands." The major looked up. His eyes were mild and interested and entirely empty. "You see, Father, this is what I want you to understand. I am not a monster. I have a son of fifteen and he plays table tennis at his school, with his hands, when the bats are broken, which they usually are. I am asking you about the table tennis because the table tennis is not the point, and I want you to see that I know it is not the point. The point is that in that club there is a library, and in that library there are books, and among the books there are books that were printed in Paris and in London and in Rome, and there are young people who read them and then go home and tell their fathers that the Party has no answer to Teilhard de Chardin. That is the point. And also — this is the second point — among those young people there is a boy called Zbyszek Malinowski, who works at the Nowotki works and who has been heard, by a person whose name I will not give you, saying that the strike committee at Poznań in 1956 was right, and that there will be another one, and that this time the priests will ring the bells." He put down his pen. "I want to know what the priest says to that."

"He says don't be a fool."

"And what else?"

"He says that a Christian does not strike against his neighbor. He strikes against injustice. And that violence is what the powerful want, because violence gives them the excuse."

Major Zawistowski wrote it down. He took his time about it. When he had finished, he looked up and said, without any change of tone at all:

"Your mother lives at 14 Lubelska Street, in the apartment on the ground floor with the bad door. She is sixty-one. She has a widow's pension of four hundred and forty złoty a month, and a place in the queue at the cooperative, and a sister in Siedlce. She has a photograph of you on the sideboard in your ordination alb. She tells the neighbors that her son is a priest, which is not against the law, and that he is a good son, which is also not against the law. She has a bad heart. It says so in her medical file."

Piotr's mouth was dry. He had finished the tea twenty minutes earlier and had been afraid to ask for more.

"Now," the major said. "You may help me, and nothing will be wrong with your mother's pension, and nothing will be wrong with her place in the queue, and nothing will be wrong with her heart. Or you may refuse to help me, and I will write in this folder that the investigation is continuing, and I will put the folder in a drawer, and every month or so someone will knock on your mother's door at six in the morning and ask her a few questions, politely, and go away, and the neighbors will watch. That is all. Nobody will be arrested. Nobody will be hurt. It is simply that from now on her life will be a little worse in every particular, and it will be your doing, and she will know it is your doing, because I will tell her." He smiled. "I am telling you the truth, Father. That is the courtesy I offer. I am not going to pretend that I am your friend."

Piotr looked at the carafe. The water in it moved very slightly, because his hands were on the table and the table was not steady, or he was not.

"I don't know anything," he said.

"You know everything. You live in the same house as the man. You hear his telephone calls. You know who comes to the rectory at night." The major opened the folder at last and turned a page, and the paper made a dry sound. "Let us begin with the twenty-eighth of November. A Sunday. There is a sermon. Somebody in the congregation takes notes. I have the notes. I want to know what he said after the sermon, in the sacristy, to the two men who came to see him."

"They came to see him about a funeral."

"Whose funeral?"

"An old woman's. A parishioner."

"What was her name?"

"I don't remember."

"You don't remember the name of a parishioner whose funeral was arranged three weeks ago."

"She was very old. She had no family."

"And the men who came about the funeral. Were they from the security service?"

Piotr looked at the table.

"Father Nowicki," the major said, gently, "I already know they were from the security service. Two of my colleagues. One of them wrote a report. I am not asking you to tell me who they were. I am asking you whether the rector knew who they were when he spoke with them, and what he said, and whether he was afraid. That last question is the interesting one. Was he afraid?"

"No," Piotr said. And then, because it was the truth and because the truth was the only thing he had that was worth anything in that room: "He was not afraid. He was angry. He is always a little angry after he has been frightened, and he was frightened before, and then he was angry."

Major Zawistowski wrote that down. He wrote for a long time. Then he closed the folder and tied the tape and put his pen in his pocket.

"Thank you," he said. "That is enough for tonight. You will sleep here. In the morning a man will come with a statement for you to sign, and it will say that you have told me what you told me voluntarily, and that no pressure was applied to you, and it will be true, as these things go. Then you will go home." He stood up and put on his jacket, which had been hanging on the back of the chair. "One more thing. Do not tell the rector about this conversation. Not because it would harm you. Because it would harm him. He is shortly to be offered a great deal, and a young priest's confession in the middle of it would be a stone in his shoe, and he is going to have enough stones." He paused at the door. "I have been doing this for twenty-five years, Father. May I give you some advice? Not as an officer. As a man."

"Yes."

"Whatever you sign tomorrow, sign it in pencil."

He went out. The lock turned. Piotr sat in the chair with his hands flat on the table and looked at the portrait of Dzierżyński, and after a while he began to shake, and he could not stop.

They let him go at nine in the morning.

The corridor was full of people who did not look at him. He walked down the stairs because he could not bring himself to get into the elevator with the guard, and at the bottom there was a vestibule with a rubber mat and a glass door and beyond the glass the street, white with snow, and a woman standing on the step with her hands in her pockets.

"Father Nowicki," Halina said.

He stopped. His face did a thing he could not control. "Comrade Sowa."

"Come. The car is around the corner." She took his arm. Her grip was firm and impersonal, a nurse's grip. "Walk normally. Do not look back. There is a man behind the glass who is enjoying this."

They walked to the corner, past the newspaper kiosk and the queue at the butcher's, and the car was there, black and steaming, and the driver got out and opened the door and did not look at either of them. Piotr got in. The leather was cold. Halina got in beside him, and the car pulled away, and Piotr discovered that he was crying, which was mortifying, and that he could not stop, which was worse.

"I signed," he said. "I signed the statement. I told them about the table tennis and I told them that he was not afraid and that he was angry. I told them. I gave him the names of the boys in the club. He wrote the names down. I gave him the names, and he wrote them down, and then he said 'thank you,' and then I signed the paper, and it was all true, every word of it, that is the thing, it was all true—"

"I know," she said.

"I would have given him more. If he had asked for more, I would have given him more. He did not even have to threaten my mother twice. Once was enough. One mention of her heart and I would have given him the cardinal."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have been in that room," Halina said. "They do not beat you any more, not in the first week. They give you tea." She was looking out of the window at the passing shops. "You are not a coward, Father Piotr. You are a man of twenty-six who has discovered what he is made of, and it is not steel, and almost nobody is made of steel, and the ones who are, are usually worse men than you. Now listen to me. You will go home and you will sleep, and then you will tell your rector what you did. Not because you owe it to him. Because if you do not tell him, they will own you for the rest of your life. They will come back in a year, and in five years, and each time they will want a little more, and each time the thing you are protecting will be a little smaller. I have watched it happen to better men than both of us. Tell him."

"I can't."

"Then you will be their man until they are finished with you, and then you will be nobody's." She turned and looked at him. Her face was pale and tired and quite without pity, and something in it was kind. "I got you out of that building this morning by telling the major's superior that the arrest had not been sanctioned by the procurator, which happens to be true, and that if the priest was held another day I would raise it at the department meeting on Monday and ask who was authorizing arrests of clergy without paperwork in the week that the Office for Religious Affairs is deciding the episcopal nomination. That is what I did. It was not mercy. It was leverage. Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then use it. Tell him."

The car stopped at the corner of Brzeska and Wileńska, because she did not want the whole street to see her bring the curate home in an official car. Piotr got out. He stood in the snow and looked back at her through the open door.

"Comrade Sowa—"

"Halina," she said. "My name is Halina."

"Halina." He swallowed. "Why did you do it?"

She was quiet for a moment. The engine ran. The driver looked straight ahead.

"Because I have been in that room," she said, "and nobody came for me."

She pulled the door shut, and the car went away up Brzeska Street, and Piotr stood in the road with the snow falling on his shoulders and his cassock wet to the hem.

Rydel saw Janusz on Friday the twelfth of December, at ten in the morning, in the office on the third floor that had a window, because Rydel rated a window.

He was standing at it when Janusz was shown in, looking down at the courtyard where the cars were parked in a row, and he did not turn around at once. The office was warm and smelled of tobacco and floor polish. On the desk were three folders, a glass of tea going cold, a photograph of a girl of about twelve with her hair in plaits, and a small bronze of a horse. On the wall, a map of Warsaw and a calendar with a photograph of the Palace of Culture and the words *Budujemy socjalizm*.

"Father Wilga. Sit down." He turned. He was a heavy man with soft hands and heavy lids, and he was smoking, and there was something almost apologetic in the way he stubbed out the cigarette before he sat down. "You are punctual. I like punctuality. It is the most underrated virtue."

"I was told ten o'clock."

"By comrade Sowa, no doubt." Rydel smiled. "I am sorry. I should say Halina. She has asked me to call her Halina now, as though we were at a dinner party." He opened the topmost folder. "You know why you are here."

"I know what you want."

"That is not the same thing. Let me put it in plain language, because I find that plain language saves everyone a great deal of time. On the nineteenth of December, the Office for Religious Affairs will meet to consider the nomination of a suffragan bishop for this archdiocese. The Primate has put forward a name. The name is yours. The office will either consent or refuse, and its decision will be transmitted to the curia, and then the newspapers will have something to say about it, and by the New Year you will either be a bishop or you will be a parish priest with a great deal of explaining to do." He tapped the folder. "I can tell you which of those two things will happen. I am, in fact, the man who decides which of those two things will happen, although of course I will do it in consultation with my colleagues, and my colleagues will do what I recommend."

"And the recommendation depends on the Commission of Priests."

"The recommendation depends on a piece of paper. It is a short piece of paper. It says that you support the work of the Commission of Priests for the reconciliation of Church and State, that you recognize the leading role of the Party in the construction of socialism in Poland, and that you will endeavour, in your pastoral work, to promote peace and the unity of our people. That is all. Three sentences. You may write them yourself if you prefer. I have known priests to improve on my version."

"And if I sign it?"

"Then you are a bishop. The file is closed. The newspaper story is not written. And you will be, I think, an unusually good bishop. You will preach, you will build, you will teach, and in twenty years you will be a cardinal, and in thirty you will be dead and they will name a street after you, and nobody will remember a piece of paper in 1958." He said it without irony, as a man reading a timetable. "This is not a trap, Father. It is a door. It is the only door in the wall, and it is open, and it will be closed on the nineteenth."

"And if I do not sign?"

"Then on the twentieth the office refuses you. And in the new year, one or two newspapers will publish material from a German police file of 1943 about a young man in Lublin who was arrested and who was released after three days, and who was released because he gave the Germans the address of a Home Army hideout in which three men were killed, one of whom was a boy of seventeen named Tadeusz Kowal." Rydel did not raise his voice. "It will be printed as the story of a traitor whom the Church attempted to make a bishop. It will be printed in *Trybuna Ludu*, and in *Żołnierz Wolności*, and it will be read aloud at party meetings in every factory in this city, and the men in the Nowotki works who listened to you in November will be told that the priest who talked about the truth was a man who talked to the Gestapo. And I will not have to do anything else. I will not have to arrest you. You will do the rest yourself, and it will take about six weeks."

The radiator clicked. Janusz looked at the photograph of the girl on the desk. She had a ribbon in her hair that did not match her dress, and she was squinting into the sun, and she was laughing.

"Why the commission?" he said. "You have bishops. You have Bochenek. He builds churches with your money and his Party secretary's wife comes to his Christmas Mass. Why do you need me?"

"Because Bochenek is a practical man and the young people do not listen to him." Rydel stubbed out his cigarette and immediately lit another, which was a habit Janusz had seen in men who were afraid of something they could not name. "Do you know what my daughter asked me last month? She is twelve. She asked me why the priest on the radio in the old days used to say that the Pope was a prisoner, and whether it was true, and if it was true, why nobody let him out. I told her it was a figure of speech. She looked at me as though I had told her the moon was made of cheese. She goes to the church on Miodowa with her aunt, did you know that? My sister-in-law takes her. I could stop it. I have not stopped it." He tapped ash into the brass tray. "That is what I am dealing with, Father. Not the cardinal. The cardinal I can negotiate with. That is what I am dealing with." He pointed the cigarette at Janusz. "You are the man who could talk to my daughter. And that is precisely why you must be either with us or destroyed, because a man who can talk to children about the truth is more dangerous than a man who can talk to bishops about politics."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe it every morning when I shave."

Janusz was silent. He thought about the cell in Lublin and the smell of the corridor and the way the light had come in under the door. He thought about the boy he had been at twenty, who had believed that a man could be measured by the purity of his intention, and who had discovered in three days that he could not. He thought about Halina in the parish hall saying *I forgive you* as though she were laying down a stone she had carried up a hill for fifteen years. He thought about the cardinal's warm study and the apples and the notebook, and about his old confessor, Father Ambroży, in his room by the garden at the Jesuit house on Rakowiecka Street, who was seventy and dying and knew it and had told him once that a bishop who is not afraid is a fool.

"I will not sign it," he said.

Rydel did not look surprised. He looked tired, in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"You understand what I have just told you."

"I understand it."

"You understand that the file is not a threat I am making. It is a file. It exists. It has existed since 1943, and since 1945 it has had a companion, and between the two of them they will end you and a woman I have known for thirteen years whom I am fond of, in my way." He said it almost gently. "You are not refusing me, Father. You are refusing her. Have you thought about that?"

"I have thought about nothing else for three weeks."

"And?"

"She has told me not to decide it for her."

Rydel laughed. It was a short, ugly laugh, and he cut it off and looked annoyed with himself.

"That," he said, "is the most Halina thing I have ever heard." He opened a drawer and took out a sheet of paper that was already typed. "Here is the statement. Take it with you. If you sign it, bring it to me by Wednesday the seventeenth at six in the evening. If you do not sign it, do not bring it, and do not come to this office again, because the next time we meet will be across a table, and I will not be able to offer you tea." He pushed it across the desk. "Father. One thing, man to man, off the record. Do you actually believe all of it? The resurrection, the virgin, the angels? Or is it just that you have no other trade?"

Janusz picked up the sheet of paper and folded it once and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

"I believe in God," he said. "Some days more than others. And I have seen what happens to men who believe in nothing."

"The Party believes in something."

"The Party believes in the future. It is a very great thing to believe in, and it justifies almost anything, which is the trouble with it. A man who believes in the future will put his friends in prison for the sake of the men who are not born yet." Janusz stood. "I have been in a room where a man explained to me that the future justified the present. He was a German. He was very reasonable. He had a folder." He put on his hat. "Good morning, comrade Rydel."

"It is 'citizen,' now," Rydel said. "Since October they have decided that 'comrade' sounds intimate. Good morning, Father."

Halina was waiting for him at the bottom of the stairs, in the vestibule, by the rubber mat.

"Well?"

"I am to bring it in by Wednesday or not at all."

She closed her eyes for a moment. Then she opened them and nodded, once, like a woman who has been told the result of a test she already knew she had failed.

"Come on," she said. "There is a café on Krucza that has not been closed yet, and I have an hour before Kaczmarczyk wants his notes, and I am going to have a glass of something and you are going to sit there and drink tea and be unbearable."

They went to the café. It was a small place with steamed-up windows and four tables and a waiter with a mustache who knew her, and they sat in the corner and she drank a small glass of vodka and he drank tea and they did not talk about the nineteenth of December. She told him about the department meeting and the argument over the tractor article. He told her about the boiler and Pani Genowefa's war with the coal depot. She laughed twice. He memorized it, the way a man memorizes a road he is not going to travel again.

Then she said, "You will not sign."

"No."

"Then it will be in the papers."

"Probably."

"And I will be in the papers."

"You will not. It is one thing to print a Gestapo report about a priest. It is another to print that a Party official signed a false denunciation in 1945. That is a story about them." He turned his glass around on the table. "Rydel knows it. That is why the file has been sitting in his drawer for thirteen years and has not been used to advance him one grade. The moment he uses it, he is the man who used it. He will give the German report to the newspapers and keep the second page for the day he needs it, and the day he needs it will be the day he is in trouble, and by then it will be worth more than it is worth now. That is what I have been thinking about for three weeks. I am not a politician. I am a priest who has been blackmailed by professionals and has learned the trade."

Halina set down her glass. "You have become frighteningly good at this."

"I have had a good teacher."

"Don't."

"I meant the cardinal."

She laughed again, and it surprised her, and she looked at him over the rim of the glass with an expression he had not seen on her face since 1939.

They parted on the street outside. It was snowing again, thick and slow.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday."

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"When it is over — whatever it is — come and find me. Not as a priest. Not as anything. Just knock on the door."

"I will knock on the door."

She nodded and walked away toward Marszałkowska, and the snow took her in thirty paces.

He went back to the rectory and found the curate sitting on the bottom step of the stairs with his head in his hands.

Piotr told him everything. It took forty minutes, and he cried twice, and at one point he got up and went to the window and stood there with his back to the room for a long moment before he could go on. He gave Janusz the names he had given to the major. He described the tea and the photograph and the maternal heart and the pen lying parallel to the edge of the desk. When he had finished he sat with his hands between his knees and said, "Now you will send me away."

Janusz got up and went into the kitchen and put the kettle on the ring. He took down two glasses and the tin where Genowefa kept the tea. He heard in his memory the voice of the old priest in the room by the garden: *You are commanded to love.*

"You will write to the provincial," he said, "and ask to be transferred to a parish in the provinces. Not because I am sending you. Because you cannot stay here now, and you know it, and it would be cruel to make you say it twice. I will write to the provincial too, and I will tell him that you are a good priest who was frightened by professionals at the age of twenty-six, and that I have known better men to break in worse rooms for less."

Piotr's face crumpled. "You are not going to forgive me?"

"I am going to forgive you every day for the rest of my life, because that is what it costs." Janusz put the glass in front of him. "It is not a feeling, Piotr. It is a practice. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Drink your tea."

They sat in the kitchen while the light went out of the afternoon. At half past four the Angelus rang and they both stood up out of habit and said it, and Piotr's voice broke on the second line and he got through it anyway, and Janusz thought: *That is what a Church is. Not the building. The two of us in a kitchen, saying it badly, and getting to the end.*

On Sunday he preached on John the Baptist.

The church was full. It was the third Sunday of Advent, and the rose vestments came out of the cupboard where they smelled of mothballs, and the choir sang *Rorate caeli* with more enthusiasm than accuracy. In the fourth row the two men from the security service sat with their hats on their knees. Pani Genowefa had put them in the fourth row deliberately, as a hostess seating difficult guests where she could see them.

"John the Baptist is in prison," Janusz said. "He has been put there by a king who was afraid of him, and he sends two of his disciples to ask Jesus a question. And the question is not 'When will you free me?' The question is: 'Are you he who is to come, or shall we look for another?' That is the question of a man in a cell. It is not a question about politics. It is a question about whether the thing he has given his life to is real."

He looked down at the front pew, where the widows sat in their black coats with their hands folded, and at the back, where the workers stood because they had come in late and would not push forward, and at the fourth row, where the two men were writing.

"And the answer that comes back," he said, "is not a liberation. It is a list. The blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. It is a list of things that have happened quietly, in villages, to people whose names nobody wrote down. It is not the answer a man in a cell would choose. It is the answer he is given."

He paused. The church was very quiet. The candles moved.

"On Wednesday," he said, "a decision will be made in an office in this city about this parish and about your priest. I am not going to tell you what it is, because I do not know, and because it is not the gospel. But I am going to tell you what I have learned this Advent, and it is this. A man may be destroyed. A parish may be dispersed. A bishop may be refused and a file may be opened and a name may be printed in a newspaper in letters a centimetre high. None of that is the answer to the question. The answer to the question is that in this parish, this year, the blind have been visited and the lame have been carried and the poor have been fed and the children have been taught, quietly, by people whose names nobody is writing down. If they take me tomorrow, that remains. It remains because it is not mine. It is His."

He stepped down. The organ began. The two men in the fourth row closed their notebooks and put on their hats and went out before the last verse, and the draft from the door made the candles lean.

On Wednesday the seventeenth of December, at six in the evening, Janusz Wilga did not go to Rakowiecka Street. He went to the church instead and heard confessions until eight, and then he went into the sacristy and took the folded sheet of paper out of his coat and burned it in the little iron stove, and watched it curl and blacken, and thought that it was the first thing he had burned in that stove that had given him any pleasure.

# Chapter Eight: The Fourth Sunday of Advent

On Thursday the eighteenth, a letter came from the curia by messenger, on paper so heavy that Janusz could feel the weave of it through the envelope.

It was three sentences long, in the cardinal's own hand, which the secretary had clearly been instructed to forgo any attempt to type.

*I am told what you have decided. I will not pretend that I am not angry, but I have been angry before and it has not killed me. Come to the church on Saturday at five o'clock, and do not speak of this to anyone.*

That was all. There was no signature, only the little cross and the name, and the ink had blotted at the tail of the *n*.

Janusz read it in the kitchen with a cup of tea going cold in front of him. He read it twice, then folded it and put it in the breviary and then took it out again, because it seemed wrong to put it where he kept the photograph.

On Friday the nineteenth of December, the Office for Religious Affairs met at eleven o'clock in the morning in a room in the building on Krucza Street, and at four in the afternoon a clerk typed a decision onto a form and a messenger carried it to the curia, and by six o'clock it was known in three offices and four newspaper desks and the archbishop's palace. *The nomination of the Reverend Janusz Wilga to the titular see of A... is not confirmed. The office will consider the candidacy of the Reverend Canon Zygmunt Bochenek.*

They did not bother to give a reason. There was a line on the form for a reason, and the reason on the form was *lack of political suitability*, which was the standard phrase and meant nothing, and everyone who read it knew what it meant.

Janusz heard at a quarter past six, from Pani Genowefa, who had heard it from the woman who did the curia's laundry, who had heard it from the cardinal's cook. She came into the kitchen wringing her hands and told him, and then she burst into tears, and then she was furious, and then she went into the pantry and slammed the door.

That was the strange thing about the next morning: how little it resembled the disaster he had imagined. He had imagined the ceiling coming down. Instead the sky was clear and very cold, the boiler was repaired at last, the church was warm, and at seven o'clock there were thirty people at Mass, and afterwards three of them shook his hand and said nothing about the bishopric and one old woman, Pani Zofia, who had been in the parish since 1948 and had never once called him anything but *ksiądz* in the way one says a word one has decided to trust, pressed a paper bag of gingerbread into his hand and said, "For your name day. It is late, but the oven is late too."

He understood the gingerbread for what it was: a declaration. He put it in his coat pocket and wore it all day.

The cardinal came on the Saturday before the fourth Sunday, at five o'clock exactly, in a black greatcoat with a fur collar, with the young secretary at his elbow and a second priest behind, and the church was empty because he had asked for it to be empty except for one man.

He went and knelt in the front pew, the one where the widows sat, and he stayed there for a long time with his hands over his face, and Janusz knelt in the second pew and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say and because the cardinal had not come to talk.

After a quarter of an hour the cardinal got up with a grunt — he was not a young man, and his knees were bad, and the cold of the church got into everything — and went into the sacristy and sat down on the bench by the cupboard and looked at the floor.

"You have ruined my episcopate," he said.

"Eminence—"

"That is the wrong thing to answer. That is the thing a man answers when he wants to be told that he has not ruined it." The cardinal rubbed his knee. "I do not mean the archdiocese. I mean my episcopate, the government of the Church in Poland, the arrangement I made with them in 1956, brick by brick. They have refused you, which is a humiliation with my name on it, and they have accepted Bochenek, which is a humiliation with my name on it, and they have done both in the same afternoon, and the newspapers will be given a lesson about what happens to a priest who is too popular. That is my situation. It is not worse than the situation I was in in 1953, and I survived 1953, and I will survive this. But I am going to be very disagreeable for a month, and I would like one person to know that it is not because I have lost my nerve."

"I am sorry, Eminence."

"Are you?"

"No."

The cardinal looked up. There was something in his face that had not been there in the warm study on Miodowa: exhaustion, and underneath it a hard amusement.

"Then say what you are."

"I am sorry that it costs you. I am not sorry that I did it."

"That is the correct answer, and it has cost about as much as I expected." The cardinal got up and went to the little stove and held his hands out to it. "Bochenek came to see me this morning. He asked for the appointment, and then he asked whether he might be consecrated by you."

Janusz stared at him. "By me?"

"He has a curious sense of propriety. He said that his elevation would be blamed on you, and that he did not want it said that he had robbed a man he had never had the courage to argue with." The cardinal turned around. "I told him no. It would be a scandal and a farce. But I thought you should know the kind of men you have been competing against."

"I am not competing."

"You are not a bishop. That is a fact. It is not a virtue, and it is not a martyrdom. You are a parish priest who refused an appointment and has made a great deal of noise, and the noise is not finished. I have come because there is a thing I must ask you, and I have to ask it here, in this church, and you must not lie to me." He came back and stood over Janusz. "Do you intend to speak about the file?"

Janusz did not answer at once. The stove ticked.

"Yes," he said.

"When?"

"Tomorrow. At the eleven o'clock Mass."

"To the whole parish."

"To whoever is there. And to the newspapers, afterward, if they ask me, because otherwise they will print the German report alone, and a German report without a man's own voice is a weapon. I have seen what they did to Father K. I will not be a weapon."

The cardinal was silent for a long moment. Outside, a car engine turned over and idled, and one of the men in the street said something and was answered and laughed.

"If you do this," the cardinal said, "I cannot defend you."

"I know."

"If the newspapers print the Gestapo report on Monday and you have spoken on Sunday, I will be asked at the Christmas dinner of the diplomatic corps what I think of it, and I will say that a man's wartime conduct is not the measure of his priesthood, and that the Church does not abandon priests to newspapers. I will say that, and it will be true, and it will also be insufficient, and every priest in this city who spent the occupation in a cellar will be told by his church council that he is not safe either." His voice was quiet and hard. "You will not be a martyr. You will be a warning."

"I know that too."

"Do you know what will be left of you in this parish in February? A priest can be an example and still be lonely. They will come to your Mass and then they will not walk home with you in the street, because the man who walks with you is written down. Your youth club will be closed by the office before the month is out, and the school catechists will be reassigned because their names are on the same permit, and the boys who play table tennis with you will be told in their factories that they have been associating with a traitor and a reactionary, and some of them will believe it, and some of them will not, and the ones who believe it will not come back."

"I know."

"Then you know more than most men in the Church." The cardinal looked at him. "I am going to move you."

"Eminence—"

"Not as a punishment. As arithmetic. In February there will be a chaplaincy vacant in a parish in Wyszków, eighty kilometres up the Bug, in the middle of the country, where nobody reads *Express Wieczorny* and the district militia commander goes to Christmas Mass because his mother is buried in the parish cemetery. You will go there and you will hear confessions and teach Latin to the boys and be nobody, and in five or ten years, when this has been forgotten, you will come back. That is the offer. You may refuse it and stay here, and I will not force you out of this parish, but I would think less of you."

"I would rather stay."

"There are two men sitting on the ground when the storm comes. The one who stands is killed. The one who lies down lives and gets up in the spring. Both of them are brave, but only one of them knows the shape of the weather." The cardinal pulled on his gloves. "You are not required to be the man who stands, Janusz. It is not in the vows."

"And Bochenek? Is he the man who lies down?"

"Bochenek is a man in a ditch with a church on his back. He will hold the diocese together in the lean years, and he will build twenty churches, and he will never once say a thing that costs him anything, and at the end of it there will be more Poles at Mass because of him than because of you. God keeps accounts in a currency I do not understand." He walked to the door. "You will not answer me now. Answer me in February."

"Eminence."

The cardinal stopped.

"I am going to say it tomorrow," Janusz said. "Not because it is brave. Because a man in a cell gave them a hideout in 1943 and they have been feeding me ever since, a spoonful a year, and I am not going to die with their food in my mouth."

The cardinal looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Janusz had not expected: he nodded, once, the way one soldier nods to another.

"Then preach it well," he said. "A man's confession is a poor thing if it is badly said."

He went out. Ten minutes later, when Janusz put his head around the church door, two black cars had drawn up at the bottom of Brzeska Street and four men in gray coats were standing in the cold with their hands in their pockets, waiting for the cardinal to finish. The door of the sacristy closed behind him and the cold came in and then the stove beat it back. Janusz stood in the sacristy for a while. Then he sat down and took out his breviary and put it on his knees and did not open it.

The fourth Sunday of Advent was the twenty-first of December, and the church was full.

Not as it had been in November — there were no flowers, and the men from the works came in their work coats because the week had been short, and the choir was thin because three of the sopranos had the grippe. But it was full. The two men from the security service were in the fourth row again, with their hats on their knees and their notebooks on their hats. There was a young man with a camera at the back door of the sacristy, and Pani Genowefa had tried to stop him and failed.

Janusz came out of the sacristy in violet vestments that had been kept in camphor, and he stood at the foot of the altar and said the prayers. Then he climbed the pulpit and put both his hands flat on the rail and said:

"Before I read the Gospel, I am going to tell you something."

The church quieted. Somebody at the back said *szszsz*, and was silent.

"In August of 1943 I was arrested in Lublin by the Gestapo. I was twenty years old. I was a courier for the Home Army, and I was carrying a message, and I was taken to the prison on the Castle Hill, and I was put in a cell, and over three days they broke my fingers and burned my feet and put a wire on me, and on the third day they showed me a photograph of my mother and told me what they would do to her.

"I told them what they wanted. I gave them the location of a hideout in the forest near my village. Three men were killed there on the fourteenth of August. One of them was seventeen years old and his name was Tadeusz Kowal, and I had known him since he was born, and I had loved his sister since I was sixteen. I did not know he was there. That is true, and it is not an excuse, because the hideout was a house and a house has people in it, and I gave it to the Gestapo knowing that whoever was in it would be killed."

He stopped. There was no sound in the church at all. The old women in the front pew had turned to stone. The man with the camera had lowered it.

"Since that day," Janusz said, "I have said Mass, heard confessions, buried the dead and blessed marriages, and every one of those things has been done by a man carrying this over his shoulder. I have confessed it to God and to the Church and to my superiors. I have done penance. In the last month I have done it also to the woman whose brother it was, and she has forgiven me, which I did not deserve and did not ask for.

"You will read it in *Express Wieczorny* tomorrow. The newspaper has the German file, and it will print the German file, and it will say that the Church tried to make a bishop out of a traitor. So I thought that you should hear it from me first, in this church, in my own words, because you are my people and you have a right to know who is standing at this altar. It is not the office on Krucza Street that has the right to say who I am. It is not me either, and it is certainly not the newspaper.

"The cardinal has offered to move me to a parish in the country in February, and I have not yet given him my answer. I am giving it now, and the answer is yes. It is a wise decision and I do not complain of it. Before February, though, some of you will want to know whether you can come to Communion from these hands. I will not answer that for you. You must answer it. If your answer is no, then go to Saint Florian or to the chapel of the Sisters and the sacrament will be given to you by a better priest. There will be a Mass here at seven every morning in January either way. The church does not depend on me being a good man. That is the whole point of a church.

"And one thing more. I have been in this parish ten years, and in that time I have met men and women who came to me with worse than what I have just told you, weeping, at the point of despair, certain that God had finished with them. I have told them all the same thing, and it has been easy to say because I would not say it about myself: that God's mercy is not a reward for people who kept their hands clean. It is for people who did not. I have been able to say it because I wanted it to be true. Now I am asking you to believe it for me, and I find that I cannot ask the way I used to ask. So I will ask it badly and then I will get on with the Mass."

He came down from the pulpit.

The organist began the introit, and the choir did not come in on time, and the organ went on alone for four bars and then the old women's voices came up after it, thin and late, singing *Rorate caeli desuper*.

At the reading, Zbyszek Malinowski, who was serving the Mass and had washed his hands and combed his hair for it, stood at the lectern in his cassock and read the Gospel and got to the end of it and then did not go back to his place. He stood there. He was twenty-two years old and his collar was too big, and he stood at the lectern with the book open in front of him and his face working.

Then he said, into the microphone, which the parish was not supposed to use except for the homily: "I want to say something."

There was a small commotion at the back. Janusz did not get up. He stayed on his knees at the sedilia and looked at the boy.

"I was at that church in November," Zbyszek said. "I heard what he said about Brunon. Half the men in the works heard it. And now he has told us that he was a traitor, and I want to say that I do not care." His voice was shaking. "My father was in the AK and he came out of it in 1945 and was arrested in 1948 and they held him for eleven months and let him go and he never told us why. He would not talk about it. He says he does not remember. Nobody remembers. That is what this country runs on, Father. Nobody remembers. And I am twenty-two years old and I have been told my whole life that I have to understand why the men on the other side did what they did, and I understand nothing, because nobody will tell me anything." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I do not care what you did in 1943. You were twenty and I have never been beaten for anything in my life. I would have told them everything. I would have told them about the river, I would have told them about my mother, I would have told them anything at all. I do not want a priest who has never been afraid. I want one who has."

He sat down in the front pew with the cassock over his knees, and the church was silent, and then an old woman in the third row began to clap, and stopped herself immediately, and put her hand over her mouth, appalled.

Janusz got up and went to the altar and began the Creed.

After Mass he stood at the door as he always did. Some people came past with their hands out and their eyes wet and said nothing. Some people said, "It is all right, Father." One man, a railwayman of about sixty who had been in the parish longer than Janusz had been alive, came past him, stopped, took off his hat, and said: "My son was with the AL near Radom. Three of them were taken." Then he put his hat on and went out into the street without another word.

And one man went past him with his head down and did not shake his hand, and Janusz knew him, and knew what he had lost — a brother, in the forests, in 1944 — and did not call after him.

By two o'clock the church was empty and the parish hall was full, because Father Piotr had put out the tea and Pani Genowefa had cut up the last of the gingerbread, and somebody said that the press would be there in the morning, and somebody else said that the office had already closed the youth club, and somebody said that this was 1956 all over again and the thaw had ended.

Halina came at four, when the light was going and the streetlamps came on at six in a city that was always short of coal.

She came into the empty church and found him sitting in the front pew with his coat on and his hat on the seat beside him. She sat down next to him.

"I heard," she said. "The whole of Praga has heard. The caretaker at the department heard. He is a Party member of thirty years and he said to me in the lift, 'That priest has more courage than the whole Party committee.' The door was open. He said it in the corridor."

"You should have a care for your lift."

"I have spent all day having a care." She was quiet. "I read the statement about the office's decision. I know about Bochenek. I know about Wyszków."

"They did not tell me Wyszków. I told them."

She turned her head and looked at him. He could see her face in profile, the tired line at the mouth, the cheek still held like a girl's.

"You gave them the name of the town," she said. "You are the only priest in Poland who is negotiating his own exile."

"I am not negotiating. I am going." He looked at the altar. "I have been here ten years. The boys who played table tennis in 1949 have children now. It is enough."

"It is not enough."

"No. It is not enough. But it is what there is."

They sat for a while. The church was cold. The red lamp burned. Outside, somebody was scraping snow off the steps and somebody else was arguing about a bucket.

"Halina," he said. "Tomorrow the paper comes out."

"I know."

"With my name in it, and the German words, and a photograph of the document. And on the same page, if Rydel is clever, something about the 1945 report. Not the report itself. A hint. A sentence in a box: 'The investigation into Wilga's war record is continuing.'"

"He is not clever in that way. He is clever in the way that keeps the page in his drawer." She shook her head. "Do not think about Rydel. He has what he has. It is not as much as he thinks."

"What will you do?"

"What will I do." She considered it. "On Monday I will go to my office and do my work. On Tuesday I will take the early train to Otwock, because in Otwock there is a woman of fifty-two who works in a school kitchen and has two grown children, and she has been told for thirteen years that her husband died of a heart attack in a cell in Lublin, and she has never once been told the truth. I am going to tell her." She said it evenly. "And then I am going to come back and tell the Control Commission what I did, and they will expel me, and I will lose this job and this salary and the flat on Marszałkowska, and I will not be arrested, because the service does not arrest its own for the killings of 1945. It buries them."

"Halina."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me not to. We are past that. We have been past it since the parish hall."

He looked at her. She had not once, in all of it, asked him to save her. He understood, suddenly and with a kind of awe, that she never would.

"What is her name?" he asked.

"Berezowska. Zofia Berezowska. She lives on the Otwock road with her son in the flat, and the daughter is in a technical school in Warsaw, and the son works at the railway depot. He was nine when his father was killed. He will remember his father the way a nine-year-old remembers a man — the smell of his coat, the way he lifted him up." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading a file. "I have known her address since 1949. I have had it in my head for nine years. I have walked past the end of that road twice."

"And what will you say?"

"That I signed the paper. That is all. There is nothing else to say."

"Will you say why?"

"I will say that I was twenty-three and frightened and that I thought I was saving a man's life, and that I did not know what they would do to her husband." She looked at her gloves. "It will not matter to her why. It will matter that somebody has said it out loud. That is what the file is. It is a silence with a stamp on it."

Janusz put his hand out and took hers. Their hands lay together on the cold wood of the pew.

"On Wednesday," he said, "it will be Christmas Eve."

"I know."

"Maryla has invited me. She has invited you. She has been inviting you for eleven years and you have gone to her for eleven years, and this year she has invited your priest, and you have both been very careful not to say so."

Halina turned and stared at him. "She wrote to you?"

"She telephoned the rectory on Thursday. She told me that she has a carp and a great deal of cabbage and that a priest at Christmas Eve in a rectory with a housekeeper is a scandal of loneliness. She said—" he paused, "she said that I was to come at six and bring nothing and not to argue."

"Maryla," Halina said, with enormous feeling, "is a great and terrible woman."

"She is a doctor. They have no fear."

She laughed. It was a small, sudden, exhausted laugh, and it went up into the roof of the church and came back changed. And then she did something that she had not done in the whole of the autumn: she leaned her head against his shoulder, and closed her eyes, and stayed there, and he sat absolutely still, and the lamp burned, and outside the snow began again.

They did not speak. There was nothing to be gained by speaking.

After a while she sat up and put her gloves on and blew her nose and said, "I have to go and write a letter to a widow, and I have been putting it off for nine years, and now I find that I am going to do it in the morning rather than at night, which is the same cowardice in a better suit."

"Halina."

"Yes."

"When you come back from Otwock," he said, "come here first. Not to the rectory. Here. The church is open until seven."

"I will come here first."

She went out, and he heard her heels go down the stone aisle and stop at the door and the door open, and the noise of the street came in for a moment and then the door closed and the noise stopped.

Janusz sat in the pew until the sacristan came and asked, very gently, whether the Father would like the lights left on.

On Monday morning, *Express Wieczorny* printed the story on the front page under the headline:

*A Gestapo Document Accuses: The Bishop the Church Wanted.*

They printed the German report in facsimile with the black eagle and the swastika at the top, and beside it a photograph of the Church of the Transfiguration with an arrow pointing at the door, and a short article which said that the Church had attempted to place a former collaborator in the episcopate and that the office had properly refused. The article ended: *Father Wilga could not be reached for comment.*

At the bottom of the page there was a box in smaller type: *See also page 4: "I gave them the hideout," says priest.*

On page four they had printed the sermon.

The editors had had it from the young man with the camera, and also, in the middle of the night, from a parishioner who had telephoned the night desk, and the night editor had decided that it was a better story than the German document, because it was a confession, and confessions sold papers. They had cut it. They had introduced it with two lines of editorial describing the parish as *well known for its reactionary clergy*. But they had printed it, in quotation marks, almost whole.

Halina read it on the tram. She read it twice, standing up, with her bag under her arm. When she got off at Marszałkowska she stood in the snow for a moment and laughed — a single, hard, delighted sound that made the woman beside her move away.

Rydel telephoned her at nine.

"I have seen the paper," he said.

"So have I."

"That was not the plan."

"No."

"He confessed first. Do you understand what he has done? He has taken a rifle out of my hands and given it to me butt first. There is no blackmail in the world that works on a man who has confessed in the pulpit of his own church. I have a file, and the file is public, and the public has heard the man in the file say the same thing in a better voice." He was silent. "The office is furious. They think I mismanaged it."

"What will you do?"

"I will do what I always do. I will write a report explaining that the outcome was in fact the desired outcome and that my handling of it was correct in every particular. And then I will go home and my daughter will ask me why the paper says the priest was a traitor, and I will tell her that the paper is not always right." He put down the telephone without saying goodbye.

Halina sat at her desk and looked at the wall.

At eleven o'clock Kaczmarczyk put his head around the door and said, "The First Secretary has read the sermon. He has asked that no further action be taken on the Wilga matter at this time." He looked at her steadily. "You will understand, Halina, that this is not mercy. It is a decision that the story has been used up."

"I understand."

"Good." He started to close the door, and then opened it again. "The paper is on my desk. I read the sermon twice. My wife's uncle was a priest in Kielce." He shut the door.

On Tuesday the twenty-third of December, at half past six in the morning, Halina Sowa got on a train at the Warszawa Wschodnia station, in the dark, with a handbag containing a letter, a photograph, and a copy of an office report that had never been filed, and rode south-east along the river to Otwock.

# Chapter Nine: The Vigil

Otwock was a town of pines and peeling villas and sanatoriums for people with lungs, and in December the snow lay deep under the trees and the air smelled so sharply of resin that it made Halina's eyes water as she came down the station steps.

She had been there once before, in 1951, on a ministry errand to a rest home where a writer was being dried out. She remembered nothing of it. Now she walked up the road past a fence of larch posts and a clinic with a sign saying *Zakład Leczniczy*, and turned into a street of small wooden houses with carved verandas, and stopped by a gate with the paint gone.

The house was small and well kept. There were lace curtains in the windows, and a runner of homemade carpet on the step, and a wreath of dried leaves on the door, the kind people make in the villages out of what is left in the garden.

She stood at the gate for perhaps a minute. Snow fell on the shoulders of her coat and did not melt, it was so cold. Then she pushed the gate and went up the path and knocked.

The woman who opened the door was small and upright, in a gray wool dress and an apron, with her gray hair pinned back. She had the face of a woman who has spent thirteen years being careful with her face. She looked at Halina and did not know her.

"Yes?"

"Pani Berezowska."

"Yes."

"My name is Halina Sowa. I work — I worked — at the Ministry of Internal Affairs." She had rehearsed it for a week and it came out flat and stupid. "I have come about your husband."

The woman's hand went to the door frame. She did not step back. She did not invite her in. Behind her, from the depth of the house, came the sound of a radio playing a carol, and the smell of cabbage and mushrooms and something sweet.

"What about my husband?"

"May I come in? It will take some time."

"What about my husband?"

Halina looked at her. There was no way to do this in the snow at the gate, and there was no way to do it anywhere else either.

"I signed the paper that killed him," she said. "In Lublin, in February 1945. I gave his name to the security service. I have come to tell you so. I have not come to ask you for anything."

The woman stood in the doorway. Her face did not change at all. That was the worst of it: it did not change, as though she had been waiting so long in one position that a new blow simply confirmed the shape she was already in.

"Come in," she said, "before the neighbors see you crying, and then we will close the door."

Zofia Berezowska's kitchen had a stove with two rings, a table covered with a checked cloth, a shelf of crockery, and a clock with a brass pendulum. On the wall was a crucifix, a photograph of a man in a Home Army uniform with his cap under his arm, and a small picture of the Black Madonna cut from a magazine and framed.

Halina sat down where she was told. The woman put a glass of tea in front of her and sat down opposite with her hands folded.

"Say it," she said.

So Halina said it. She said it without excusing herself, because she had rehearsed that too. She said that in February 1945 the security service had arrested Janusz Wilga, and that Rydel had brought her a report to sign, and that she had known that Wilga was a seminarian and that he had been in the Home Army, and that Rydel had told her that if she gave him a name, Wilga would be released. She said that she had given him the name of Wiktor Berezowski, whom she knew as Bór, the commander, because the whole village knew the commander's name and she had thought it would cost him an interrogation and no more. She said that he had been held for six weeks and shot in a forest near Lublin, and that she had learned of it from a colleague, and that she had never asked a single question about it in thirteen years, and that she had known the widow's address since 1949 and had walked past the end of the road twice.

"And why did you not come before?" the woman said.

"There is no answer to that which will not make you angrier than you are."

"Try."

"Because I am a coward," Halina said. "Because I had made a life out of not being the person who did it. Because every year I did not come made the next year harder, and thirteen years of not coming is not thirteen years of forgetting. It is thirteen years of knowing." She put her hands around the glass. "And because on the thirtieth of November a priest stood up in a church in Praga and told his whole parish what he had done in a cell in 1943, and I understood that I was going to have to do the same, or stop calling myself a person."

The woman looked at her for a long time. The radio in the next room finished the carol and a man's voice began to read a list of figures about coal production.

"You are a Party member," she said at last. "You have a card."

"Since 1945. Since I was twenty-three."

"And now?"

"I mean to give it back."

"And they will let you?"

"They will take it. They will be delighted to take it. Then they will decide how much of me to break, and there is a chance they will decide that the cheapest thing is to let me disappear."

"And my husband? What do they say about my husband, in your office? That he was a fascist? That he was a reactionary? That he was an enemy of the people who died of a heart attack?"

"They say he was a heart attack," Halina said. "They said it for thirteen years and they said it so well that his daughter-in-law probably believes it. That is what they are good at. It is not the killing. It is the sentence afterward."

Zofia Berezowska got up. She went to the window and stood with her back to the room, and her shoulders were quite straight, and the light came through the lace curtain and made a pattern on her apron.

"Everybody said it was a heart attack," she said. "His mother said it. My mother said it. The priest at the funeral said it. They let me have the funeral, you know: a coffin, an open coffin. There was nothing in it that I was not allowed to see. They had washed him. His hands were folded. He had a mark under his jaw where the rope had been, or where he had fallen, or where something had been done, and nobody would say, and I put a lace handkerchief over it, and I said to my son, 'Your father died of his heart.'" Her voice did not break. "My son was nine. He is a man now. He works at the depot in Warsaw. He does not come home very often. When he comes, he stands in the doorway of this kitchen and he does not sit down. He says, 'What was my father really like, Mother?' and I say, 'He was a good man, he was a soldier, he liked to sing,' and he listens to me with the face of a man who has been lied to all his life and has decided not to mention it.

"Now you come and you tell me that a woman signed the paper. That is a thing I have been waiting for. Not for the name. For the paper." She turned around. "Do you understand? I have not been able to say to my son, 'They killed him on purpose,' because I did not know. I thought perhaps they did it for no reason at all, the way weather happens. And now I know it was done with a pen and a signature, and there is a person who did it, and I want to be sick, and I want to thank you, and I want you to leave my house and I want you to have another glass of tea."

"I will have another glass of tea," Halina said.

She had it. They sat for twenty minutes. The woman asked questions and Halina answered all of them: the office, the smell of it, the cigarette, the glass of water, the man standing behind the door with a pistol because there had been a man behind the door with a pistol. She named Rydel. She described the form. She described the tea and the flat, kind voice in which she had been told that the man would be interrogated, which she had chosen to understand as would be released.

At a quarter past one the front door opened and the son came in.

He was twenty-two, a broad young man with his father's face in a photograph taken before he was born, in a railwayman's coat and a good wool scarf, and he stopped short in the kitchen doorway with his cap in his hands.

"Mother. There is a woman in the front garden gate. She left a car on the road." He looked at Halina. "Who are you?"

"Sit down, Andrzej," his mother said.

"Who is she?"

"She is the woman who signed the paper."

The son stood in the doorway. He did not sit down. Halina looked at him, and thought, with a strange detachment, that she had never in her life seen anyone come so close to killing someone with his eyes.

"You," he said.

"I have told your mother everything," Halina said. "I will tell you the same, if you want it. It is not a long story and I will not dress it up. I signed a report, in Lublin, on the twentieth of February 1945, giving your father's name to the security service. It was typed, it was formally correct, and I was told by the officer who took it that it would put an end to the questioning of another man. Your father was arrested three days later."

"And you have lived," he said. "All this time. You have lived, in an apartment, on a salary paid by the state that killed him."

"Yes."

"And now you have come to tell us so that you can feel better."

"I have come to tell you so that you know. Whether I feel better is my business and it is not going in the ground with your father." She stood up, because she was not going to be seated while he stood over her. "You may put me out of this house. You may go to the militia and tell them that a Party official has confessed to a political killing in 1945; they will laugh at you, or they will file it, and either way it will cost you something. You may write to *Życie Warszawy*. Or you may do nothing at all, which is what most people do, and I would not blame you for it."

The son looked at his mother. And Zofia Berezowska, who had been careful with her face for thirteen years, said: "Sit down, Andrzej. In this house nobody gets up from a table on Christmas Eve."

"It is not Christmas Eve, Mother. It is the twenty-third."

"It is near enough. Sit down."

He sat down. He sat down slowly, on the chair nearest the door, and put his cap on his knee, and looked at the floor.

"Tell him," his mother said. "Start at the beginning and tell him."

So Halina told it again, from the beginning, in the kitchen, in the light that came through the runner blinds, while the clock ticked and the son sat with his cap on his knee and his jaw working, and did not once look up at her, and did not once tell her to stop.

When she had finished the son said, to the floor: "Did they hurt him?"

"They interrogated him for six weeks. That is the phrase that was used to me. Six weeks is not a phrase that means nothing."

"And he told them things."

"I don't know. I have wondered. He was a commander; he had men in the forest. He would have had things to tell."

"My father would not have told them anything."

"Then it was six weeks of nothing, and he died with nothing to give them, and it cost him six weeks of pain to do it. Or he gave them something, and they killed him anyway. You will never know which, and it is not a shame either way. It is only men in cells. Nobody is themselves in a cell." She heard how harsh her voice was and did not soften it. "I am sorry. That is the truth as I know it."

The son nodded, once, still looking at the floor.

Then he said: "I have to go and see to the car, Mother. The radiator is leaking and if I leave it, it will freeze." And he got up and went out, and stood in the front garden for a long time with his back to the house, in the snow, until his mother went out to him and put her hand on his arm, and Halina, from the kitchen window, watched the two of them standing in the garden among the pines, and took her hat and coat and let herself out of the back door and away.

She came back to Warsaw in the dark, on the slow train, and sat in a compartment with three soldiers who slept with their mouths open, and looked at the black window and at her own reflection in it.

On the morning of Christmas Eve she did two things, and she did the smaller one first.

The smaller one was at the ministry. She came in at eight, before the corridors filled, and went up to the fourth floor with a sealed envelope, and put it in the tray of the Office for Religious Affairs: a copy of the factory investigation she had written and not sent on the second of December — the frayed cable, the June letter, the safety commission that had met twice and minuted nothing, the crane that had been scheduled for repair in the spring and was still lifting in October. She had typed it herself on Sunday on Maryla's borrowed machine, because the ministry's typists read what they typed. At the bottom she had written: *Respectfully submitted, H. Sowa, formerly deputy head, Propaganda and Agitation Department. A copy of this document has been lodged with the district prosecutor's office and with the editors of* Życie Warszawy *and* Express Wieczorny*.* That last sentence was not true, and it was the sort of untruth that a woman who has spent thirteen years in the machine can tell without any difficulty at all: three copies in three envelopes, all of them in the same tray, and the office would spend a week trying to find out who else had one.

She walked out of the building without being stopped, because nobody stops a woman in a gray coat who looks as though she is late for something.

The larger one was at the Control Commission, where she handed in a signed statement, three pages long, which named the widow and the son, described the office in Lublin and the man behind the door with the pistol, and said that she would repeat every word of it at any investigation the Party wished to hold.

She waited in the corridor for two hours. Nobody came out to her. At noon a young man with a briefcase told her that the commission was in session and that her case would be heard on the second of January and that she might consider herself suspended from duty in the meantime, on full pay, which was not a kindness but a procedure.

Then she walked out of the building into the snow. It was the twenty-fourth of December, 1958. She was thirty-six years old, she had no family, she had no Party, she was shortly to have no job, and at six o'clock she was expected at Maryla Zawadzka's flat in Mokotów with a bottle of vodka, a poppy-seed cake, and a priest.

She was, she discovered to her own surprise, happy.

Maryla's building was on Puławska, a pre-war block with a courtyard and a wide staircase and the smell of a hundred families cooking at once. Halina climbed to the third floor carrying the cake in its tin and the bottle in a paper bag and found the door already open and Maryla in a red dress with flour on her hands.

"You are early," Maryla said, kissing her. "You are never early. Who are you and what have you done with Halina Sowa?"

"I am standing on the landing. Let me in."

The flat was full. There was a great deal of furniture and a great deal of food and a great deal of noise. Maryla's husband, Kazimierz, who was an engineer at the waterworks and had the calm voice of a man used to disasters, took her coat. Their daughter Ewa, who was in her third year of medicine and had her mother's round face and none of her mother's patience, was arguing in the kitchen with Maryla about the salt. Their son Marek, home on leave from the army in a sweater with a hole in the sleeve, was sitting on the arm of the sofa reading a newspaper and pretending to be idle.

And in the corner by the window, in a black suit with a face that had been freshly shaved and nicked in three places, sat Father Janusz Wilga, with a glass of tea in his hand and Pani Genowefa's instructions on his hat, which he had written on a piece of paper and put inside the band.

Maryla had done it deliberately. She had invited Wanda Kowalik and the three children as well; they were not there yet. She had put the two of them in the same room with twenty minutes to spare before the guests arrived, and she had then retreated to the kitchen with a bang of the door and left them to it.

Halina stood in the doorway with the cake in her hands.

"You are here," she said.

"On the third floor, at the top of the stairs, with a cake in my hat," Janusz said, standing up. "I have been here for eighteen minutes, and a small child from the first floor has asked me whether I can turn water into wine."

"Can you?"

"I told him that the trouble is not turning it into wine, it is getting people to stop."

Halina laughed. She put the cake on the sideboard and took off her coat and hung it in the hall and came back and sat down on the sofa beside him and then, a moment later, realizing that they were alone in the room and that Maryla had engineered it, she leaned over and kissed him on the cheek.

It was a small, plain, domestic kiss, delivered to a man with a cut on his chin, and it landed on him like a stone in still water.

"Halina—"

"Do not get theological about it. It is Christmas Eve." She stood up. "Give me the tea and tell me about Otwock."

"I was going to ask about Otwock."

"I asked first."

So he told her: about the cardinal and the sacristy and the knees and the arithmetic; about Prefect Bochenek, who had come to the rectory on the twenty-second in a good overcoat with a parcel of books, and had said, at the end of a half-hour, "When I am consecrated, will you pray for me? Not for the mitre. For the thing the mitre is supposed to be," and had then gone out into the snow and turned around on the step to say, "The books were mine when I was a student. They are better than you deserve, but you will need them in Wyszków"; about the parish council, who had met on the twenty-first and voted to petition the curia for him to stay and had been told, correctly, by the curia that the curia had already decided; about the youth club, closed on the twenty-second by the office, its table-tennis table and its books and its six bats taken away in a van while Pani Genowefa stood on the steps with her arms folded and watched them, and the boys stood along the wall of the presbytery, and nobody said anything, and one of them cried, and it was not the youngest.

Halina told him about Otwock: the pines and the lace curtain and the tea and the son standing in the doorway. She told it in the compressed way she told things, and when she came to the part where the mother had said *sit down, in this house nobody gets up from a table on Christmas Eve*, her voice did something that she had to stop and drink tea over.

"You have not done a small thing," Janusz said.

"I have done a very small thing, thirteen years too late, to one widow out of the many."

"That is not how it works."

"You are always telling me how it works."

"I am a priest. It is my trade." He set down his glass. "Halina. When I am gone to Wyszków, and you are in some office in Warsaw with a new job and a smaller salary, and the file is in Rydel's drawer and the year has turned — what is to become of you?"

"I have not the faintest idea," she said. "I have spent my whole life knowing exactly what would become of me. It is restful."

"Will you write to me?"

"If I have anything to say."

"That is not a yes."

"It is a yes with my conditions in it." She looked at him. "I will not write you letters about the weather, and I will not write you the other kind of letters, and you would not want either. I will write when there is something true to write. And you will write back the same way, and if nine months pass in silence, nobody is to be hurt about it."

"Agreed."

"Say it as if you mean it."

"I mean it," he said. "I will be very lonely in Wyszków."

"I know," she said. "So will I. There is a kind of comfort in the arithmetic."

The doorbell rang, and Maryla came out of the kitchen at a run, and Wanda Kowalik came in with snow on her headscarf and Ewa beside her carrying a covered dish, and Marek behind her carrying Tomek, who was seven and asleep on his feet. There was a great business of boots and coats and exclamations. The children, once awake, went about the flat at a speed that made the furniture seem to move. Kazimierz put the bottle on the sideboard and set out the glasses. And then Maryla took command, in the way of a woman who has been cooking for two days, and everyone was put where she wanted them and the lights were turned off.

The table was set for eleven. There was hay under the white cloth, a handful of it, in the old way, and a little pile of straw at the corner, and Maryla's children pretended to be embarrassed by it and were, in fact, pleased. There was carp in aspic and herring in oil and beetroot soup with mushroom dumplings and cabbage with mushrooms and pierogi and dried-fruit compote and a great deal of bread, and the extra place laid at the end of the table where nobody sat, with a candle beside it.

"Who is that for?" asked Tomek, who was seven, at the top of his voice.

"For the people who are not here," said Maryla, without a pause. "Now sit up straight and do not put your elbow in the compote."

There was a wafer at each place. Maryla rose and went around the table with hers in her hand and broke it with each person in turn, and said a thing to each, quietly, so that the others would not hear, and when she came to Halina she said, "No more of the old life. We are doing the new one now," and Halina, who had been expecting something sentimental, found that her eyes had gone hot and that she could not answer.

Then Kazimierz went around, and Wanda, and Ewa the medical student, and Marek the soldier, being extremely correct with the wafer and very shy.

And then Janusz stood up, and went around the table with the wafer in his hand, and came at last to Halina, and they stood facing each other over the hay and the twelve dishes and the candle for the absent.

"Halina," he said, very low. "I have nothing to forgive you for. That is the first thing. The second thing is that I have loved you for twenty years, and it is not a sin, and I am not going to pretend any more that it is; I am only going to be careful with it. The third thing—" and here his voice failed him, and he stopped, and looked at the wafer in his hand, and then said it anyway, "—the third thing is that I am glad, in a way that I cannot justify before God or man, that it is you who are standing here and not someone else."

"Janusz," she said. "Will you be all right in Wyszków?"

"I will be all right in Wyszków."

"Will you be all right in ten years?"

"I do not know."

"Neither do I," she said. "That is my whole answer to everything. I do not know." She reached out and broke the corner of his wafer and put it in her mouth. "But I am glad it is you I am not knowing it with."

He broke her wafer and ate a piece of it, and they sat down, and Maryla said grace, and everybody began to talk at once.

After supper they sang. Ewa played the piano, badly and with great enthusiasm, and Marek sang in a fine, embarrassed baritone, and Wanda wept a little at *Bóg się rodzi* and pretended that it was the onions, and Tomek climbed onto Janusz's knee and asked him whether he was really a priest, and when Janusz said he was, Tomek asked whether he had ever seen God, and Janusz said, "Not with my eyes," and Tomek considered this and said, "That's all right. I haven't either," and went to sleep against his shoulder.

At half past ten the children were carried down to the car that Kazimierz had borrowed for Wanda and the family, and Wanda kissed Halina on both cheeks and said, "You are different," and Halina said, "I am the same," and Wanda said, "That is what I mean."

At eleven, Halina and Janusz came out of the building together and stood under the archway in the snow.

The street was quiet. There was a light in a window across the road and a dog barking somewhere. It was snowing the way it snows on Christmas Eve, slowly, in large flakes, as though somebody had been given the job of decorating and had taken it seriously.

"Walk with me to the corner," she said.

They walked. The snow creaked under their feet. At the corner of Puławska and the side street, a black car was standing at the kerb with its engine running and a light in the cabin, and in the front seat sat a man in a gray coat with a cigarette, and in the back a girl of about twelve, in a red hat, looking out of the window at the lights.

Rydel got out. He stood by the car with his hand on the door, and he had not put on a hat, and the snow was settling on his hair.

"Halina," he said.

Janusz stopped and stood still and did not go forward or back.

Rydel looked at him and then at her. "I am not here for the office," he said. "I am taking Agnieszka to see the lights on the Old Town. She has been asking all evening. She is twelve, and she has decided that she believes in the baby Jesus, and she has asked me whether I do, and I have been driving around this city for an hour and a half trying to think of an answer." He looked at the car; the girl was still looking out of the window; he raised a hand to her, and she waved back with the enormous condescension of the young. "So when I saw you—" He stopped. "I read your statement, you know. The one you left with the commission this morning. It came to me at noon because my office is copied on everything that touches 1945, and I read it in this car while my daughter was buying chestnuts, and then I sat in the car for twenty minutes and did not send it up."

"I know," she said. "I have been waiting for you to send it up."

"I have not sent it up," Rydel said, "because it says nothing that the Party does not know, and because you wrote it in the third person and signed it as if you were someone else, and I do not know what to do with a document written by a woman who wants to be punished and has gone about it in a way that makes the Party look ridiculous." He lit another cigarette off the old one. "It will be heard on the second of January. I know what the recommendation will be. Suspension for one year, expulsion, no prosecution. The Party does not put its own in prison for 1945. The Party puts its own in a room and explains to them that they have disappointed the working class, and then they are given a pension and a very small flat and they are told that if they keep quiet they may keep both. That is the punishment. It is untidy but it is not nothing."

"I know," she said again.

"I have hated you for a week," Rydel said. "That is a strange thing to say to a woman I have known for thirteen years. I have hated you because you have taken away the only thing I had that made me sure that what I was doing was necessary. If you can be a murderer and a good woman, then what am I? If the man in the file can stand up in his own church and confess, then what is the file for?" He smoked. "I do not want an answer. I am not going to ask you for one. I am going to get back in this car and take my daughter to look at the lights."

"Rydel."

He stopped.

"Your daughter asked you whether you believe in the baby Jesus," Halina said. "Tell her you do not know. Do not tell her that she is too young to ask, and do not tell her that it is a fairy tale, and do not tell her what to believe. Tell her that you do not know, and that your father did not know, and that there are men who die for not knowing and men who die for knowing, and that she must decide which kind of person she is going to be. She is twelve. She can take it. She is your daughter."

Rydel looked at her. Then he looked at Janusz, over her shoulder, and something passed between the two men that Halina did not try to read.

"Good night, Father," Rydel said.

"Good night," said Janusz.

Rydel got into the car. The window came down and the girl leaned out and said, "Good night!" to them both with great cheerfulness, and the window went up again, and the car pulled away from the kerb and went off down Puławska toward the center of the city with the snow following it.

Halina and Janusz stood at the corner for a while.

"Well," she said at last.

"Yes."

"He will not send it up."

"No."

"He will not send it up," she said, "and in ten years he will be in a better flat, and his daughter will be a woman with a face like his, and I will be a person that the Party does not speak of. That is what the file comes to. It is not justice. It is not even a settlement. It is just that the machine goes on, and a few people get out of it, and a few people are pushed out, and it keeps grinding."

"You are out of it," Janusz said.

"Yes," she said. "I am out of it."

He walked her to the tram stop on Puławska. The tram came; it was almost empty. She got on and stood in the doorway and looked down at him, standing in the snow with his hat in his hand and no coat collar turned up because he never did turn it up.

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"It is Christmas."

"It is Christmas."

"Go home," she said. "Say your Mass. And when you go to Wyszków in February, go without all this. It is finished. It is over. The file is closed and you were never the man in it."

The tram bell rang. The doors began to close.

"I was the man in it," he said.

"Yes," she said. "So was I. That is why it is closed."

The doors shut. The tram pulled away. She stood in the doorway with her hand on the rail and watched him standing under the streetlamp until the tram turned the corner and the street and the light and the man were gone.

The second of January came. The hearing took forty minutes.

Halina Sowa was expelled from the Polish United Workers' Party for moral misconduct and conduct contrary to the principles of socialist morality, and suspended from her post, and granted, by a decision taken at four o'clock on the same afternoon by a deputy minister who had never met her, a disability allowance of six hundred and forty złoty a month, which was rather more than the average wage and rather less than she had been earning, and which was accompanied by a letter that said, in its second paragraph, that the decision was taken in recognition of her many years of service and in the expectation that she would continue to observe the discretion required of a former officer of the department.

She read the letter in the corridor. She read the second paragraph twice.

Then she folded it and put it in her handbag and went out into the snow, and walked across the city to Marszałkowska, and began, at last, to pack.

# Chapter Ten: The Birch and the Bell

They consecrated Bochenek on the eighteenth of January, in the cathedral of St. John, in a cold that made the candles gutter, and the state came in its good black cars and stood in the front with their hats held in front of them, and the pews were full of priests and nuns and laypeople who had come because a bishop is a thing one goes to see.

Janusz sat at the back, under the gallery, in a borrowed overcoat, and was not invited to sit anywhere else.

It was a fine ceremony. The old rites had been kept, and the choir had been rehearsed, and the ring and the crozier and the mitre were real and very old, and out in the square afterwards the people pressed around the new bishop and kissed his ring, and an old peasant woman from Wyszków went down on her knees on the cobbles to him, and he took her by both hands and lifted her up himself and said something to her that made her cry.

Halina stood at the edge of the crowd with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, and watched.

"Well?" she said, when Janusz came out by the side door.

"He is a good bishop," he said. "Better than I would have been. He will build twenty churches and not lose one of them to the state, and he will never say a thing that costs him, and he will be there in 1970 when I am an old man in a village, and he will still be there in 1980 when half of the men who stood in the front of that cathedral have been moved to other jobs and forgotten." He turned up his collar, remembered he never did, and turned it down again. "It is a strange thing to be relieved and ashamed at the same time."

"That is what it is to be a Pole," Halina said. "It is a national condition."

He laughed. They walked away from the cathedral, down toward the river, past the monument to Copernicus with the snow on his shoulders and his arm around the armillary sphere.

The last Sunday of January was his last Sunday in the parish, and he said the eleven o'clock Mass in a church that was fuller than it had been on Christmas, and preached for eleven minutes — the shortest sermon of his life — on the woman at the well and the water that does not run out. Then he went out to the door and stood there while the parish came past him.

It took an hour and a half. The old women came with their hands out. Pani Zofia, who was ninety-one and had been carried up the steps by her grandson, gave him a pair of knitted socks. Pan Tadeusz the sacristan, who had been in that church since 1931 and who had not spoken of the matter at all since December, shook his hand and said, "The boiler will be all right. I will not let them take it back to the old way," and then went into the sacristy and shut the door. The railwayman came with his hat off. The men from the works came in a group, and one of them said, "Father, the twenty-two is running again," which meant the table-tennis club, which meant that somebody had bought a table out of his own money and put it in a cellar, and Janusz did not ask who.

Zbyszek Malinowski came last. He had come in a suit that was too small for him and a white shirt and a tie borrowed from somebody.

"I am being called up in March," he said. "Two years. Signals."

"Write to me."

"I will write to you." He stood there with his hands at his sides. "Father, I wanted to tell you. In November I was going to ask you whether it was all true, the whole thing, the resurrection, everything. I was going to give you a book by a Frenchman. I thought I was very clever." He looked at the floor. "Then in December you stood in the pulpit and told us you were a traitor. And I understood that you could not have done that if it was not true, because a man who is lying to himself cannot do that."

"Men do worse things than that for lies."

"Yes," Zbyszek said. "But not that."

Janusz took his hand and held it, and then pulled the boy's head down and kissed him on the forehead, which was not something he had ever done.

At three o'clock Pani Genowefa fed him cabbage soup and a piece of pork and two potatoes, and sat opposite him with her arms folded and watched him eat.

"You will not eat a decent meal in Wyszków."

"There will be a housekeeper in Wyszków."

"There will be an old woman in Wyszków who has been keeping house for a priest since 1946, and she will have her own ways, and she will not know about the salt." Genowefa sniffed. "You will write to me."

"Once a month."

"Once a week."

"That is not what you told me in 1952."

"In 1952 you were a young man with ideas. Now you are an old priest with a suitcase. Once a week."

He promised. She stood up and took his plate and put it in the sink and did not turn around, and said, to the window: "The kettle is broke. It has been broke since Thursday. I have been meaning to tell you." And then she went out of the kitchen into her own room and closed the door.

He left on the sixth of February, on the nine-forty from Warszawa Wschodnia, with a suitcase, a wooden crate of books, a crate of vestments, and the photograph of his parents wrapped in a shirt.

There was nobody to see him off except Halina, who came with a paper bag of apples.

"Eat them on the train," she said. "They are from Maryla's cellar. She has been giving me apples for a week as though I were emigrating."

They stood on the platform. There was the usual business of the railway: porters, a woman with three hens in a crate, a soldier asleep on a bench. The snow on the platform had been trodden to gray slush. A locomotive further down the tracks sent up a long plume of steam that hung and drifted.

"Wyszków," she said. "What is it like?"

"Eighty kilometres of the Bug. A church, a school, a mill, a garrison. In 1944 the whole center was burned, and they have rebuilt it with the money the state gave them and the money the state did not give them. There are pines. The parish is four thousand souls and eleven villages and one of them is called Deskurów, and there is a chapel there that has not had a Mass in it since 1957."

"You will have it open by June."

"I will have it open by May. I have written to the prefectorial office about the flooring. They will say no. I will write again in April."

She laughed. The train came in. The platform shook. He picked up his suitcase and she picked up the crate of books, and they carried them along to the third carriage and put them in the rack.

"Janusz."

"Halina."

"Get on the train."

"I am on the train."

"You are standing in the door of the train, and the conductor is looking at you, and if you stand there much longer you are going to say something that we will both regret."

"Yes," he said. "I was going to say that you should come to Wyszków in June."

"You should not say that."

"No."

"And I will come in June."

"Halina."

"Not to the rectory. There is a hotel by the market square. I will come on the first Sunday in June, and I will sit at the back of your church, and afterwards you will walk me to the bus, and the whole town will see it and be scandalized, and it will be one more thing for them to decide about, and they will decide in your favor, because the people of Wyszków are not fools." She held out her hand. He took it. "Go on. Go and be a parish priest in a burned town. It is a better life than being a bishop."

He got on the train. The whistle went. The doors closed. She stood on the platform with snow on her hat and watched the carriage move away, and he stood at the window with one hand on the glass, and neither of them did anything foolish, and the train went around the curve and out of sight.

She stood there for a while in the cold. Then she went down the station steps and out into the street and took the tram home to Marszałkowska, where the flat was already half packed in boxes, because she had to be out of it by the first of March.

She had found a room on the top floor of a house in Praga, on a street off Brzeska, with a window facing a courtyard and a lime tree, at a rent of two hundred złoty a month. She had found work as well, then lost it in the same week, and then found another: four mornings a week in the office of the clinic on Radzymińska where Maryla had intervened and where the chief accountant, a Party man of long standing, had said to her on the first day, "You are the one from the ministry. I do not care what you did. You will keep the ledgers correctly, and you will not discuss politics with the nurses." She agreed to both, and kept the second one.

So she kept the ledgers correctly, and did not discuss politics with the nurses, and in the evenings she walked to the church on Brzeska Street and sat in the last pew while the altar boys lit the candles for Benediction.

In March a letter came to Wanda Kowalik's room on Brzeska Street from the district prosecutor's office. The matter of the crane at the Nowotki works had been reopened. The director and the chief engineer had been dismissed from their posts and referred to the factory party committee; the safety commission had been reconstituted; and the widow's pension had been recalculated and increased by two hundred and ten złoty a month, with arrears from October. Nobody was charged with anything. Nobody was going to be. It was the most that the machine could be made to do, and it had taken three copies of a document that did not exist, in a tray on the fourth floor, to make it do that much. Wanda read the letter standing at her kitchen table and then sat down and cried for a quarter of an hour, and when Halina came on Saturday she was still angry about it, and they ate bread and dripping and did not talk about it, and it was, on the whole, the best thing either of them had ever done.

The winter went. In April the ice went out of the Vistula in a single noisy night, and there were floods in the low streets on the Praga side, and in the clinic they had to move the filing cabinets up to the second floor. In May the chestnuts came out. In June, on the first Sunday, she took the early bus north to Wyszków and sat at the back of a rebuilt church with a new tin roof and a statue of the Virgin that had been rescued from the ruins of the old one, and heard a priest she had known since 1938 preach on the man born blind. Afterwards he walked her to the bus stop in the market square, and the whole town saw it, and two widows in the front pew discussed it for a week, and the housekeeper said something at the kitchen table that was not entirely a complaint.

She went again in July. In August Maryla came with her and they stayed a week at the hotel by the market square and were given the best room and charged almost nothing for it, and Maryla said, at the end of the week, walking to the station: "He is not your husband and he never will be, and half the town thinks he is, and the other half thinks he ought to be. Are you going to be able to live with that?" And Halina said, "I have lived with considerably worse," and Maryla said, "That is not an answer," and Halina said, "No. It is a fact."

And in September, at the end of the harvest, she and Janusz went together to Brzóza.

---

The bus from Puławy came down the hill through the pines and turned at the bend of the river, and the village was there in the way it had always been there: the road, the houses, the poplars, the church with its tower.

They got out at the church square. There were four people waiting there: a boy of about fifteen, with a bicycle, who had been sent by the parish priest to meet them; an old woman selling eggs from a basket; an old man on a bench outside the co-operative; and a dog.

Halina's mother's house at the edge of the village, near the forest, had stood empty for eight months. The roof leaked over the kitchen. A pane was broken in the small window. Someone had cut the lock off the shed and taken the scythe and the spade. But the stove was sound, and the walls were standing, and when she got the door open and the shutters back the light came in and lay on the bare floorboards exactly where it had lain in 1938.

"Her chair is gone," Halina said. She stood in the middle of the room. "There was a chair by the window. She sat there in the evening. It was her mother's chair. It was 1907 or something. Somebody has taken it."

"We will get you another chair."

"It is not the chair." She touched the wall where the crucifix had hung, and where the nail was still there and the plaster around it gone pale. "It is that they came into her house and took her chair and thought nothing of it. There is no malice in it. That is what I cannot get used to. Malice I understand. It is the indifference."

They swept out the kitchen. They put up the shutters on the inner side against the weather. They walked to the cemetery and stood at the two graves, and then at the third grave — smaller, older, with a stone that had been repaired — and Janusz said the prayers for the dead, and after them Halina said, in a voice that was almost casual, "Tadek. It is the third time I have been here. I am going to come every year now. I am going to be a bore about it."

And then they walked down through the village, past the co-operative and the new barn and the tractor shed where Góra's name was painted on a board, and past the corner where the manor had been — there was a school there now, a long low building with wide windows and a photograph of Gomułka in the lobby — and out to the meadows above the river, where the birch tree stood.

It had been there for a hundred years. It was there still, leaning slightly out over the water, its bark peeling in long pale strips, its leaves beginning to go yellow in the first week of September. The river ran below the bank, brown and quiet, and on the far side there was a man with a fishing rod, sitting on an upturned bucket, absolutely motionless, as a man with a fishing rod will sit.

Janusz stopped at the top of the bank.

"How many years?" he said.

"Since 1939. Twenty years."

"It was August."

"It was the fourteenth of August, 1943. The day they shot Tadek. And two days later you came out of Lublin with your hand bound up and told me you had caught it on a nail." She looked at him. "I knew it was not a nail. I did not ask."

They sat down on the bank with their backs against the trunk. The bark was warm. It was one of those afternoons in early September when the light lies flat and gold across the fields and the air smells of cut hay and the first rot of the year, and everything seems, for an hour, to have been forgiven.

"He is still there," Janusz said. "The man with the rod."

"There is always a man with a rod."

"When I was a boy I thought he was the same man. I thought he had been sitting there since the Piasts."

"He probably has. He is the spirit of the river. He has been sitting on that bucket since the year 966, and he has caught nothing, and he is entirely content." She leaned her head back against the tree. "Janusz, I want to tell you something, and I do not want you to answer it, and I do not want you to be a priest about it."

"I will do my best."

"I have thought about it for a long time. Since Christmas. What I am going to do with the rest of my life." She looked at the river. "I am not going to be a Party official. I am not going to be a martyr. I am not going to be the woman at the clinic with a past, either, the one who is very quiet and whom nobody asks about. I am going to be a teacher."

He turned his head.

"You were going to be a teacher," he said. "In 1938."

"I was going to be a teacher in this village, and I was going to marry a blacksmith's son, and we were going to have four children and a garden and be very poor and very tiresome." She said it lightly, as an inventory of goods. "Well. I am not going to have the blacksmith's son or the four children. But there is a thing called the pedagogic course in Warsaw for people without qualifications, and it takes a year and a half, and Maryla knows a woman who runs it, and it is not the Party's course, it is the co-operative's, and you can teach infants in a village school with it. I have written to the school in Brzóza. They want a teacher for the first class in September 1960. The present teacher is a good woman from Lublin who has been here since 1954 and wants to go home."

"You would come back here."

"I would come back here." She said it without drama. "I have a house. I have a garden, if I can get the garden back, and I have a mother buried in the cemetery and a brother and a girl who is not buried anywhere, and there is a school with forty children in it, and half of them will be Góra's grandchildren, and I will teach them to read." She looked at him. "I am not doing it for the dead. I want to be very clear that I am not doing it for the dead. I am doing it because when I was sixteen I wanted to be a teacher more than I wanted anything in the world except you, and I have spent twenty years being something else, and I am tired of being something else."

"Then do it," he said.

"You see. I told you not to be a priest."

"That was not priestly. That was a man saying yes to a woman on a riverbank."

She laughed, and then she was quiet.

They sat there for a long time. The light changed. The man across the river caught nothing and did not move. Somewhere up in the village a dog barked, and a woman called a child in for supper, and there was the sound of a cart.

"Halina," Janusz said at last. "There is a thing I have never said to you, in nineteen years. I mean to say it now, and then we will go up to the village and eat eggs and bread, and it will not be mentioned again."

"Say it."

"I do not regret the priesthood."

She turned her head and looked at him. His face was turned toward the river, and he was not smiling, and there was no self-pity in it at all.

"I have been a priest for ten years," he said, "and I have buried thirty-one people and baptized sixty and heard confessions that would make a man's hair stand up, and I have been in that confessional with a woman who lost all four of her children in 1944, and I have been with a boy who tried to hang himself with a belt in the sacristy, and I have held the hand of a man dying of the cancer who thought that God had turned his face away, and I have said Mass in a room with a dead man in it and in a field on a plank and once, once, on the wing of a shot-down German aeroplane. If you had asked me in 1939 what I wanted, I would have said you. I would still say you. But I would not give up one of those hours for a house and a garden and four children, and I have finally, at thirty-six years of age, understood that this is not a betrayal of you. It is not even a sacrifice. It is a thing that happened to me, in a cell in Lublin, and I did not choose it, and I have spent fifteen years knowing that I did not choose it, and the not choosing was the mercy.

"You asked me once whether I was the boy by the river. I am not. I will be a priest in a burned town for the rest of my life, and there will be a woman who comes on the first Sunday in June, and I do not know what that is, and I am not going to call it anything, because I do not have words for it and the words that exist are all wrong."

Halina looked at the river. Her eyes were dry.

"You are a great fool," she said.

"I know."

"You are a great fool, and you have not said one untrue thing to me since 1943, and I would rather have that than a house and a garden and four children."

"Even the four children?"

"I would like the four children," she said. "I would like them very much. But one cannot have everything, and I have had a great deal more than Barbara Kowalik's daughter had any right to expect."

They got up. He held out his hand and she took it, and they climbed the bank, and as they came up onto the meadow the bell began to ring.

It was the old bell of St. Michael's, the one that had been taken down in 1915 and taken down again in 1940 and hidden in a barn and put back in 1946. It had a crack in it, a small one, in the waist, that had been there since before any living person's memory, and the crack gave the note a roughness, a slight unclean hum underneath the tone. It was the bell of her childhood. It was the bell that had rung for her First Communion and for Tadek's funeral and for the funeral of her mother, and it would ring for her own, if she was buried here, which she had decided that she would be.

It rang the Angelus: three strokes, three, three, and then the long closing peal.

They stopped in the meadow and stood still.

Halina did not bow her head. She had not bowed her head for sixteen years, and she was not going to start now, and she had made a great deal of peace with the fact that she was a person who did not believe, who would not be reconciled, who would go to the church for the singing and the silence and the company of the man she loved and would not go forward for Communion and would not lie about why.

But she stood still. She looked out over the village, over the roofs and the poplars and the road, over the cemetery on the hill where the graves were new and old and the grass was high, over the forest where three boys had been shot in 1943 and a girl had been shot in 1942 and where fifty people had been shot or marched or buried in the years when the world was on fire, and over the fields, which were the same fields, and did not remember anything.

And out beyond that the river went on, the same slow brown water, going north to the Vistula and the Vistula going north to the sea.

"Halina," Janusz said.

"Don't," she said. "Not while the bell is ringing."

So they stood in the meadow, in the yellow light, and let it ring out over them: the old cracked bronze, ringing the thing it had rung every evening for two hundred years and would be ringing long after both of them were under the grass on the hill.

When it stopped, the silence was enormous. You could hear the river.

"Now," she said.

"Now nothing," he said. "We are going up to the village and eating eggs and bread, and I will sleep in the kitchen, and in the morning we will see about the roof."

They went up the hill to the village.

# Afterword

The Church of the Transfiguration on Brzeska Street still stands. The parish records for 1959 show a rector appointed in February of that year, a Father Wincenty Bąk, who served until 1971 and who is remembered, in the parish chronicle, for having repaired the roof of the presbytery and for the length of his sermons.

The same records show, in the hand of a curial clerk, the entry for the previous rector: *Wilga, Janusz, b. 1922, priest 1948, curate at the Transfiguration 1949, rector 1951–1959, transferred to Wyszków.* That is the whole of him in the archive. Everything else — the sermon, the file, the refusal, the woman who came on the first Sunday in June — is in the memories of the people he buried, and in a few letters in a shoebox, and in a parish chronicle in Wyszków which records, under May 1959, that the chapel at Deskurów was reopened for the worship of God after an interruption of two years, and that the flooring was paid for by public subscription.

This is a work of fiction, and its people are invented. But there were many like them, on both sides of the file: the ones who signed, and the ones who were signed; the ones who stood up in their churches and confessed, and the ones who sat in offices and wrote the reports. The century they lived in asked almost everyone the same question — tell the truth and lose your life, or keep your life and lose the truth — and a great many people, in the end, told the truth, and paid for it, and are not remembered either.

The bell of Brzóza was recast in 1968. It does not have the crack in it any more. By all accounts it sounds much better.

# A Note on History

This is a work of fiction. Brzóza, the Kowals, the Wilgas, the Berezowskis, Rydel, Bochenek, and the parish of the Transfiguration on Brzeska Street are all invented, and nothing in this book should be read as an accusation against any real person, living or dead. Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński appears as a historical figure; his words in these pages are imagined, though they are meant to be consistent with the man his contemporaries described — shrewd, unsentimental, and very hard to frighten.

The background is real. In 1958 Poland was two years past the "thaw" of October 1956. Gomułka had returned to power, Cardinal Wyszyński had been released from three years of internment, and the Church had recovered a little room to move in. The room was narrow and getting narrower. In July 1958 the security service raided the Primate's Institute at Jasna Góra, broke into the archives, and beat the priests and pilgrims they found there; the state was simultaneously blocking the construction of a church at Nowa Huta and pushing religious instruction out of the schools. Under the arrangement the Primate had negotiated after 1956, the Church presented a single candidate for each vacant see and the state could only accept or refuse him. That power of refusal is the machinery on which this story turns.

The Party's Propaganda and Agitation Department, the Security Service, the Office for Religious Affairs, the Party Control Commission, and the Polish Press Agency's confidential bulletins all existed. The files, the interrogations, and the bargains described here are inventions built out of the kind of testimony that survivors gave for decades afterward: the photograph of a mother on a table, the glass of tea, the promise that nobody would be hurt.

Karol Wojtyła was appointed auxiliary bishop of Kraków in July 1958 and consecrated in September, at thirty-eight — younger than almost any bishop in the Church's recent history, and younger than the man at the center of this book. He is mentioned twice and appears nowhere; the reader will know what became of him.

The novel also draws on the wartime history of the Lublin region: the German occupation, the Home Army, the People's Guard and People's Army, the destruction of the shtetls and the killing of the region's Jewish population, and the postwar prosecution of the Home Army by the communist security apparatus. The characters remember these events imperfectly, in the way that people do. The book does not claim to be a history. It claims only to be a story about what history does to love, faith, and conscience — and about the ordinary, unfashionable acts that a person can still perform when the file on them is open.

The title comes from two things in Brzóza: the birch tree by the river, where two children first kissed in 1939, and the cracked bell of the church of St. Michael, which rang for their baptisms, their dead, and their homecomings, and which rang on the evening this story ends.

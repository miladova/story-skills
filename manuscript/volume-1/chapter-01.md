# Chapter One

The man in the cart had been dead since morning, but the mule did not know it. She stopped at the river to drink, as she had on the journey every market day for seventeen years, and waited with her muzzle in the water for her master to tell her when she had drunk enough.

Marcia saw her from the dye-yard. The mule had come down the bank by the narrow path, dragging one wheel through the reeds. The cart tilted, and the man tipped with it, his arm hanging over the side. A sack of wool lay across his legs. Even from a distance she could see the dark stain on his tunic, though at first she thought it was another dye, a blue-black from the vats. Then the mule lifted her head, and a little red ran from the cart into the river.

Marcia set down the pot of ash-water she was carrying. It broke on a stone. Her mother, who was bent over a fleece, looked up.

“What have you done?”

“There’s a cart.”

“There are carts every day.”

“There’s blood in this one.”

Her mother straightened slowly. She had broad hands, stained to the wrists, and a burn on her neck from an accident before Marcia was born. People still told her how fortunate she had been not to have her face ruined. She had once asked a man what work he thought her face did for her, and he had not come back to buy cloth.

They went down together. The mule watched them approach and put her head down again. Flies rose from the man’s mouth and settled on his open eyes.

“It’s Celer,” her mother said. “Don’t touch him.”

But Marcia had already climbed onto the wheel. There was a cut beneath his jaw. It was not a clean cut. Somebody had tried more than once.

“Don’t,” her mother said again, not loudly.

Marcia stepped down. The mule’s harness was tied in a knot she recognized. Celer tied his knots as if he were afraid of being robbed by the rope itself. She had teased him about it when he brought wool from the farms above the town. He had told her that a woman who could not tie a knot should not be allowed to marry. She had told him he could safely go on living as he pleased.

His purse was gone. One of his sandals had come loose, and the toes showed through the straps. Mud had dried between them. Celer had always made a point of washing his feet before he drove into town. If someone had killed him on the road, they had taken his money but left the wool. Marcia wondered how much money a man would have to carry for the wool not to be worth the trouble.

Her mother put her hand on the mule’s bridle. “Go and find his brother.”

“His brother’s at the quarry.”

“Then find someone who knows where his brother is.”

Marcia hesitated. “We should bring the cart in.”

“We should leave it where we found it.” Her mother glanced upstream, toward the bridge and the road that ran beyond it to the hills. “Go.”

At the gate she met Lucius coming toward the yard, carrying a pair of worn shoes. He had grown since she last saw him three weeks ago; at least that was what she thought until she realized he was wearing a new tunic, short at the wrists. Its hem had been let down with a strip of darker cloth. He looked prosperous from a distance and poor when he got close, which was a familiar condition in their town.

“I’ve brought your mother a gift,” he said, holding up the shoes.

“She can’t eat them.”

“I thought she could throw them at me instead of the good ones.”

“Celer’s dead.”

Lucius lowered his hand. For a moment his face remained arranged for a joke; then it changed. “Where?”

“In the cart. By the river.”

He walked past her without asking anything else. Marcia followed him down the path. Her mother had moved away from the mule and stood with her arms folded, looking toward the bridge. Lucius climbed onto the wheel and leaned over the cart.

“Don’t touch him,” Marcia said.

“I heard her.” He looked at the wound. “Was he robbed?”

“His purse is gone.”

Lucius lifted the edge of the wool sack with one finger. It was full. He let it drop. “He came down from the farms?”

“He always does.”

“I passed two carts on the upper road. Neither of them mentioned him.”

“Perhaps they didn’t see him.”

“Perhaps.” He got down and studied the bank. Hoofprints lay over the marks of earlier carts, pressed into mud softened by the river. One print had a crescent chip at the edge. Lucius squatted beside it. “That isn’t from Celer’s mule.”

Her mother said, “What good will it do him to know that?”

“None. He’s dead.”

“Then find somebody who can take him away before the children come down here.”

Lucius stood. “I’ll find his brother.”

“He’s at the quarry,” Marcia said.

“I know.” He turned the shoes in his hand. “I came through there.”

“You came through the quarry with shoes?”

“They’re mine.”

“Why aren’t they on your feet?”

“Because I’m saving them.”

She looked down. His feet were bare, and the second toe of the left foot was bleeding. “You’ve been to see your father.”

He did not answer. His father was clerk to a contractor at the quarry, a position that required him to be able to count other men’s money without appearing to notice whose money it was. Marcia had known Lucius long enough to see the small alteration in his face when he was angry with his father and did not wish to talk about it. He walked away with the shoes, taking the steep path up toward the road.

Her mother said, “You’ll want to follow him.”

“I was told to find Celer’s brother.”

“Lucius will do it.”

“He doesn’t know where he is.”

“He knows where the quarry is.”

Marcia looked back at the cart. The dead man’s arm had shifted when Lucius touched the sack. His fingers were curled, as if they held something small. She thought of prising them open and saw her mother watching her.

“Go on,” her mother said. “You’ll be no use to me here.”

Marcia caught Lucius at the bridge. A woman selling figs had set her basket on the parapet and was trying to persuade him to buy the soft ones. He had offered her the shoes in exchange. She was considering them, though one sole had a hole.

“They’re too big for me,” the woman said.

“Your husband could grow into them.”

“He’s fifty.”

“Then you haven’t long to wait.”

The woman snorted and handed him two figs. He gave her the shoes. Marcia waited until they were out of hearing.

“You got the better of her.”

“She did. I stole the shoes.”

“From whom?”

“My father’s employer.”

She stopped. “You idiot.”

“He was throwing them away.”

“Then you didn’t steal them.”

“He hadn’t thrown them away yet.” Lucius offered her a fig. She took it and split the skin with her thumbnail. The flesh inside was pink and hot from the sun.

They crossed the bridge together. The town lay behind them, its roofs descending toward the river, its small temple bright with fresh paint. Beyond the temple the new bathhouse rose above the older buildings. Its benefactor’s name had been carved so deeply into the stone that even when the plaster cracked, people would know who had paid for it. He had died before it was finished, and his sons had quarreled over whether the inscription should mention their mother. In the end she had paid for another inscription on a bench, where people could sit on her name.

Lucius sucked the fig from its skin. “My father says they’re bringing an imperial officer to inspect the quarry accounts.”

“Is that why you went there?”

“He sent for me.”

“And gave you shoes?”

“He gave me advice.”

“Was it useful?”

“He said I ought to keep my mouth shut. I said he’d found it a profitable habit.”

Marcia ate the rest of her fig. The river smelled of wet stone and the bitter vats downstream. “You should try it once. You could buy another pair of shoes.”

“He wants me to copy the accounts before the officer arrives.”

“You can write. That’s what people pay you for.”

“He doesn’t want to pay me.”

“He’s your father.”

“That was his argument.”

They walked between fields that had been cut close, leaving the stubble bright and sharp. A boundary stone stood at the turn, its inscription worn away on one side. Celer’s cart had passed it often enough to leave a groove in the road. Marcia looked for blood in the dust, but carts and animals had gone over it all day, and the earth offered her nothing she could understand.

“His wool was still there,” she said.

Lucius glanced at her. “Yes.”

“Who takes a purse and leaves a cart?”

“Someone who can’t drive a mule.”

“Everyone here can drive a mule.”

“You can’t.”

“I can. They just refuse to go where I tell them.”

“Then it might have been you.”

She nudged him into the ditch. He caught her wrist and pulled her after him, and for a few steps they stumbled together, laughing despite Celer, despite the blood in the river. Then she saw a group of quarrymen coming down the road and pulled free. One of them was Celer’s brother.

They told him beside the boundary stone. He listened without moving. He was older than Celer, heavy through the shoulders, with a white seam of dust across his forehead where his headcloth had been. When Lucius had finished, he asked what had happened to the mule.

“She’s at the river,” Marcia said. “My mother is there.”

He nodded. He seemed relieved, and ashamed of being relieved. “I’ll go.”

One of the other men offered to come with him. Celer’s brother refused, then asked whether the man would fetch his wife. The man set off at once. The others stood a little apart, studying the road and the fields, leaving the brother space to grieve or not to grieve as he chose.

“He’d been worried,” the brother said. “About the wool.”

“Why?” Lucius asked.

But the man had already started down the road. He walked quickly, and Marcia thought he might be trying to reach the cart before he had to imagine it again.

Lucius watched him go. “Come up to the quarry with me.”

“Your father won’t be pleased.”

“He isn’t pleased with me. You could be a distraction.”

“I don’t distract your father.”

“No. But you distract me.”

He said it lightly, as he said most things that mattered. She brushed the fig seeds from her fingers. The road curved uphill, and beyond the bend she could hear the blows of hammers, regular as someone calling from a great distance.

“I’ll come as far as the gate,” she said.

* * *

The quarry gate was a pair of upright stones with a chain stretched between them. A man sat in their shade, mending a harness. He was missing three fingers from his left hand and held the leather against his knee while he stitched with his right. He did not look up until Lucius stood over him.

“I've come to see my father.”

“Then go to his house.”

“He's here.”

“He went home.” The man pushed the needle through the leather with his thumb. “Somebody sent him a message. He went down by the old road.”

Lucius looked at Marcia. If the messenger had passed Celer’s cart, he had not stopped at the dye-yard to tell them. But there were several ways down to the river; people chose different paths depending on the weather and whom they wished to meet.

“When?” Lucius asked.

“Before the meal.”

“He didn't take the main road?”

“He wanted to see a man.”

“Which man?”

The guard pulled his thread tight. “You could ask him.”

Lucius stepped over the chain. The guard looked up at last. His eyes moved to Marcia and back again.

“Your father’s gone home,” he said.

“I heard you.”

“If you’re here about the shoes, he paid for them.”

Lucius stopped. “Who did?”

“Your father. The steward sent me after him.”

“He could have said they were mine.”

“He could have. He paid instead.”

“How much?”

“Two denarii.”

“For those?”

“You know what they cost new.”

“I know what they cost before I wore them.” Lucius put his hand against the gatepost. He looked tired suddenly, and Marcia knew he had walked the whole way from the quarry into town and back. “The steward’s here?”

“Up at the weighing shed.”

“I’ll give them back.”

“You don’t have them.”

“I’ll get them.”

The guard’s needle caught. He worked it through with his remaining fingers. “Your father paid. Leave it alone.”

Lucius went past him. Marcia followed, though she had promised to stop at the gate. The guard watched her go without saying a word; she knew his wife, who bought damaged cloth from the yard and made it into clothes for their children. The older girl had a bent foot, and last winter Marcia’s mother had sent them wool without taking payment. The guard would remember that. He was not a man to mistake kindness for a debt that had expired.

They climbed between the stone faces of the quarry. Chips of pale rock covered the ground, and the sun came back from them so strongly that Marcia narrowed her eyes. Workers stood on ledges above them, some cutting channels, others driving wedges into stone. The blows sounded different at close range: an iron note, then a duller one, and sometimes a pause while a man shifted his grip or spat into his palm. Farther down the slope, teams of men hauled a block on a wooden sledge. One of them wore a collar. Another had no collar but was tied to the same rope.

Water ran from a trough beside the path. Marcia cupped some in her hands and drank. Lucius leaned against the trough and examined the cut on his toe.

“You could have told the woman you needed the shoes,” she said.

“She needed them more.”

“You gave them away to make a joke.”

“I gave them away because I’d already taken them off. If I’d put them on again, the steward would have seen.”

“Then you knew they weren’t yours.”

“I knew he’d make a fuss.”

She waited. He was twenty-four, a year older than she was, and could be persuaded to admit he had been wrong if he was given enough time and nobody demanded it. Often the wait was not worth it.

“My father shouldn’t have paid,” he said.

“Perhaps he thought you’d be whipped.”

“I’m not one of his slaves.”

“You’re his son.”

He dipped his foot in the trough. The water turned pink. “Thank you for reminding me.”

At the weighing shed, two men were checking a block marked with red paint. The block rested on timbers, and beside it stood a beam scale for the smaller pieces. A clerk sat at a table under the overhang, adding figures on a wax tablet. He was young, with a polished ring and a narrow face browned by the sun except for the pale band where his hair had recently been cut. When he saw Lucius, he shut the tablet.

“The shoes are gone,” he said.

“So I hear.”

“I didn’t know they were yours.”

“They weren’t.”

“I’m surprised you admit it.”

“You’d have known if I lied.”

“Would I?” The steward glanced at Marcia. “Has your father gone home?”

“The man at the gate says he has.”

“He’s due back.” The steward called to one of the men at the block, who brought him a small piece of stone. He laid it on the tablet and pushed the tablet across the table. “Your father left this. Take it to him. Tell him I want the figures checked before the officer gets here.”

Lucius looked at the tablet but did not pick it up. “He sent for me to copy them.”

“He told me. Do you want the work?”

“I want the money for it.”

The steward smiled, though his eyes stayed on Lucius. “So do I.”

Marcia had seen him once at the bathhouse, arguing with the attendant over the price of oil. She remembered him because he had won the argument and then given the attendant the disputed coin anyway, in front of the people waiting behind him. She had not known which part of the exchange had pleased him more.

“I’ll pay you,” he said. “When it’s done.”

“What if the figures don’t agree?”

“Then I’ll pay you for finding it.”

“And if you don’t like what I find?”

“Will you like being hungry?”

Lucius took the stone off the tablet and set it on the table. “I’d rather agree a price now.”

The steward named one. Lucius named another. They went back and forth, each pretending that the difference mattered more to the other. Marcia looked at the block while they bargained. The paint on its face was fresh, a broad red stripe crossed by a shorter one. Its upper corner had been chipped, leaving a pale scar. A line had been scratched below the paint, and another name, older and almost erased, ran along the bottom edge.

“Four denarii,” Lucius said.

“Three, and I’ll forget about the shoes.”

“My father paid for the shoes.”

“Then you should ask him for your money back.”

Marcia said, “Why did you charge two for a pair you’d worn through?”

The steward turned to her. “I haven’t had the pleasure.”

“You charged him the price of a new pair.”

“I did.”

“Did you give them to Lucius’s father?”

“No.”

“Then you’ve sold the same shoes twice.”

He rested his elbows on the table. “Who are you?”

“Her name is Marcia,” Lucius said. “Her mother dyes cloth.”

“I know the yard. She does good work.” The steward looked at Marcia again. “You should leave business to people who understand it.”

“You could give his father the shoes. Then you’d only have sold them once.”

“I could. But your friend gave them away.”

“To a woman on the bridge,” Lucius said. “She’ll take two denarii for them. She’ll probably take less.”

The steward’s expression shifted, not toward anger but interest. “Will she?”

“If you want them back, I’ll ask her.”

“No. I want the accounts copied.” He held out his hand. “Three and a half. Half now.”

They settled on four, a quarter now. The steward counted out the coins, including a worn one whose face had almost disappeared. Lucius tested its edge with his thumbnail. The steward let him, which made Marcia think the coin was sound; a man who passed bad money generally objected to having it examined.

From the road below came a shout, followed by another. One of the workers at the block went to the shed’s corner and peered down. “A mule,” he said. “And a cart.”

Marcia saw the top of the wool sack first, then Celer’s brother walking beside the animal. He had fastened his brother’s arm inside the cart and covered him with a cloak. Three men came with him, including the one who had offered to fetch his wife. She walked behind, holding up her skirt above the dust. Celer’s brother had refused help on the road and accepted it farther down, or perhaps the men had simply come after him.

The steward stood. “What’s this?”

“Celer,” Lucius said. “Someone killed him.”

The steward went down the slope. Marcia and Lucius followed. As they reached the gate, the guard put aside his stitching and rose, wiping his hands on his tunic. Celer’s brother did not stop for the chain. He lifted it from one of the stones and set it down, then took hold of the bridle again.

“Where are you taking him?” the steward asked.

“To the magistrates.”

“The town is the other way.”

“First I’m bringing him here.”

The steward looked under the cloak and let it fall. “I’m sorry.”

Celer’s wife said, “He was carrying your money.”

The steward looked at her. “He was carrying wool.”

“And money.”

“Not mine.”

“He went to collect from the farms for you. He said so before he left.”

The men behind her had gone quiet. Celer’s brother kept one hand on the mule’s bridle and the other on the cart, as though it might roll away.

The steward said, “He bought wool for me. I paid him for it.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“You paid him yesterday to collect it today?”

“I paid him before he went.”

The woman took a step toward him. “How much?”

“That’s between me and your husband.”

“My husband is dead.”

One of the quarrymen came up behind the steward, carrying a mallet. Marcia recognized him as the man who had brought the piece of stone to the table. He held the mallet loosely, its head against his thigh. Perhaps he had forgotten he was carrying it; perhaps he wanted everyone else to see it. The steward glanced back and told him to put it away.

“I’m sorry for your loss,” he said to Celer’s wife. “But if he was robbed, you should go to the magistrates. They’ll know what to do.”

Celer’s brother asked, “Where did he take the money?”

“To the farms. To buy wool.”

“Which farms?”

“Do you think I murdered him?”

“I think he’s my brother, and I want to know where he went.”

The steward looked past him at the men by the cart. “The farms above the east road. I don't keep a list of everyone who owns a sheep.”

Celer’s wife said, “He kept a list.”

“Then you’ll have it.”

“He carried it with him.”

Marcia thought of the dead man’s hand, fingers curled around nothing she could see. She had not opened it. She had not looked beneath the sack. There were so many places a folded scrap might have fallen, and so many hands that had already been near the cart.

The steward told the guard to let them through. Celer’s brother picked up the chain and placed it back on the post before he led the mule out. His wife walked on the other side of the cart now, her hand resting on the edge. The quarrymen followed them toward town.

The steward waited until they had gone a little way. “Well,” he said to Lucius. “You heard the woman. A robbery on the road. Take care with my money.”

Lucius put the coins in the fold of his belt. “I thought it was mine.”

“Only if you finish the work.”

He returned to the shed. Lucius picked up the wax tablet; Marcia saw that he wanted to say something and was choosing not to. He held the tablet under his arm until they had passed the gate.

“What are you thinking?” she asked.

“That he charged my father too much for the shoes.”

“You were thinking that already.”

“Yes.” He shifted the tablet. “I’m trying to keep my mouth shut.”

* * *

By the time they reached the dye-yard, the sun had moved beyond the roof and the vats were in shadow. Marcia’s mother had hung the washed wool on a line. It stirred above their heads as Lucius crossed the yard, shedding drops that smelled faintly of sheep.

“Your father came looking for you,” she said. “He’s inside.”

Lucius paused at the door. “What sort of mood?”

“He didn’t stop to tell me.”

The room behind the yard was cool. A jar of vinegar stood on the table beside a dish of olives, and Lucius’s father sat on the bench with his hands around a cup. His hair was thinner than Marcia remembered, but he held his back very straight, as if he had been placed there for an inspection. The cup belonged to her mother. He had chosen the one with a broken handle, the cup she gave people she did not care to encourage.

“Father,” Lucius said.

His father looked at the tablet under his arm. “Did you see the steward?”

“He gave me work.”

“I know. I arranged it.”

“You could have told me you’d paid for the shoes.”

“I told you to come to the quarry this morning.”

“I came.”

“You came at midday. I was supposed to wait for you?”

Marcia went to the cupboard for another cup. Neither man asked her to stay, and neither asked her to leave. She had known them both since she was small; she had eaten at their table and seen Lucius’s father take a beating from his own brother after a dispute over a strip of land. She had also watched him make Lucius stand outside in the rain for answering back. There was no predicting which of his grievances he would consider a private matter.

Lucius set the tablet down. “I’ll repay you.”

“You haven’t done the work yet.”

“I’ve been paid a quarter.”

His father held out his hand. Lucius gave him the coin. The older man turned it once, saw how worn it was, and put it in his purse.

“You owe me another,” he said.

“He made you pay too much.”

“And you made me pay anything at all.”

Lucius took an olive and bit into it. His father watched him chew, then pushed the dish toward Marcia. She took one because refusing would have made her part of their quarrel.

“I went to find you because Celer’s dead,” the older man said.

Lucius put the olive stone down. “We found him.”

“Where?”

“At the river.”

“I met his brother on the road. He said the same.” His father looked at Marcia. “Your mother was there?”

“Yes.”

“Did she see anything?”

“She saw the cart come down to drink.”

“The mule came down,” Lucius said. “The cart came with her.”

His father’s mouth tightened. “What about the purse?”

“There wasn’t one,” Marcia said.

“Did you look?”

“We looked at him. His belt was empty.”

His father nodded, though he seemed to be considering something else. He drank from the cup and set it carefully on the table. “I saw him this morning.”

“Where?” Lucius asked.

“On the old road. He was coming down from the farms.”

“What time?”

“After the meal.”

“The guard said you left before the meal.”

“I didn’t say I went straight home.”

Lucius looked at the tablet. Marcia could see the question he wanted to ask, and she wondered whether his father could see it too.

“I’d gone to meet him,” the older man said. “He had a message for me.”

“From whom?”

“A farmer.”

“What message?”

His father took up the broken-handled cup. “He’d paid Celer for wool he owed the contractor. It was supposed to come off his rent.”

“The steward said he bought wool from the farmers.”

“Then the steward knows something I don’t.”

“Was it the same wool?”

“How should I know?”

“You keep the accounts.”

“I keep the accounts I’m given.”

Outside, Marcia’s mother was speaking to someone. A man answered, and another voice, a woman’s, rose sharply over his. Marcia went to the door. The fig seller stood in the yard with one of Lucius’s shoes in her hand. The other was on the foot of a boy of about twelve, who was trying to walk in it without losing it. He was laughing. His mother was not.

“He said they were his,” she told Marcia’s mother. “The guard stopped me on the bridge and told me they were stolen.”

Marcia’s mother looked at Lucius. “He’s in here.”

The woman came inside. “I want my figs back.”

“I ate them,” he said.

“Then I want the money.”

He produced another coin. She examined it, held it to the light, and put it in her purse. Then she took the shoe off her son’s foot and threw it at Lucius. It struck him on the shoulder and fell to the floor.

“The other one,” she said.

The boy picked it up and handed it to his mother. She threw that one too, less accurately, and it landed in the vinegar jar. For a moment nobody spoke. Then the boy laughed again, clapping his hand over his mouth when she turned on him.

Marcia’s mother took the shoe from the jar, held it over the table until it had stopped dripping, and gave it to Lucius. “If you ever bring those into my house again, I’ll put them somewhere you won’t need to lace them.”

The fig seller took her son by the arm and left. Lucius wiped the shoe on the hem of his tunic. His father put his head in his hands.

“You see?” Lucius said to Marcia. “I paid the full price after all.”

His father lifted his head. “There’s a man coming from Nicomedia tomorrow. He’ll want to know what stone has gone out and what’s left in the yard. The steward wants clean copies of the figures. Make them clean.”

“I know how to copy.”

“I mean copy what’s there. No remarks in the margins. No questions for the man from Nicomedia.”

“What if he asks me?”

“Tell him to ask me.”

Marcia’s mother returned to the yard. She picked up the broken pot from beside the vat and carried the pieces to the wall, where other broken things waited to be made useful. The men in the room fell silent. Marcia took the tablet from Lucius and opened it. Columns of numbers ran down the wax, cut in a small, controlled hand. At the top was a name, a date and a mark like the red stripe on the stone.

“Can you read it?” his father asked.

“Some of it.”

“Give it back.”

She did. Lucius closed it and slid the cord around the covers. The wax would hold the figures until somebody warmed it and smoothed it over. Marcia had learned to write on scraps of pottery because the yard had plenty of those. Her mother had made her practice the names of colors and the amounts owed by customers, then had taken the pieces to the kiln to be fired. A debt written on wax could be erased; a debt fired into a shard had to be broken.

Lucius’s father got up. He thanked her mother for the drink, though she was outside and could not hear him. At the door he turned to his son.

“Come home tonight.”

“I have a room.”

“I know. Come to the house.”

Lucius said nothing. His father waited, then left without asking again.

Marcia fetched the jar and carried it into the yard. Her mother watched her pour vinegar onto the earth.

“Your father had that jar from his father,” she said.

“The jar’s all right.”

“It’s the vinegar I’m worried about.” She tipped it to catch the light and examined the sediment. “It was nearly empty. I suppose we can spare it.”

Lucius appeared with the shoes. He had rinsed them, but they still smelled sharp. “I’ll leave them outside.”

“You’ll take them away,” Marcia’s mother said.

He tied their laces together and hung them around his neck. “Better?”

“No, but I don’t have to look at you much longer.” She took the jar from Marcia. “You’re both in the way. Go.”

They went through the yard gate. Lucius had the tablet, the shoes and two of the steward’s coins. He had given away a third to the fig seller and the fourth to his father. Marcia thought of pointing this out, but he knew it already.

At the bridge, Celer’s cart stood outside the magistrates’ building. A crowd had gathered around it. A town officer was trying to move people back, and the mule had found a patch of weeds between two stones. She tore at them patiently while the officer argued with a man who wanted to see the body.

Lucius slowed. “Will you come with me?”

“Into the building?”

“To my father’s house.”

“Why?”

“He’ll behave better if you’re there.”

“That’s a poor reason to invite someone to supper.”

“He has good olives.”

“Those were ours.”

“I know. Your mother sells them to him.”

She could have refused. She had work waiting at home and a mother who would not be pleased to lose her for the evening. But Lucius had asked without a joke, and she had seen his father’s face when he spoke of Celer. There was something in it she wanted to understand.

“I’ll come,” she said. “You can take the shoes off first.”

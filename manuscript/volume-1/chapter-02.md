# Chapter Two

Lucius’s father had once told him that an honorable man paid his debts before he ate. The rule had seemed a good one until Lucius found out that a man could decide which of his debts were honorable.

The house above the river belonged to his father’s sister. She had lent them two rooms after his mother died and had then spent twelve years regretting the arrangement aloud. Her name was Damaris. She met them at the door with a lamp in one hand and a ladle in the other, and her first question was whether Marcia had come to stay.

“For supper,” Marcia said.

“Then come in before he changes his mind.” Damaris moved the lamp aside and looked at her nephew. “Your father says he wants to talk to you. If you’ve stolen anything, give it back now.”

“He’s paid for it,” Lucius said.

“I’m sure he has. Put the shoes outside.”

Marcia stepped into the room ahead of him. Lucius hung the shoes on a peg by the door, then set the tablet on the table and drew Celer’s tally from under its cord. His father was sitting on the far side of the hearth, watching a pot. He had removed his outer tunic and rolled the sleeves of the one beneath. A burn crossed his forearm, an old scar Lucius had seen every day of his childhood without ever being told how it had happened.

Damaris peered into the pot. “You’ve let it stick.”

“It hasn’t been on long.”

“That’s when it sticks.”

She took the ladle and stirred. Lucius’s father got up and went to the table. His name was Gaius, though he was known at the quarry as the Greek because his mother had been born in Nicaea and he counted in her language when he was tired. He had a citizen’s name, inherited from his father, and no patience with men who believed a name could feed a household. He had once made Lucius recite the names of the emperors in order, then had sent him out to collect a debt from a man who kept a dog at the gate. Lucius had returned without the money and with a bite in his leg. His father had dressed the wound, and on the following morning sent him back with a stick.

“Close the door,” Gaius said.

Lucius did. His father looked at the tablet.

“I spoke to the steward,” Lucius said. “We agreed a price.”

“You agreed it. He’ll pay what he likes.”

“He paid part already.”

“You gave it to me.”

“I gave you a quarter. You said I owed two.”

“I can count.”

“That’s what the steward says.”

Damaris set down the ladle. “If you’re going to fight, wait until I’ve served. I want both of you hungry enough to stop.”

Gaius took the tally from the table. “Where did this come from?”

“Celer’s wife.”

“What did she want you to do with it?”

“Find out what it means.”

“She should have given it to the magistrate.”

“She did. They said it was rubbish.”

Gaius rubbed the notch with his thumb. Lucius noticed that he did not touch the stained end. “It’s a quarry tally. A laborer carries one half and the overseer keeps the other. At the end of the week they put them together and pay for the days marked.”

“There are only two marks.”

“Then he worked two days.”

“He was a carter.”

“Perhaps he worked two days.”

Marcia had been watching the pot. Now she said, “Could he have carried someone else’s tally?”

“He could have carried anything. You found him, didn’t you? Was it in the cart?”

“In his hand.”

Damaris turned from the hearth. “You said he was robbed.”

“His purse was gone,” Lucius said.

“His wife told me his belt was cut,” Marcia said. “She saw it when they washed him.”

Gaius laid the tally down. “Give it back to her.”

“She wants to know whose it is.”

“There’s a letter on it. It might be his. It might be anyone’s.”

“Your name starts with the same letter,” Lucius said.

“So does yours. Yours starts with two.”

Damaris made a noise behind them. Lucius turned to see her carrying the pot to the table. She had wrapped a cloth around each handle, and her arms trembled under the weight. He went to help her, but she set the pot down before he reached it.

“I asked you to mend the hook over the fire,” she said to him.

“I mended it.”

“You tied it with a cord. It caught fire.”

“It held until it caught fire.”

She gave him the ladle. “Serve.”

There were lentils, a little onion, and a piece of salted pork that Damaris cut into four. Lucius received the smallest portion. She gave the largest to Marcia, explaining that she was young and had work to do. Gaius drank water, then poured wine into his cup and added more water. He had a great dislike of being seen drunk. Lucius had only once seen him truly lose his balance, on the day they buried his mother; even then Gaius had leaned against a wall and told everyone it was the wall that was crooked.

For a while they ate. Through the open window came the sound of the river and a dog barking at something that did not answer it. Damaris asked about Marcia’s mother and whether the burn on her hand had healed. Marcia said it had, but the skin tightened in cold weather. Damaris offered a salve that smelled so strongly of garlic that Lucius protested before she had finished describing it.

“You’ve never tried it,” she said.

“No one could get close enough.”

“Your mother used it.”

His father set down his spoon. It was not that his mother was never mentioned in the house. Damaris mentioned her often, especially when she wanted to remind Lucius of a virtue he was failing to display. But the name still had the power to interrupt a meal.

“She did,” Gaius said. “On his knee.”

Lucius looked up. “What happened to my knee?”

“You fell off a wall.”

“I remember that.”

“You fell off it twice,” Damaris said. “The second time she told you not to come crying to her. You went to your father instead, and he carried you in.”

Gaius had picked up his cup again. He drank without looking at either of them. Lucius tried to remember being carried. He remembered the wall, and Marcia daring him to walk along its top; he remembered blood on his shin and the shame of crying where she could see. The rest had gone. He was irritated by the gap, and still more irritated that his aunt possessed a part of his life he could not take back from her.

Marcia said, “You told me you jumped.”

“I did.”

“Off the wall?”

“Onto it. I hadn’t meant to get down yet.”

Damaris laughed. Gaius smiled into his cup, and for a moment Lucius had the old feeling of having made a room safe. Then he saw that his father’s attention had returned to the tally.

“You know who it belongs to,” he said.

Damaris stopped smiling.

“No,” Gaius said.

“You looked at it as soon as you came in.”

“Because it was on my table.”

“You told us what it was without looking at the mark.”

“I’ve seen one before.”

“And you asked whether Celer had it in the cart.”

“Where else would he have it?”

“In his hand.”

Gaius set the cup down so hard that wine splashed onto the table. “You asked me what it was. I told you. Take it back to the woman.”

Marcia said, “If it matters, she ought to tell the magistrate.”

“It doesn’t matter.”

“Then why are you angry?”

“Because your friend won’t listen.” He pushed back from the table. “Tomorrow a man comes from Nicomedia. The steward has asked for the books. I need them copied tonight. If you don’t want to do it, leave the tablet and I’ll find someone else.”

Lucius drew the tablet toward him. “I want the other half of the tally.”

“You want a great many things.”

“Where is it?”

His father put his hand flat on the table. It was a broad hand, with small cuts around the nails and a dark stain between finger and thumb where he held his pen. “I have been responsible for you since you were born. I’ve kept a roof over you, and I’ve paid for your mistakes. You want me to answer every question you can think of. You want it now. And when I’ve given you the answer, you’ll find a new question, because what you want isn’t the answer. It’s to make me stand here and give it to you.”

Lucius felt Marcia watching him. His father had done this before: named something ugly and plausible, then left Lucius to prove that the truth was more complicated. The trouble was that it often was not much more complicated.

“I’ll copy the tablet,” he said.

“Good.”

“But I’ll give her the tally back myself.”

Gaius withdrew his hand. “Do as you please.”

Damaris rose and took the dishes. Lucius offered to help, but she sent him outside to fetch water. Marcia came with him. The well was shared by three houses, and at this hour a line of jars stood beside it, their owners talking in the dusk. A girl Lucius recognized from the baker’s house was lowering the bucket. He waited until she had filled her jars before he took the rope.

“You were both enjoying that,” Marcia said.

“My father and I?”

“He had you at his table. You had him talking about something he didn’t want to talk about.”

“You were enjoying it too.”

“I was eating.”

“You asked him a question.”

“He was answering it until you decided you’d caught him.”

Lucius lowered the bucket. It struck the water with a sound that came up the shaft a moment later. “You think I should have let him lie?”

“I think you should have let him finish.”

He hauled the bucket up. The rope rasped against his palms. “He wanted the tally.”

“He didn’t take it.”

“Because we were there.”

“Then put it somewhere he can’t find it.”

He poured water into the jar. The girl from the bakery had gone; only her younger brother remained, looking into the well as if he could see all the way to the bottom. Lucius sent the bucket down again.

“When we were children,” Marcia said, “he came to the yard one night and brought my mother a sack of grain. She told me he’d been paid in grain and didn’t have room for it.”

“Did she?”

“Years later she told me he’d bought it. We couldn’t afford food that winter.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I was ashamed.”

“You didn’t need to be.”

“I know that now.” She lifted the filled jar onto the low wall. “I’m not saying he’s a good man. I’m saying he’s more than the man you argue with.”

Lucius set the bucket on the rim. He had known Marcia as a child with bare knees and a sharp stone in her fist, prepared to hit anyone who touched her mother’s things. He remembered the winter she spoke of: the snow that had stayed in the shade by the wall, her mother’s hands raw from the cold vats, Marcia refusing to come to his house for a meal. He had thought she was angry with him. He had retaliated by refusing to visit her for three days, until she came to find him and told him he was a fool.

“I didn’t know,” he said.

“No. You were busy being offended.”

“I was offended for four days.”

“Three.”

“You came on the fourth.”

She picked up the jar. “Do you want to carry this, or shall I?”

He took it from her. She let him, although she was strong enough to carry two.

* * *

By the time Marcia reached the dye-yard, the gate was shut and the officer stood outside it. He held a short staff and wore a cloak pinned neatly at his shoulder. Celer’s cart had been moved out of sight. Behind the gate, voices rose and fell; her mother’s was the clearest.

The officer knocked with his staff. “Open.”

The gate opened a hand’s breadth. Her mother put her face in the gap. “I’ve told you. There’s no room for the cart.”

“Where is it?”

“Ask the mule.”

“You’ve taken the wool.”

“I take wool every day.”

“This is evidence.”

“Of what?”

“That’s for the magistrate to decide.”

“Then ask him.”

The officer drew breath. Marcia stepped up beside him before he could use it. “Where is the mule?” she asked her mother.

“Celer’s brother took her.”

“And the cart?”

“With her.”

The officer turned. “Did you help unload it?”

“No. I’ve been at supper.”

“You’re the one who found him.”

“His mule came down to our yard.”

“Did you see anyone with him?”

“No.”

He looked toward the road, where Lucius waited. “Who’s that?”

“Lucius.”

“What’s he carrying?”

“A tablet.”

The officer called him over. Lucius came, still wearing the damp shoes. He opened the tablet when asked and held it so the officer could see. The officer studied the wax, then looked at it from the other side. Marcia could not tell whether he was reading the figures or looking for a reason to take them.

“Quarry accounts,” Lucius said. “I’m copying them for the steward.”

“The magistrate may want those.”

“Then he can ask the steward.”

The officer shut the tablet. “The steward will answer to him tomorrow.”

“Then he won’t need me.”

The officer held the tablet by its cord. The tally was tucked behind the covers, mostly hidden. Marcia watched his fingers move along the leather. He could feel the piece of wood there; he drew it out and held it up to the light from the yard.

“What’s this?”

“A tally,” Lucius said.

“Whose?”

“Celer’s wife gave it to me.”

The officer looked at the mark. “Why?”

“She wanted to know what it was.”

“And you told her?”

“I told her it might be a quarry tally.”

The officer put the piece of wood back beneath the cord. “She should give anything she finds to the magistrate.”

“She did.”

“What?”

“She showed it to him. He said it was rubbish.”

The officer’s mouth moved, but no sound came out. At last he returned the tablet. “Don’t leave town tomorrow.”

“I live here.”

“You don’t live everywhere you go.”

He turned back to the gate. Marcia’s mother had closed it while he was occupied. He knocked again. This time she did not open it.

A woman carrying a basket stopped on the road. The officer told her to move along. She moved as far as the corner and waited there. A boy came out of the house opposite and sat on the step to watch. Marcia had seen crowds form around a fight, a runaway horse, and once a jar of oil that had split open in the market. It took very little to make an occasion in town, but once there was one, people disliked being sent away from it.

“I’m going to fetch the magistrate,” the officer called through the gate.

“Bring him a cup,” her mother called back. “I’ve no clean ones.”

He went. The woman at the corner followed at a distance, her basket forgotten. The boy on the step waited until they had turned out of sight, then ran after them.

Marcia knocked. Her mother opened the gate wide enough for them to come in, then barred it again.

Celer’s wife was in the yard, washing blood from her hands at the trough. The wool sack stood beneath the awning. It had been opened, and a length of cloth lay across its mouth. Marcia saw the bundle was not as full as it had been in the cart.

“You’ve taken some out?” she asked.

“I sorted it,” her mother said. “The best is on top.”

“Where’s the rest?”

“In the storehouse.”

“Why?”

“Because if the magistrate comes for a sack of wool, he’ll take the sack. He won’t count what’s inside.”

Celer’s wife dried her hands on her skirt. “He can have the rest when he pays me for it.”

Marcia looked at the gate. “What did Celer owe the farmers?”

“I don’t know. He bought from some and collected from others. There were families who paid the contractor in wool instead of coin.”

“Then some of it may belong to them.”

“Some of it may belong to the contractor. Some of it may belong to me. I don’t know which, because the list is gone.” She held out her hands. The creases were still dark. “My husband’s on a table in the magistrate’s house. They’re asking how much money he had. I don’t know that either. I asked them what I’m supposed to bury him with, and the magistrate told me to wait.”

Her voice had been steady until then. She pressed her palms together, and Marcia saw that she was trying not to shake. Her mother took the woman’s arm and guided her toward the room at the back of the yard.

“You can sit,” she said. “There’s water.”

“I don’t want water.”

“Then sit without it.”

They went inside. Lucius put the tablet on a bench and came to the sack. He lifted the cloth, looked at the wool and let it fall.

“Your mother’s taking a risk.”

“She knows.”

“If the steward claims it—”

“He can claim it. He’ll have to say where he got the money to buy it.”

“He says he paid yesterday.”

“My mother thinks Celer collected it against rent.”

“Does she know?”

“No. But she thinks the woman should have enough to bury him before anyone else gets paid.”

Lucius leaned against the bench. “My father says you should give her the tally back.”

“What do you say?”

“I say I should copy the accounts first.”

“Why?”

“The steward is expecting them. I’ve been paid.”

“Your father said to copy what was there. He didn’t say you couldn’t read it.”

He smiled, but did not reach for the tablet. “If I find something, what do we do?”

“We tell her.”

“And if she tells the magistrate?”

“She can do as she likes.”

“He might ask where she got it.”

“Then she can tell him.”

Lucius looked at her. “You don’t know what my father owes the steward.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

“Then read.”

From the room came the sound of Celer’s wife speaking, too quietly for Marcia to hear the words. Her mother answered. A door opened at the back of the house and shut again. Marcia had helped her mother hide wool once before, from a creditor who had the right to take it. They had put it beneath the floor of the storehouse, wrapping it in old sailcloth against the damp. The creditor had found nothing and gone away. Later her mother had sold half the wool to pay him and had kept the rest for her own work. Marcia had asked why they had hidden it at all. Her mother had said that a person who came to take everything should be made to leave something behind.

Lucius opened the tablet. “Bring me a lamp,” he said.

* * *

The man from Nicomedia arrived before sunrise, while Lucius was still awake. He came with a driver, two pack animals and a woman who sat sideways on one of the animals with her feet wrapped in a blanket. They stopped outside the house where Lucius rented his room. The driver knocked on the door below, and the landlord, who had gone to bed drunk, shouted from an upstairs window that no one lived there.

Lucius opened his own window. “What do you want?”

The man looked up. He wore a traveling cloak whose hem was thick with dust. “Gaius.”

“He lives above the river.”

“I’ve been there. They said I’d find his son here.”

“Who are you?”

“Someone who would like to come inside.”

The landlord appeared behind the man with a lamp, having discovered that people did live in his house after all. Lucius went down to the door. The driver had already led the animals into the yard, where the woman was trying to climb down without putting her foot to the ground.

“Get a stool,” the man told the driver.

The driver fetched an overturned basket, but the woman waved it away. “It’ll break. Put your shoulder there.”

He did, and she lowered herself with one hand on him and one on the animal’s back. Her left foot was swollen. She wore a plain traveling dress and a gold ring with a red stone. When she stood, she leaned against the wall and removed one of her shoes.

“The road gets worse every year,” the man said.

“The road was fine,” she said. “It was the ditch you put us in.”

“I didn’t put you in it.”

“You told the driver to go around the cart. It was your ditch.”

Lucius’s landlord had gone to look at the pack animals. The man asked him where they could get water, and the landlord pointed to a trough. The driver set about unfastening the packs while the woman examined her ankle. Lucius fetched a stool from the room below and brought it into the yard.

“Thank you,” she said. “If the leg comes off, you can have the shoe.”

Her companion did not smile. “My name is Severus. I’m here about the quarry. Your father says you have the accounts.”

“They’re upstairs.”

“I want to see them.”

“I haven’t finished the copy.”

“I didn’t ask for the copy.”

Lucius took him up. The room was narrow, with a bed against one wall and a table by the window. Wax crumbs and scraps of papyrus lay on the table. A lamp had burned down beside them. Lucius had copied most of the tablet and made notes on a separate piece of papyrus, which he had folded and put beneath the mattress. He had also taken Celer’s tally home with him. It was under the mattress too, though he could feel the place where it lay whenever he turned over.

Severus examined the tablet without sitting. He wore no rings and had a small white scar above one eyebrow. Lucius saw ink on the side of his hand. He liked that; it suggested a man who did at least some of his own writing.

“You’ve copied these?” Severus asked.

“Most of them.”

“Have you added them?”

“Yes.”

“Do they agree?”

“With what?”

“With each other.”

“The figures add up.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Lucius pulled the stool away from the table. Severus declined it. He set the tablet down, opened the copy and ran his finger along the columns.

“Your hand is good,” he said.

“Thank you.”

“Can you read Greek?”

“Yes.”

“Latin?”

“Yes.”

“Can you write either as well as you copy figures?”

“Yes.”

“That must have been expensive for your father.”

“My aunt paid for it.”

“Is she rich?”

“No.”

“Then why did she pay?”

“She thought it would be useful.”

Severus closed the copy. “She was right.”

He sat down at last. Lucius stood at the window, where the sky over the roof opposite had begun to pale. The woman in the yard was giving instructions about the packs. The driver was pretending not to hear her, and she was making him repeat each instruction before she gave him the next.

“I work for a man in Nicomedia,” Severus said. “He has an interest in the quarry. Last year he was told that it had produced a certain quantity of stone. He paid for that quantity to be transported. This year the governor’s people want to know what became of it.”

“Why?”

“There’s a building at Nicomedia. It needs stone.”

“Then they should buy some.”

“They did.”

Lucius looked at the tablet. “The accounts aren’t complete.”

“They rarely are.”

“There are marks for loads that aren’t listed under either quarry.” He pointed to a column. “These are the totals my father gave the steward. They’re entered here again, but with a different number of loads.”

“More or fewer?”

“More. The weights are the same.”

“Could the loads have been divided?”

“Yes.”

“Could a load have gone by a different road?”

“Yes.”

“Could the clerk have made a mistake?”

“Yes.”

“Could he have been told to?”

Lucius looked at him. “You’ve come a long way to ask questions you know the answers to.”

“I’ve come to find out which answer is true.” Severus tapped the copy. “You said the weights are the same. Where?”

Lucius showed him. Severus took a stylus from his belt and made two marks on a spare patch of wax. He did not ask permission. Lucius could have objected; the tablet belonged to the steward, and he was responsible for it. Instead he watched Severus draw a line through one of the figures, write it again, then restore it as it had been.

“A mistake in the copying,” Severus said.

“Mine?”

“No. The clerk who made the tablet.”

“How do you know?”

“He left the weight the same when he changed the number of loads. Unless someone weighed each load twice, that shouldn’t happen.”

“Perhaps it was a mistake.”

“Perhaps. Can you show me the original books?”

“My father has them.”

“Then take me to him.”

The woman in the yard called up. “Severus. Are you buying him or questioning him?”

He went to the window. “Do you need something?”

“A better family.”

“You’ll have to wait until we reach Nicomedia.”

“You told me that at the last town.”

“And I was right.”

She saw Lucius behind him and raised a hand. “Come down when he’s finished. I want to know where I can get a bath.”

Severus turned back. “My sister. She’s been promising to leave me since we set out. I keep hoping the next town will make her an offer.”

Lucius gathered the tablet and the copy. “The bathhouse opens later.”

“Good. I’ll leave her there.”

He started for the door, then stopped. “A man was killed on the road yesterday.”

Lucius kept his hands on the papers. “His name was Celer.”

“Did you know him?”

“Yes.”

“Did he have the accounts?”

“No.”

“What did he have?”

“Wool.”

“Was it taken?”

“No.”

“Odd.”

“His purse was taken.”

“Less odd.” Severus studied him. “You can bring the papers. I won’t make you leave them here.”

On the stairs, Lucius put one hand against the wall to steady himself. He had not slept. The tally and his notes remained under the mattress, and he was not certain whether Severus knew. The man had asked about Celer before Lucius had mentioned him. That could mean he had spoken to someone at the quarry or the magistrate’s house. It could mean anything. Lucius disliked not knowing and disliked more the possibility that he had been led into showing how much he knew.

Downstairs the woman had the landlord’s wife kneeling beside her with a bowl of water. She was protesting that the ankle was not worth the attention, while holding it out to be bathed. She had a long, narrow face, a small scar on her chin, and the assured manner of someone accustomed to getting assistance without having to explain why she deserved it.

“His name is Lucius,” Severus told her. “He knows where the bathhouse is.”

“Excellent. I’m Claudia.” She held out her hand. When Lucius took it, she said, “Not to kiss. I need help standing.”

He braced her while she rose. Her grip was surprisingly strong. “Will you be here long?” he asked.

“That depends on him. I only came because he promised we’d be in Nicomedia before the festival.”

“There’s a festival here next week,” the landlord’s wife said.

“Is there dancing?”

“There was last year.”

“And wine?”

“There was until the magistrates ordered it stopped.”

Claudia looked at Severus. “Perhaps we’ll stay.”

The driver laughed. Severus gave him a look, and he returned to the packs. Lucius went to the yard gate with the tablet under his arm. The landlord’s wife called after him that his rent was due. He said he would bring it by evening and saw her exchange a look with her husband. The promise had been made before.

On the road above the river, a woman passed them carrying a basket of loaves. Severus asked her where the quarry was. She pointed with her chin and went on, then stopped and turned to look at Lucius. He recognized her as Celer’s sister-in-law. She said nothing, but he knew by the way she held her basket that she had something she wanted to say and would not say it in front of Severus.

His father’s door was open. Damaris stood in the yard, wringing out a cloth. She had a smear of soot across one cheek and looked at Lucius with an expression he could not read.

“Where is he?” Lucius asked.

“Gone.”

“To the quarry?”

“No.”

Severus stepped past Lucius. “Did he say where?”

“He left a message for the steward.”

“What message?”

“He said he’d be back.”

“When?”

“He didn’t say.” She spread the cloth over a wall. “There’s bread inside if you want it.”

Lucius went into the house. His father’s sleeping mat had been rolled up. His belt was gone from its peg, and so was the small chest in which he kept his papers. One of his sandals remained under the table. Lucius picked it up. Its strap had broken and had been repaired with a strip of rawhide. There was no reason for his father to leave one sandal behind unless he had another pair, or had gone without.

“Did he take shoes?” he asked.

Damaris came to the doorway. “He took the good pair.”

“When did he leave?”

“Before it was light.”

“Why didn’t you send for me?”

“I was here. You weren’t.”

Severus examined the room. “The books?”

“I told you,” Damaris said. “He took his chest.”

“I want the quarry books, not his chest.”

“He keeps them together.”

Severus looked at Lucius. “Did he tell you he was going?”

“No.”

“Did you tell him I was coming?”

“He knew.”

“Did you tell him what you found in the accounts?”

“I didn’t find it until after I left here.”

Damaris said, “What did you find?”

Severus did not answer her. He held out his hand to Lucius. “The copy.”

Lucius gave it to him. Severus unrolled it, glanced at the figures and rolled it again. “Come to the quarry.”

“I’ve done the work.”

“You haven’t been paid for it.”

“I can wait.”

“I can’t.”

Damaris took the sandal from Lucius and set it on the shelf. “Go with him. I’ll be here if your father comes back.”

Lucius wanted to ask her whether Gaius had been afraid, whether he had eaten, whether he had said anything about Celer. But Severus stood between them, and Damaris was looking at the open door. Lucius had known her to protect her brother when he did not deserve it. He had also known her to protect Lucius from him. He could not tell, this morning, which of them needed it.

Outside, Severus waited until Lucius had closed the door. “If your father is hiding the books, he may have a good reason.”

“Then why do you want them?”

“Because I have a better reason to find them.”

“And what’s that?”

“I was sent for them.”

Lucius thought of the piece of wood under his mattress, of his notes, and of Celer’s wife, who had entrusted him with something she did not understand. He had meant to return it to her before she had to ask. Now Severus was walking beside him toward the quarry, and the town was waking around them. A baker opened his shutters. A boy carried water up the hill. Across the river, smoke rose from the dye-yard.

“What happens if you don’t find the books?” Lucius asked.

Severus looked ahead. “Someone will have to explain why.”

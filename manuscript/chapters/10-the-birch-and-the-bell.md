# Chapter Ten: The Birch and the Bell

They consecrated Bochenek on the eighteenth of January, in the cathedral of St. John, in a cold that made the candles gutter, and the state came in its good black cars and stood in the front with their hats held in front of them, and the pews were full of priests and nuns and laypeople who had come because a bishop is a thing one goes to see.

Janusz sat at the back, under the gallery, in a borrowed overcoat, and was not invited to sit anywhere else.

It was a fine ceremony. The old rites had been kept, and the choir had been rehearsed, and the ring and the crozier and the mitre were real and very old, and out in the square afterwards the people pressed around the new bishop and kissed his ring, and an old peasant woman from Wyszków went down on her knees on the cobbles to him, and he took her by both hands and lifted her up himself and said something to her that made her cry.

Halina stood at the edge of the crowd with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, and watched.

"Well?" she said, when Janusz came out by the side door.

"He is a good bishop," he said. "Better than I would have been. He will build twenty churches and not lose one of them to the state, and he will never say a thing that costs him, and he will be there in 1970 when I am an old man in a village, and he will still be there in 1980 when half of the men who stood in the front of that cathedral have been moved to other jobs and forgotten." He turned up his collar, remembered he never did, and turned it down again. "It is a strange thing to be relieved and ashamed at the same time."

"That is what it is to be a Pole," Halina said. "It is a national condition."

He laughed. They walked away from the cathedral, down toward the river, past the monument to Copernicus with the snow on his shoulders and his arm around the armillary sphere.

The last Sunday of January was his last Sunday in the parish, and he said the eleven o'clock Mass in a church that was fuller than it had been on Christmas, and preached for eleven minutes — the shortest sermon of his life — on the woman at the well and the water that does not run out. Then he went out to the door and stood there while the parish came past him.

It took an hour and a half. The old women came with their hands out. Pani Zofia, who was ninety-one and had been carried up the steps by her grandson, gave him a pair of knitted socks. Pan Tadeusz the sacristan, who had been in that church since 1931 and who had not spoken of the matter at all since December, shook his hand and said, "The boiler will be all right. I will not let them take it back to the old way," and then went into the sacristy and shut the door. The railwayman came with his hat off. The men from the works came in a group, and one of them said, "Father, the twenty-two is running again," which meant the table-tennis club, which meant that somebody had bought a table out of his own money and put it in a cellar, and Janusz did not ask who.

Zbyszek Malinowski came last. He had come in a suit that was too small for him and a white shirt and a tie borrowed from somebody.

"I am being called up in March," he said. "Two years. Signals."

"Write to me."

"I will write to you." He stood there with his hands at his sides. "Father, I wanted to tell you. In November I was going to ask you whether it was all true, the whole thing, the resurrection, everything. I was going to give you a book by a Frenchman. I thought I was very clever." He looked at the floor. "Then in December you stood in the pulpit and told us you were a traitor. And I understood that you could not have done that if it was not true, because a man who is lying to himself cannot do that."

"Men do worse things than that for lies."

"Yes," Zbyszek said. "But not that."

Janusz took his hand and held it, and then pulled the boy's head down and kissed him on the forehead, which was not something he had ever done.

At three o'clock Pani Genowefa fed him cabbage soup and a piece of pork and two potatoes, and sat opposite him with her arms folded and watched him eat.

"You will not eat a decent meal in Wyszków."

"There will be a housekeeper in Wyszków."

"There will be an old woman in Wyszków who has been keeping house for a priest since 1946, and she will have her own ways, and she will not know about the salt." Genowefa sniffed. "You will write to me."

"Once a month."

"Once a week."

"That is not what you told me in 1952."

"In 1952 you were a young man with ideas. Now you are an old priest with a suitcase. Once a week."

He promised. She stood up and took his plate and put it in the sink and did not turn around, and said, to the window: "The kettle is broke. It has been broke since Thursday. I have been meaning to tell you." And then she went out of the kitchen into her own room and closed the door.

He left on the sixth of February, on the nine-forty from Warszawa Wschodnia, with a suitcase, a wooden crate of books, a crate of vestments, and the photograph of his parents wrapped in a shirt.

There was nobody to see him off except Halina, who came with a paper bag of apples.

"Eat them on the train," she said. "They are from Maryla's cellar. She has been giving me apples for a week as though I were emigrating."

They stood on the platform. There was the usual business of the railway: porters, a woman with three hens in a crate, a soldier asleep on a bench. The snow on the platform had been trodden to gray slush. A locomotive further down the tracks sent up a long plume of steam that hung and drifted.

"Wyszków," she said. "What is it like?"

"Eighty kilometres of the Bug. A church, a school, a mill, a garrison. In 1944 the whole center was burned, and they have rebuilt it with the money the state gave them and the money the state did not give them. There are pines. The parish is four thousand souls and eleven villages and one of them is called Deskurów, and there is a chapel there that has not had a Mass in it since 1957."

"You will have it open by June."

"I will have it open by May. I have written to the prefectorial office about the flooring. They will say no. I will write again in April."

She laughed. The train came in. The platform shook. He picked up his suitcase and she picked up the crate of books, and they carried them along to the third carriage and put them in the rack.

"Janusz."

"Halina."

"Get on the train."

"I am on the train."

"You are standing in the door of the train, and the conductor is looking at you, and if you stand there much longer you are going to say something that we will both regret."

"Yes," he said. "I was going to say that you should come to Wyszków in June."

"You should not say that."

"No."

"And I will come in June."

"Halina."

"Not to the rectory. There is a hotel by the market square. I will come on the first Sunday in June, and I will sit at the back of your church, and afterwards you will walk me to the bus, and the whole town will see it and be scandalized, and it will be one more thing for them to decide about, and they will decide in your favor, because the people of Wyszków are not fools." She held out her hand. He took it. "Go on. Go and be a parish priest in a burned town. It is a better life than being a bishop."

He got on the train. The whistle went. The doors closed. She stood on the platform with snow on her hat and watched the carriage move away, and he stood at the window with one hand on the glass, and neither of them did anything foolish, and the train went around the curve and out of sight.

She stood there for a while in the cold. Then she went down the station steps and out into the street and took the tram home to Marszałkowska, where the flat was already half packed in boxes, because she had to be out of it by the first of March.

She had found a room on the top floor of a house in Praga, on a street off Brzeska, with a window facing a courtyard and a lime tree, at a rent of two hundred złoty a month. She had found work as well, then lost it in the same week, and then found another: four mornings a week in the office of the clinic on Radzymińska where Maryla had intervened and where the chief accountant, a Party man of long standing, had said to her on the first day, "You are the one from the ministry. I do not care what you did. You will keep the ledgers correctly, and you will not discuss politics with the nurses." She agreed to both, and kept the second one.

So she kept the ledgers correctly, and did not discuss politics with the nurses, and in the evenings she walked to the church on Brzeska Street and sat in the last pew while the altar boys lit the candles for Benediction.

In March a letter came to Wanda Kowalik's room on Brzeska Street from the district prosecutor's office. The matter of the crane at the Nowotki works had been reopened. The director and the chief engineer had been dismissed from their posts and referred to the factory party committee; the safety commission had been reconstituted; and the widow's pension had been recalculated and increased by two hundred and ten złoty a month, with arrears from October. Nobody was charged with anything. Nobody was going to be. It was the most that the machine could be made to do, and it had taken three copies of a document that did not exist, in a tray on the fourth floor, to make it do that much. Wanda read the letter standing at her kitchen table and then sat down and cried for a quarter of an hour, and when Halina came on Saturday she was still angry about it, and they ate bread and dripping and did not talk about it, and it was, on the whole, the best thing either of them had ever done.

The winter went. In April the ice went out of the Vistula in a single noisy night, and there were floods in the low streets on the Praga side, and in the clinic they had to move the filing cabinets up to the second floor. In May the chestnuts came out. In June, on the first Sunday, she took the early bus north to Wyszków and sat at the back of a rebuilt church with a new tin roof and a statue of the Virgin that had been rescued from the ruins of the old one, and heard a priest she had known since 1938 preach on the man born blind. Afterwards he walked her to the bus stop in the market square, and the whole town saw it, and two widows in the front pew discussed it for a week, and the housekeeper said something at the kitchen table that was not entirely a complaint.

She went again in July. In August Maryla came with her and they stayed a week at the hotel by the market square and were given the best room and charged almost nothing for it, and Maryla said, at the end of the week, walking to the station: "He is not your husband and he never will be, and half the town thinks he is, and the other half thinks he ought to be. Are you going to be able to live with that?" And Halina said, "I have lived with considerably worse," and Maryla said, "That is not an answer," and Halina said, "No. It is a fact."

And in September, at the end of the harvest, she and Janusz went together to Brzóza.

The bus from Puławy came down the hill through the pines and turned at the bend of the river, and the village was there in the way it had always been there: the road, the houses, the poplars, the church with its tower.

They got out at the church square. There were four people waiting there: a boy of about fifteen, with a bicycle, who had been sent by the parish priest to meet them; an old woman selling eggs from a basket; an old man on a bench outside the co-operative; and a dog.

Halina's mother's house at the edge of the village, near the forest, had stood empty for eight months. The roof leaked over the kitchen. A pane was broken in the small window. Someone had cut the lock off the shed and taken the scythe and the spade. But the stove was sound, and the walls were standing, and when she got the door open and the shutters back the light came in and lay on the bare floorboards exactly where it had lain in 1938.

"Her chair is gone," Halina said. She stood in the middle of the room. "There was a chair by the window. She sat there in the evening. It was her mother's chair. It was 1907 or something. Somebody has taken it."

"We will get you another chair."

"It is not the chair." She touched the wall where the crucifix had hung, and where the nail was still there and the plaster around it gone pale. "It is that they came into her house and took her chair and thought nothing of it. There is no malice in it. That is what I cannot get used to. Malice I understand. It is the indifference."

They swept out the kitchen. They put up the shutters on the inner side against the weather. They walked to the cemetery and stood at the two graves, and then at the third grave — smaller, older, with a stone that had been repaired — and Janusz said the prayers for the dead, and after them Halina said, in a voice that was almost casual, "Tadek. It is the third time I have been here. I am going to come every year now. I am going to be a bore about it."

And then they walked down through the village, past the co-operative and the new barn and the tractor shed where Góra's name was painted on a board, and past the corner where the manor had been — there was a school there now, a long low building with wide windows and a photograph of Gomułka in the lobby — and out to the meadows above the river, where the birch tree stood.

It had been there for a hundred years. It was there still, leaning slightly out over the water, its bark peeling in long pale strips, its leaves beginning to go yellow in the first week of September. The river ran below the bank, brown and quiet, and on the far side there was a man with a fishing rod, sitting on an upturned bucket, absolutely motionless, as a man with a fishing rod will sit.

Janusz stopped at the top of the bank.

"How many years?" he said.

"Since 1939. Twenty years."

"It was August."

"It was the fourteenth of August, 1943. The day they shot Tadek. And two days later you came out of Lublin with your hand bound up and told me you had caught it on a nail." She looked at him. "I knew it was not a nail. I did not ask."

They sat down on the bank with their backs against the trunk. The bark was warm. It was one of those afternoons in early September when the light lies flat and gold across the fields and the air smells of cut hay and the first rot of the year, and everything seems, for an hour, to have been forgiven.

"He is still there," Janusz said. "The man with the rod."

"There is always a man with a rod."

"When I was a boy I thought he was the same man. I thought he had been sitting there since the Piasts."

"He probably has. He is the spirit of the river. He has been sitting on that bucket since the year 966, and he has caught nothing, and he is entirely content." She leaned her head back against the tree. "Janusz, I want to tell you something, and I do not want you to answer it, and I do not want you to be a priest about it."

"I will do my best."

"I have thought about it for a long time. Since Christmas. What I am going to do with the rest of my life." She looked at the river. "I am not going to be a Party official. I am not going to be a martyr. I am not going to be the woman at the clinic with a past, either, the one who is very quiet and whom nobody asks about. I am going to be a teacher."

He turned his head.

"You were going to be a teacher," he said. "In 1938."

"I was going to be a teacher in this village, and I was going to marry a blacksmith's son, and we were going to have four children and a garden and be very poor and very tiresome." She said it lightly, as an inventory of goods. "Well. I am not going to have the blacksmith's son or the four children. But there is a thing called the pedagogic course in Warsaw for people without qualifications, and it takes a year and a half, and Maryla knows a woman who runs it, and it is not the Party's course, it is the co-operative's, and you can teach infants in a village school with it. I have written to the school in Brzóza. They want a teacher for the first class in September 1960. The present teacher is a good woman from Lublin who has been here since 1954 and wants to go home."

"You would come back here."

"I would come back here." She said it without drama. "I have a house. I have a garden, if I can get the garden back, and I have a mother buried in the cemetery and a brother and a girl who is not buried anywhere, and there is a school with forty children in it, and half of them will be Góra's grandchildren, and I will teach them to read." She looked at him. "I am not doing it for the dead. I want to be very clear that I am not doing it for the dead. I am doing it because when I was sixteen I wanted to be a teacher more than I wanted anything in the world except you, and I have spent twenty years being something else, and I am tired of being something else."

"Then do it," he said.

"You see. I told you not to be a priest."

"That was not priestly. That was a man saying yes to a woman on a riverbank."

She laughed, and then she was quiet.

They sat there for a long time. The light changed. The man across the river caught nothing and did not move. Somewhere up in the village a dog barked, and a woman called a child in for supper, and there was the sound of a cart.

"Halina," Janusz said at last. "There is a thing I have never said to you, in nineteen years. I mean to say it now, and then we will go up to the village and eat eggs and bread, and it will not be mentioned again."

"Say it."

"I do not regret the priesthood."

She turned her head and looked at him. His face was turned toward the river, and he was not smiling, and there was no self-pity in it at all.

"I have been a priest for ten years," he said, "and I have buried thirty-one people and baptized sixty and heard confessions that would make a man's hair stand up, and I have been in that confessional with a woman who lost all four of her children in 1944, and I have been with a boy who tried to hang himself with a belt in the sacristy, and I have held the hand of a man dying of the cancer who thought that God had turned his face away, and I have said Mass in a room with a dead man in it and in a field on a plank and once, once, on the wing of a shot-down German aeroplane. If you had asked me in 1939 what I wanted, I would have said you. I would still say you. But I would not give up one of those hours for a house and a garden and four children, and I have finally, at thirty-six years of age, understood that this is not a betrayal of you. It is not even a sacrifice. It is a thing that happened to me, in a cell in Lublin, and I did not choose it, and I have spent fifteen years knowing that I did not choose it, and the not choosing was the mercy.

"You asked me once whether I was the boy by the river. I am not. I will be a priest in a burned town for the rest of my life, and there will be a woman who comes on the first Sunday in June, and I do not know what that is, and I am not going to call it anything, because I do not have words for it and the words that exist are all wrong."

Halina looked at the river. Her eyes were dry.

"You are a great fool," she said.

"I know."

"You are a great fool, and you have not said one untrue thing to me since 1943, and I would rather have that than a house and a garden and four children."

"Even the four children?"

"I would like the four children," she said. "I would like them very much. But one cannot have everything, and I have had a great deal more than Barbara Kowalik's daughter had any right to expect."

They got up. He held out his hand and she took it, and they climbed the bank, and as they came up onto the meadow the bell began to ring.

It was the old bell of St. Michael's, the one that had been taken down in 1915 and taken down again in 1940 and hidden in a barn and put back in 1946. It had a crack in it, a small one, in the waist, that had been there since before any living person's memory, and the crack gave the note a roughness, a slight unclean hum underneath the tone. It was the bell of her childhood. It was the bell that had rung for her First Communion and for Tadek's funeral and for the funeral of her mother, and it would ring for her own, if she was buried here, which she had decided that she would be.

It rang the Angelus: three strokes, three, three, and then the long closing peal.

They stopped in the meadow and stood still.

Halina did not bow her head. She had not bowed her head for sixteen years, and she was not going to start now, and she had made a great deal of peace with the fact that she was a person who did not believe, who would not be reconciled, who would go to the church for the singing and the silence and the company of the man she loved and would not go forward for Communion and would not lie about why.

But she stood still. She looked out over the village, over the roofs and the poplars and the road, over the cemetery on the hill where the graves were new and old and the grass was high, over the forest where three boys had been shot in 1943 and a girl had been shot in 1942 and where fifty people had been shot or marched or buried in the years when the world was on fire, and over the fields, which were the same fields, and did not remember anything.

And out beyond that the river went on, the same slow brown water, going north to the Vistula and the Vistula going north to the sea.

"Halina," Janusz said.

"Don't," she said. "Not while the bell is ringing."

So they stood in the meadow, in the yellow light, and let it ring out over them: the old cracked bronze, ringing the thing it had rung every evening for two hundred years and would be ringing long after both of them were under the grass on the hill.

When it stopped, the silence was enormous. You could hear the river.

"Now," she said.

"Now nothing," he said. "We are going up to the village and eating eggs and bread, and I will sleep in the kitchen, and in the morning we will see about the roof."

They went up the hill to the village.

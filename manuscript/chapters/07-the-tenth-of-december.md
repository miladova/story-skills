# Chapter Seven: The Tenth of December

The room on the third floor of the ministry had no window, only a grille high in the wall that gave onto a shaft, and the shaft gave onto the sky, and the sky that night was the color of a wet slate. There was a table with a green blotter, two chairs, a brass ashtray, a carafe of water, and a portrait of Felix Dzierżyński in a black frame. The radiator under the window made a noise like a man clearing his throat. It was twenty past two in the morning.

Father Piotr Nowicki had been sitting in the chair for three hours. He had not been beaten. That was the first surprise, and it had taken him an hour to understand that it was also the technique. Nobody had shouted at him. Nobody had taken his watch or made him stand facing the wall. A man had brought him tea in a glass with a metal holder, and the tea had been hot and sweet, and he had drunk it, and then he had been ashamed of drinking it.

The interrogator's name was Major Zawistowski. He was a small, tidy man of about fifty with thinning hair combed carefully across his scalp and a faint smell of eau de cologne. He wore a gray suit that had been pressed and a wedding ring worn thin at the back. He had a folder in front of him, tied with tape, and he had not opened it.

"Tell me about the table tennis," he said.

Piotr blinked. "The table tennis?"

"The club. You have a table. Where did the table come from?"

"We bought it. The parish bought it. Second-hand, from a club in Wola that was closing. It cost four thousand two hundred złoty."

"Four thousand two hundred." The major wrote it down. "And the bats?"

"We bought the bats new."

"Where?"

"At the shop on Targowa. They were ninety złoty each. We bought six."

"Six bats." The major wrote that down too. "For twenty-two young people. That is a curious ratio."

"Some of them play with their hands."

"With their hands." The major looked up. His eyes were mild and interested and entirely empty. "You see, Father, this is what I want you to understand. I am not a monster. I have a son of fifteen and he plays table tennis at his school, with his hands, when the bats are broken, which they usually are. I am asking you about the table tennis because the table tennis is not the point, and I want you to see that I know it is not the point. The point is that in that club there is a library, and in that library there are books, and among the books there are books that were printed in Paris and in London and in Rome, and there are young people who read them and then go home and tell their fathers that the Party has no answer to Teilhard de Chardin. That is the point. And also — this is the second point — among those young people there is a boy called Zbyszek Malinowski, who works at the Nowotki works and who has been heard, by a person whose name I will not give you, saying that the strike committee at Poznań in 1956 was right, and that there will be another one, and that this time the priests will ring the bells." He put down his pen. "I want to know what the priest says to that."

"He says don't be a fool."

"And what else?"

"He says that a Christian does not strike against his neighbor. He strikes against injustice. And that violence is what the powerful want, because violence gives them the excuse."

Major Zawistowski wrote it down. He took his time about it. When he had finished, he looked up and said, without any change of tone at all:

"Your mother lives at 14 Lubelska Street, in the apartment on the ground floor with the bad door. She is sixty-one. She has a widow's pension of four hundred and forty złoty a month, and a place in the queue at the cooperative, and a sister in Siedlce. She has a photograph of you on the sideboard in your ordination alb. She tells the neighbors that her son is a priest, which is not against the law, and that he is a good son, which is also not against the law. She has a bad heart. It says so in her medical file."

Piotr's mouth was dry. He had finished the tea twenty minutes earlier and had been afraid to ask for more.

"Now," the major said. "You may help me, and nothing will be wrong with your mother's pension, and nothing will be wrong with her place in the queue, and nothing will be wrong with her heart. Or you may refuse to help me, and I will write in this folder that the investigation is continuing, and I will put the folder in a drawer, and every month or so someone will knock on your mother's door at six in the morning and ask her a few questions, politely, and go away, and the neighbors will watch. That is all. Nobody will be arrested. Nobody will be hurt. It is simply that from now on her life will be a little worse in every particular, and it will be your doing, and she will know it is your doing, because I will tell her." He smiled. "I am telling you the truth, Father. That is the courtesy I offer. I am not going to pretend that I am your friend."

Piotr looked at the carafe. The water in it moved very slightly, because his hands were on the table and the table was not steady, or he was not.

"I don't know anything," he said.

"You know everything. You live in the same house as the man. You hear his telephone calls. You know who comes to the rectory at night." The major opened the folder at last and turned a page, and the paper made a dry sound. "Let us begin with the twenty-eighth of November. A Sunday. There is a sermon. Somebody in the congregation takes notes. I have the notes. I want to know what he said after the sermon, in the sacristy, to the two men who came to see him."

"They came to see him about a funeral."

"Whose funeral?"

"An old woman's. A parishioner."

"What was her name?"

"I don't remember."

"You don't remember the name of a parishioner whose funeral was arranged three weeks ago."

"She was very old. She had no family."

"And the men who came about the funeral. Were they from the security service?"

Piotr looked at the table.

"Father Nowicki," the major said, gently, "I already know they were from the security service. Two of my colleagues. One of them wrote a report. I am not asking you to tell me who they were. I am asking you whether the rector knew who they were when he spoke with them, and what he said, and whether he was afraid. That last question is the interesting one. Was he afraid?"

"No," Piotr said. And then, because it was the truth and because the truth was the only thing he had that was worth anything in that room: "He was not afraid. He was angry. He is always a little angry after he has been frightened, and he was frightened before, and then he was angry."

Major Zawistowski wrote that down. He wrote for a long time. Then he closed the folder and tied the tape and put his pen in his pocket.

"Thank you," he said. "That is enough for tonight. You will sleep here. In the morning a man will come with a statement for you to sign, and it will say that you have told me what you told me voluntarily, and that no pressure was applied to you, and it will be true, as these things go. Then you will go home." He stood up and put on his jacket, which had been hanging on the back of the chair. "One more thing. Do not tell the rector about this conversation. Not because it would harm you. Because it would harm him. He is shortly to be offered a great deal, and a young priest's confession in the middle of it would be a stone in his shoe, and he is going to have enough stones." He paused at the door. "I have been doing this for twenty-five years, Father. May I give you some advice? Not as an officer. As a man."

"Yes."

"Whatever you sign tomorrow, sign it in pencil."

He went out. The lock turned. Piotr sat in the chair with his hands flat on the table and looked at the portrait of Dzierżyński, and after a while he began to shake, and he could not stop.

They let him go at nine in the morning.

The corridor was full of people who did not look at him. He walked down the stairs because he could not bring himself to get into the elevator with the guard, and at the bottom there was a vestibule with a rubber mat and a glass door and beyond the glass the street, white with snow, and a woman standing on the step with her hands in her pockets.

"Father Nowicki," Halina said.

He stopped. His face did a thing he could not control. "Comrade Sowa."

"Come. The car is around the corner." She took his arm. Her grip was firm and impersonal, a nurse's grip. "Walk normally. Do not look back. There is a man behind the glass who is enjoying this."

They walked to the corner, past the newspaper kiosk and the queue at the butcher's, and the car was there, black and steaming, and the driver got out and opened the door and did not look at either of them. Piotr got in. The leather was cold. Halina got in beside him, and the car pulled away, and Piotr discovered that he was crying, which was mortifying, and that he could not stop, which was worse.

"I signed," he said. "I signed the statement. I told them about the table tennis and I told them that he was not afraid and that he was angry. I told them. I gave him the names of the boys in the club. He wrote the names down. I gave him the names, and he wrote them down, and then he said 'thank you,' and then I signed the paper, and it was all true, every word of it, that is the thing, it was all true—"

"I know," she said.

"I would have given him more. If he had asked for more, I would have given him more. He did not even have to threaten my mother twice. Once was enough. One mention of her heart and I would have given him the cardinal."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have been in that room," Halina said. "They do not beat you any more, not in the first week. They give you tea." She was looking out of the window at the passing shops. "You are not a coward, Father Piotr. You are a man of twenty-six who has discovered what he is made of, and it is not steel, and almost nobody is made of steel, and the ones who are, are usually worse men than you. Now listen to me. You will go home and you will sleep, and then you will tell your rector what you did. Not because you owe it to him. Because if you do not tell him, they will own you for the rest of your life. They will come back in a year, and in five years, and each time they will want a little more, and each time the thing you are protecting will be a little smaller. I have watched it happen to better men than both of us. Tell him."

"I can't."

"Then you will be their man until they are finished with you, and then you will be nobody's." She turned and looked at him. Her face was pale and tired and quite without pity, and something in it was kind. "I got you out of that building this morning by telling the major's superior that the arrest had not been sanctioned by the procurator, which happens to be true, and that if the priest was held another day I would raise it at the department meeting on Monday and ask who was authorizing arrests of clergy without paperwork in the week that the Office for Religious Affairs is deciding the episcopal nomination. That is what I did. It was not mercy. It was leverage. Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then use it. Tell him."

The car stopped at the corner of Brzeska and Wileńska, because she did not want the whole street to see her bring the curate home in an official car. Piotr got out. He stood in the snow and looked back at her through the open door.

"Comrade Sowa—"

"Halina," she said. "My name is Halina."

"Halina." He swallowed. "Why did you do it?"

She was quiet for a moment. The engine ran. The driver looked straight ahead.

"Because I have been in that room," she said, "and nobody came for me."

She pulled the door shut, and the car went away up Brzeska Street, and Piotr stood in the road with the snow falling on his shoulders and his cassock wet to the hem.

Rydel saw Janusz on Friday the twelfth of December, at ten in the morning, in the office on the third floor that had a window, because Rydel rated a window.

He was standing at it when Janusz was shown in, looking down at the courtyard where the cars were parked in a row, and he did not turn around at once. The office was warm and smelled of tobacco and floor polish. On the desk were three folders, a glass of tea going cold, a photograph of a girl of about twelve with her hair in plaits, and a small bronze of a horse. On the wall, a map of Warsaw and a calendar with a photograph of the Palace of Culture and the words *Budujemy socjalizm*.

"Father Wilga. Sit down." He turned. He was a heavy man with soft hands and heavy lids, and he was smoking, and there was something almost apologetic in the way he stubbed out the cigarette before he sat down. "You are punctual. I like punctuality. It is the most underrated virtue."

"I was told ten o'clock."

"By comrade Sowa, no doubt." Rydel smiled. "I am sorry. I should say Halina. She has asked me to call her Halina now, as though we were at a dinner party." He opened the topmost folder. "You know why you are here."

"I know what you want."

"That is not the same thing. Let me put it in plain language, because I find that plain language saves everyone a great deal of time. On the nineteenth of December, the Office for Religious Affairs will meet to consider the nomination of a suffragan bishop for this archdiocese. The Primate has put forward a name. The name is yours. The office will either consent or refuse, and its decision will be transmitted to the curia, and then the newspapers will have something to say about it, and by the New Year you will either be a bishop or you will be a parish priest with a great deal of explaining to do." He tapped the folder. "I can tell you which of those two things will happen. I am, in fact, the man who decides which of those two things will happen, although of course I will do it in consultation with my colleagues, and my colleagues will do what I recommend."

"And the recommendation depends on the Commission of Priests."

"The recommendation depends on a piece of paper. It is a short piece of paper. It says that you support the work of the Commission of Priests for the reconciliation of Church and State, that you recognize the leading role of the Party in the construction of socialism in Poland, and that you will endeavour, in your pastoral work, to promote peace and the unity of our people. That is all. Three sentences. You may write them yourself if you prefer. I have known priests to improve on my version."

"And if I sign it?"

"Then you are a bishop. The file is closed. The newspaper story is not written. And you will be, I think, an unusually good bishop. You will preach, you will build, you will teach, and in twenty years you will be a cardinal, and in thirty you will be dead and they will name a street after you, and nobody will remember a piece of paper in 1958." He said it without irony, as a man reading a timetable. "This is not a trap, Father. It is a door. It is the only door in the wall, and it is open, and it will be closed on the nineteenth."

"And if I do not sign?"

"Then on the twentieth the office refuses you. And in the new year, one or two newspapers will publish material from a German police file of 1943 about a young man in Lublin who was arrested and who was released after three days, and who was released because he gave the Germans the address of a Home Army hideout in which three men were killed, one of whom was a boy of seventeen named Tadeusz Kowal." Rydel did not raise his voice. "It will be printed as the story of a traitor whom the Church attempted to make a bishop. It will be printed in *Trybuna Ludu*, and in *Żołnierz Wolności*, and it will be read aloud at party meetings in every factory in this city, and the men in the Nowotki works who listened to you in November will be told that the priest who talked about the truth was a man who talked to the Gestapo. And I will not have to do anything else. I will not have to arrest you. You will do the rest yourself, and it will take about six weeks."

The radiator clicked. Janusz looked at the photograph of the girl on the desk. She had a ribbon in her hair that did not match her dress, and she was squinting into the sun, and she was laughing.

"Why the commission?" he said. "You have bishops. You have Bochenek. He builds churches with your money and his Party secretary's wife comes to his Christmas Mass. Why do you need me?"

"Because Bochenek is a practical man and the young people do not listen to him." Rydel stubbed out his cigarette and immediately lit another, which was a habit Janusz had seen in men who were afraid of something they could not name. "Do you know what my daughter asked me last month? She is twelve. She asked me why the priest on the radio in the old days used to say that the Pope was a prisoner, and whether it was true, and if it was true, why nobody let him out. I told her it was a figure of speech. She looked at me as though I had told her the moon was made of cheese. She goes to the church on Miodowa with her aunt, did you know that? My sister-in-law takes her. I could stop it. I have not stopped it." He tapped ash into the brass tray. "That is what I am dealing with, Father. Not the cardinal. The cardinal I can negotiate with. That is what I am dealing with." He pointed the cigarette at Janusz. "You are the man who could talk to my daughter. And that is precisely why you must be either with us or destroyed, because a man who can talk to children about the truth is more dangerous than a man who can talk to bishops about politics."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe it every morning when I shave."

Janusz was silent. He thought about the cell in Lublin and the smell of the corridor and the way the light had come in under the door. He thought about the boy he had been at twenty, who had believed that a man could be measured by the purity of his intention, and who had discovered in three days that he could not. He thought about Halina in the parish hall saying *I forgive you* as though she were laying down a stone she had carried up a hill for fifteen years. He thought about the cardinal's warm study and the apples and the notebook, and about his old confessor, Father Ambroży, in his room by the garden at the Jesuit house on Rakowiecka Street, who was seventy and dying and knew it and had told him once that a bishop who is not afraid is a fool.

"I will not sign it," he said.

Rydel did not look surprised. He looked tired, in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"You understand what I have just told you."

"I understand it."

"You understand that the file is not a threat I am making. It is a file. It exists. It has existed since 1943, and since 1945 it has had a companion, and between the two of them they will end you and a woman I have known for thirteen years whom I am fond of, in my way." He said it almost gently. "You are not refusing me, Father. You are refusing her. Have you thought about that?"

"I have thought about nothing else for three weeks."

"And?"

"She has told me not to decide it for her."

Rydel laughed. It was a short, ugly laugh, and he cut it off and looked annoyed with himself.

"That," he said, "is the most Halina thing I have ever heard." He opened a drawer and took out a sheet of paper that was already typed. "Here is the statement. Take it with you. If you sign it, bring it to me by Wednesday the seventeenth at six in the evening. If you do not sign it, do not bring it, and do not come to this office again, because the next time we meet will be across a table, and I will not be able to offer you tea." He pushed it across the desk. "Father. One thing, man to man, off the record. Do you actually believe all of it? The resurrection, the virgin, the angels? Or is it just that you have no other trade?"

Janusz picked up the sheet of paper and folded it once and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

"I believe in God," he said. "Some days more than others. And I have seen what happens to men who believe in nothing."

"The Party believes in something."

"The Party believes in the future. It is a very great thing to believe in, and it justifies almost anything, which is the trouble with it. A man who believes in the future will put his friends in prison for the sake of the men who are not born yet." Janusz stood. "I have been in a room where a man explained to me that the future justified the present. He was a German. He was very reasonable. He had a folder." He put on his hat. "Good morning, comrade Rydel."

"It is 'citizen,' now," Rydel said. "Since October they have decided that 'comrade' sounds intimate. Good morning, Father."

Halina was waiting for him at the bottom of the stairs, in the vestibule, by the rubber mat.

"Well?"

"I am to bring it in by Wednesday or not at all."

She closed her eyes for a moment. Then she opened them and nodded, once, like a woman who has been told the result of a test she already knew she had failed.

"Come on," she said. "There is a café on Krucza that has not been closed yet, and I have an hour before Kaczmarczyk wants his notes, and I am going to have a glass of something and you are going to sit there and drink tea and be unbearable."

They went to the café. It was a small place with steamed-up windows and four tables and a waiter with a mustache who knew her, and they sat in the corner and she drank a small glass of vodka and he drank tea and they did not talk about the nineteenth of December. She told him about the department meeting and the argument over the tractor article. He told her about the boiler and Pani Genowefa's war with the coal depot. She laughed twice. He memorized it, the way a man memorizes a road he is not going to travel again.

Then she said, "You will not sign."

"No."

"Then it will be in the papers."

"Probably."

"And I will be in the papers."

"You will not. It is one thing to print a Gestapo report about a priest. It is another to print that a Party official signed a false denunciation in 1945. That is a story about them." He turned his glass around on the table. "Rydel knows it. That is why the file has been sitting in his drawer for thirteen years and has not been used to advance him one grade. The moment he uses it, he is the man who used it. He will give the German report to the newspapers and keep the second page for the day he needs it, and the day he needs it will be the day he is in trouble, and by then it will be worth more than it is worth now. That is what I have been thinking about for three weeks. I am not a politician. I am a priest who has been blackmailed by professionals and has learned the trade."

Halina set down her glass. "You have become frighteningly good at this."

"I have had a good teacher."

"Don't."

"I meant the cardinal."

She laughed again, and it surprised her, and she looked at him over the rim of the glass with an expression he had not seen on her face since 1939.

They parted on the street outside. It was snowing again, thick and slow.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday."

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"When it is over — whatever it is — come and find me. Not as a priest. Not as anything. Just knock on the door."

"I will knock on the door."

She nodded and walked away toward Marszałkowska, and the snow took her in thirty paces.

He went back to the rectory and found the curate sitting on the bottom step of the stairs with his head in his hands.

Piotr told him everything. It took forty minutes, and he cried twice, and at one point he got up and went to the window and stood there with his back to the room for a long moment before he could go on. He gave Janusz the names he had given to the major. He described the tea and the photograph and the maternal heart and the pen lying parallel to the edge of the desk. When he had finished he sat with his hands between his knees and said, "Now you will send me away."

Janusz got up and went into the kitchen and put the kettle on the ring. He took down two glasses and the tin where Genowefa kept the tea. He heard in his memory the voice of the old priest in the room by the garden: *You are commanded to love.*

"You will write to the provincial," he said, "and ask to be transferred to a parish in the provinces. Not because I am sending you. Because you cannot stay here now, and you know it, and it would be cruel to make you say it twice. I will write to the provincial too, and I will tell him that you are a good priest who was frightened by professionals at the age of twenty-six, and that I have known better men to break in worse rooms for less."

Piotr's face crumpled. "You are not going to forgive me?"

"I am going to forgive you every day for the rest of my life, because that is what it costs." Janusz put the glass in front of him. "It is not a feeling, Piotr. It is a practice. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Drink your tea."

They sat in the kitchen while the light went out of the afternoon. At half past four the Angelus rang and they both stood up out of habit and said it, and Piotr's voice broke on the second line and he got through it anyway, and Janusz thought: *That is what a Church is. Not the building. The two of us in a kitchen, saying it badly, and getting to the end.*

On Sunday he preached on John the Baptist.

The church was full. It was the third Sunday of Advent, and the rose vestments came out of the cupboard where they smelled of mothballs, and the choir sang *Rorate caeli* with more enthusiasm than accuracy. In the fourth row the two men from the security service sat with their hats on their knees. Pani Genowefa had put them in the fourth row deliberately, as a hostess seating difficult guests where she could see them.

"John the Baptist is in prison," Janusz said. "He has been put there by a king who was afraid of him, and he sends two of his disciples to ask Jesus a question. And the question is not 'When will you free me?' The question is: 'Are you he who is to come, or shall we look for another?' That is the question of a man in a cell. It is not a question about politics. It is a question about whether the thing he has given his life to is real."

He looked down at the front pew, where the widows sat in their black coats with their hands folded, and at the back, where the workers stood because they had come in late and would not push forward, and at the fourth row, where the two men were writing.

"And the answer that comes back," he said, "is not a liberation. It is a list. The blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. It is a list of things that have happened quietly, in villages, to people whose names nobody wrote down. It is not the answer a man in a cell would choose. It is the answer he is given."

He paused. The church was very quiet. The candles moved.

"On Wednesday," he said, "a decision will be made in an office in this city about this parish and about your priest. I am not going to tell you what it is, because I do not know, and because it is not the gospel. But I am going to tell you what I have learned this Advent, and it is this. A man may be destroyed. A parish may be dispersed. A bishop may be refused and a file may be opened and a name may be printed in a newspaper in letters a centimetre high. None of that is the answer to the question. The answer to the question is that in this parish, this year, the blind have been visited and the lame have been carried and the poor have been fed and the children have been taught, quietly, by people whose names nobody is writing down. If they take me tomorrow, that remains. It remains because it is not mine. It is His."

He stepped down. The organ began. The two men in the fourth row closed their notebooks and put on their hats and went out before the last verse, and the draft from the door made the candles lean.

On Wednesday the seventeenth of December, at six in the evening, Janusz Wilga did not go to Rakowiecka Street. He went to the church instead and heard confessions until eight, and then he went into the sacristy and took the folded sheet of paper out of his coat and burned it in the little iron stove, and watched it curl and blacken, and thought that it was the first thing he had burned in that stove that had given him any pleasure.

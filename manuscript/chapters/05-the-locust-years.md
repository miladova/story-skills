# Chapter Five: The Years of the Locust

Halina could not sleep.

She lay in the dark and listened to the city. The trams had stopped. The Palace of Culture was a black shape against the sky and the snow came against the window with a soft hiss, like sand, and she thought about the file, and the boy by the river, and the man in the church, and the hideout in the forest, and Tadek, and it went round in that order until it was not thought at all but a wheel turning.

She got up and put on her robe and went to the table and turned on the lamp, and opened the briefcase, and took out the file, because it was three in the morning and there was nothing else in the flat she wanted to look at.

The Gestapo report was on top. She read it again, and the words were the same words they had been every night for three weeks: the hideout, the raid, the three bandits, Tadeusz Kowal, the signature of the German officer at the bottom, the eagle and the swastika stamped into the paper so hard that the ink stood up under her thumb.

She put the report down and looked at the wall, which was white, and at the yellow circle the lamp made on it, and then she closed her eyes, and the wall was a wall on Brzeska Street and the lamp was the lamp in a kitchen in Brzóza in 1941, and she could hear her mother putting the plates down.

She was seventeen again.

---

Brzóza, June 1939.

The village was a cluster of houses around a church, in a bend of the river, in the Lublin countryside. The church of St. Michael was brick, with a tall tower and a green copper roof. The manor house was white, with a red tile roof and a park full of old oaks. The Jewish mill was by the river, with a water wheel that turned slowly in the current. The fields were flat, stretching to the horizon, with rye and buckwheat and potatoes. The road was dirt, with ruts from the carts. The air smelled of manure, cut hay, and river water. It was not a beautiful village, but it was home.

Halina Kowal was the daughter of Stanisław Kowal, a landless farmhand who worked for the manor. Her mother, Aniela, kept a garden and a few chickens. They lived in a small house at the edge of the village, near the forest. Halina had a younger brother, Tadeusz, who was thirteen. He was a skinny boy with a cowlick and a laugh that could be heard across the fields. He was always getting into trouble. He was always singing. He sang in the church choir, badly, with a voice that cracked. The choirmaster, Pan Berezowski, despaired of him. Halina loved him.

Janusz Wilga was the son of Franciszek Wilga, the blacksmith. His father was a big man with a black beard and hands like hammers. His mother, Jadwiga, was a small woman with a kind face. They lived in a house next to the forge, near the church. Janusz was an only child. He was quiet, serious, and strong. He could lift a sack of grain that would make a grown man stagger. He could play the harmonica. He could imitate the priest's cough. He was the best altar boy in the parish. He was in love with Halina.

They had known each other all their lives. They had played together as children, in the river, in the forest, in the churchyard. They had gone to school together. They had sung in the choir together. When Halina was fourteen, she realized that Janusz was not a boy anymore. When she was fifteen, she realized that she loved him. When she was sixteen, he kissed her under the birch tree by the river. It was a warm evening in June. The frogs were singing. The water was low. The birch tree was white against the green. He took her hand. He said, "Halka." She said, "What?" He said, "I love you." She said, "I know." He kissed her. She kissed him back. It was the first kiss for both of them. It was clumsy and sweet. It tasted of wild strawberries.

They met at the river every evening that summer and sat under the birch tree and talked. They talked about the harvest and the church and the priest and the manor, and the Jews, of whom there were forty-three in the village and about whom neither of them knew, that summer, what was coming; and about the government and the war and the future, which they discussed with the confidence of people who had never had one taken off them. Halina wanted to be a teacher. She wanted to go to Lublin to study and come back to the village and teach the children their letters and tell them, in a way that would not get her into trouble, that the world was bigger than Brzóza. Janusz wanted to be a priest. He had wanted it since he was nine years old, and he loved the church and the Latin and the incense and, above and behind all of it, the idea of God; and what he wanted, when he was honest with himself, was to serve, which is a thing that no one had ever wanted for him at home.

Halina laughed at him. "A priest," she said. "You? You can't be a priest. You're in love with me."

"I can be a priest and love you."

"No, you can't. Priests don't marry."

"I know."

"Then what about me?"

"I will love you in my heart."

"That's not enough."

"It will have to be."

"That's not enough, Janusz."

His eyes were very dark in the lamplight. "I don't want to lose you," he said.

"Then don't become a priest."

"I have to."

"Why?"

"Because God is calling me."

"God is calling you? What about me? What about us?"

"God is calling me to be a priest. He is also calling me to love you. I don't know how both can be true. But I believe they are."

"That's a beautiful answer. It's also a lie."

"It's not a lie."

"It's a lie. You're choosing God over me."

"I'm not choosing. I'm trying to be faithful."

"To God?"

"To both."

"You can't be faithful to both. You have to choose."

"I won't choose."

"Then I will."

She stood up and walked away down the bank, and he did not follow her, which was the right thing and also the wrong thing; and she came back the next evening and he was waiting under the birch tree with his hands in his pockets, and he did not say anything, and took her hand, and they sat down. They did not talk about the future. They talked about the river and the frogs and whether there was a war on the moon, and they kissed, and they were seventeen, and they thought they had time.

The war came in September.

---

Brzóza, September 1939.

The German planes came on the first day, low, with the black crosses showing on the underside of the wings. They bombed the railway station at Puławy, twenty kilometres away, and the explosions came rolling back over the fields like thunder with a direction to it. The windows rattled in their frames. The chickens ran in circles. The villagers stood in the road and looked at the sky, and did not go to the shelters, because there were no shelters in Brzóza and no one had thought to dig any.

The army came three days later: young men, dusty, tired, riding motorcycles and trucks with their boots hanging over the tailgates. They stopped at the manor and took the horses and the grain and the radios, and then they nailed a notice to the church door. All Jews were to wear a white armband with a blue star. All Poles were to surrender their weapons. All men between sixteen and sixty were to report for labour. Father Grudziński read it aloud to the congregation on Sunday and told them to be quiet and to survive and not to provoke anybody; and then he went back into the sacristy and sat down and was sick.

Janusz's father hid the forge hammer under the floorboards, though what good a hammer was going to be against a Panzer he could not have said. Janusz hid in the thatch of the barn a rifle he had found in the forest with a broken stock, and oiled it once a month for three years, and never fired it once.

Halina's father was taken for forced labour in October. The Germans came to the house and asked for his papers and looked at his hands and said he was strong, and took him to Germany, and he was gone two years. When he came back he was thin and grey and would not talk about it, and slept in the barn for a month because he had got used to sleeping alone. Halina's mother cried for a week. Then she stopped, and went out to the fields, and did the work of two people, because the farm was now hers and there was no one else to do it.

The village changed, in the way that a village changes when one set of masters is exchanged for a worse set. The manor became the German headquarters and the park was cut down for firewood, tree by tree, until there was no shade in it. The mill was closed. The Jews were made to wear armbands and to step off the pavement into the gutter when a German passed, and then they were made to do other things, and then they were not seen for a while. The Polish police, the blue police, helped. And the headman, Góra — a fat man with a small moustache and a gold tooth he showed whenever he smiled, which was often — discovered that he had become somebody. He had been nobody before the war. Now he had a telephone call to make in the evening and lists to draw up: who had a radio, who had a rifle, who had been seen carrying a sack to a farmhouse in the forest. He was a Judas, and everybody in the village knew it, and nobody said it, because he was also the man who decided who got the good coal.

Janusz joined the underground in the winter of 1940, having turned eighteen in November. He was sworn in, in the forest at night, by a man named Berezowski, who was thirty-one and had a wife in Otwock and a way of speaking to the boys that made them feel they had been chosen for something. The oath was short and he remembered all of it for the rest of his life. He was given the name Sosna — Pine — because he was tall and straight and because Berezowski had a weakness for the obvious, and for three years men he had never met would come up to him in barns and farmyards and say *Sosna?* and he would say yes.

Halina joined the other underground in the spring of 1941, at nineteen, by way of a man named Marek at a market in Puławy. He had a thin face and burning eyes and a satchel of pamphlets, and he talked to her for an hour behind the cattle pens about the Soviet Union and the class struggle and the coming of the workers' state. He told her that the AK was the tool of the London government and the landlords; she had been brought up to think that was the natural order of things, and had begun, that winter, to think it was not. She had seen the manor. She had seen the poverty. She had seen the priest bless the rich and tell the poor to be patient. She believed him. She joined the People's Guard and was given the name Sowa — Owl — because she was quiet and watchful, and because she sat at meetings saying nothing and then asked, at the very end, the one question that nobody had an answer for.

Tadeusz joined the People's Guard in the spring of 1942, at sixteen, which was too young, and he lied about his age to a man in a cap in a barn outside Puławy who could see perfectly well how old he was and did not care. He wanted to fight. He hated the Germans with a pure, uncomplicated hatred that his sister, who was nineteen and had begun to read pamphlets, could already see was going to get him killed. Halina tried to stop him. He did not listen, because he was a boy, and because boys of sixteen in the third year of a war believe in the specific and stupid way of boys of sixteen that they are immortal.

They met in secret, in the forest and the church and the mill, and they argued. They argued about the AK and the AL and the Soviet Union and the London government and the future of Poland, in the way that two people argue when they are in love and have discovered that love does not settle anything. They argued about Katyn.

It was April 1943, and the Germans had announced the discovery of a mass grave in the Katyn forest: Polish officers, thousands of them, shot in the back of the head. The Polish government in London asked the Red Cross to investigate; the Soviets broke off relations with it; and the news went through the village in a week, in the way news went through Brzóza, from the smithy to the shop to the pump. The AK believed the Germans, which cost them something to say out loud. The People's Guard believed the Soviets, which cost them nothing at all.

"They murdered our officers," Janusz said. They were sitting in the loft of the mill, in the dark. The flour dust was in the air. The wheel was still.

"You believe the Germans?" Halina said. "The Germans are liars. They killed millions of people. They would say anything to divide us."

"The Red Cross should investigate."

"The Red Cross is a tool of the bourgeoisie."

"The Red Cross is neutral."

"There is no neutral. There are only class forces."

"That is a lie."

"That is Marxism."

"That is a lie."

"The Soviets are our allies. They are fighting the Germans. They are dying for us."

"They are not dying for us. They are dying for themselves. They murdered our officers. They will enslave us."

"You are a reactionary."

"I am a Pole."

"So am I."

"No. You are a communist."

They looked at each other in the dark of the mill with the flour dust in their hair, twenty and twenty-one years old, and in love, and on opposite sides of a war that had another two years to run and would not be finished, as it turned out, in either of their lives.

"I am a Pole," Halina said. "I am a Pole who wants a free Poland. A Poland where the workers rule, where there is no manor, no landlord, no priest telling us what to do. A Poland where Tadek can go to school, where my mother does not have to work in the fields, where no one is taken to Germany. That is my Poland. The AK does not want that Poland. The AK wants the old Poland. The Poland of the manor, the church, the rich. I do not want that Poland."

"The AK wants a free Poland. A Poland where we can vote, where we can speak, where we can worship."

"Vote? Speak? Worship? The AK is led by the London government. The London government is led by the rich. The rich do not care about us."

"The communists are led by Moscow. Moscow does not care about us either."

"Moscow cares about the workers."

"Moscow cares about Moscow."

"You are impossible."

"So are you."

She stood up and looked at him with the flour dust on her face and her eyes bright with a thing that was not quite anger.

"Janusz," she said. "I love you. But I cannot follow you. I cannot follow the AK. I cannot follow the church. I cannot follow the past. I have to follow the future."

"Then follow it."

"I will."

"Without me?"

"Without you."

He looked at her. His face was pale in the dark. "I will pray for you," he said.

"Pray for what?"

"For your soul."

"I don't believe in the soul."

"I know. I will believe for both of us."

She laughed. It was a sad laugh. "You always were a better man than I was," she said.

"That is not true."

"It is true."

She kissed him. It was a hard, desperate kiss. Then she left.

He did not follow her. He sat in the loft, in the dark, with the flour dust, and he prayed.

---

Brzóza, 1942.

The Jews of Brzóza were taken away on a Tuesday in October.

There were forty-three of them. The Kreiners, who owned the mill, and the Gutmans, who owned the shop, and the Rosenbergs, who made shoes, and the Silbermans, who had the tailor shop, and the Feinmans, who had the bakery and had given Halina a roll every Friday for six years because she was a child who came in with her mother. The children. The old people. The young men. The young women. They were assembled in the square in front of the church with one suitcase each, and told that they were being resettled, and told to be quiet.

Halina stood at the edge of the square. She was twenty. She was holding her mother's hand. Her mother was crying. Rutka Kreiner was in the square. She was twenty. She was Halina's best friend. They had grown up together. They had played in the river together. They had shared their lunches at school. Rutka was wearing a dark coat and a white armband with a blue star. She was carrying a small suitcase. She looked at Halina. She did not wave. She did not cry. She just looked.

The Germans loaded them onto trucks and the trucks went up the road towards the forest, and Halina watched them go and did not move and did not speak and did not cry. She was afraid, and the fear was very simple and very clear: if she moved they would shoot her; if she spoke they would shoot her mother; if she cried they would see her. So she stood still in the square with her mother's hand in hers and watched the trucks until the road was empty, and then she went home, and it was the last time in her life that she was ever that afraid of anything.

It was a secret, and it lasted two weeks. Rutka had got off the truck in the forest — she never said how, and Halina never asked — and had come through the woods at night, and to the house, because there was nowhere else. Halina's mother fed her. Halina gave her clothes out of her own chest. Tadeusz, who was sixteen, stood guard in the yard with a stick. They hid her in the attic under the straw, and brought her up water in a bottle, and at night she came down and sat by the stove and did not talk much. She had seen her parents taken away, and the children, and did not know whether any of them were alive, and had decided, in the way people decide such things, that they were not.

Góra found out.

He came to the house on a Sunday. He was wearing his best coat. He had his gold tooth. He said he knew about the Jewish girl. He said he would tell the Germans unless Halina's mother gave him money. Halina's mother gave him everything she had. It was not enough. He took the money and told the Germans anyway.

The Germans came at night, three of them and a Polish policeman from Puławy whose face Halina knew from the market, and they took Rutka down from the attic by the ankle. They took Halina's mother to the station and beat her for six hours and asked her who else had helped, and she did not tell them; and in the morning they let her go, and she walked home with her face swollen shut and her dress torn at the shoulder. She did not cry. She sat down in the kitchen and looked at the wall, and it was three days before she spoke again.

Rutka was taken to the forest and shot with the others. Halina never saw her again. There was no grave and no stone; there was the forest, and a place in it that everyone in Brzóza could have walked to and nobody ever did.

Halina stopped believing in God that winter, and she did it the way most people do it, which is without a decision and without a scene. She did not argue with the priest, or announce anything to her mother, or throw a crucifix into the river. She simply stopped praying one week, and then stopped going to church, and then stopped crossing herself when she passed the church door, and found that nothing happened to her, and that the hole where the thing had been was not a hole at all but a room, and that the room could be furnished. She furnished it with the Party. She worked harder than anyone in the district, carried messages, distributed pamphlets, sat through meetings that went on until midnight, learned the slogans and the songs and the names of the men in the photographs. She learned to hate the Germans, and the landlords, and the AK, and the Church, and the whole dead world of the village. She learned to hate Janusz. It was the easiest of the lessons, and it was the one she was best at.

There was a great deal she did not know. She did not know that Janusz's family had hidden a Jewish boy in the forge that same autumn, or that his father had been beaten half to death for it, or that Janusz had carried the boy out to the forest at night and given him to the partisans and come home with his hands shaking. She did not know that he had prayed for Rutka, or that he had cried. As far as she was concerned he was a coward hiding behind his church, on the wrong side of a war he had refused to fight.

---

Lublin, August 1943.

The Gestapo arrested Janusz on a Thursday.

He was in Lublin, delivering a message to the underground. He was staying in a safe house on Krakowskie Przedmieście. The Germans came at four in the morning. They broke down the door. They took him in his shirt. They put him in a black car. They drove him to the prison on the Castle Hill.

The cell was small and cold, with a concrete floor and walls painted the green that institutions use when they have given up, and a bucket in the corner, and a window high up with bars in it through which the light came in grey. Janusz sat on the floor with his hands tied behind his back and was afraid in a way he had not known he could be afraid.

The interrogation began in the morning.

The Gestapo officer was a polite man who spoke good Polish with a slight Berlin accent and offered Janusz a cigarette, which he took, because his hands were shaking and it was something to do with them. He asked about the underground, and the AK, and Berezowski, and the hideout in the forest. Janusz said he did not know. The officer smiled and said that was a pity, and that they had ways of helping people remember.

They beat him. They kicked him and beat him with a rubber truncheon and broke two of the fingers of his left hand, one at a time, and burned the soles of his feet with cigarettes. They hung him from a hook by the arms until his shoulders went out. They put a wire on him and passed a current through it, and asked him the same four questions, in the same order, for two days and two nights. He said he did not know. He said he was a courier and knew nothing. He said he was sorry. He cried, and begged, and prayed, and in the end he gave them the hideout — on the second night, at about four in the morning, with his own voice saying the words before his mind had agreed to them — because a man's body will do its own arithmetic at some point, and his did it sooner than he had believed a body could.

On the third day they brought him a photograph of his mother. They said they would kill her if he did not talk, and his father, and burn the house with them in it. He looked at the photograph. She was smiling in it; she was in the kitchen with the flour up to her elbows, making bread, waiting for a son to come home; she had no idea, when the picture was taken, what her son would be asked to trade for her life.

He broke.

He told them about the hideout in the forest. He told them where it was and that the People's Guard used it. He did not know who was in it that week; he did not know Tadeusz was there, or that anyone was; he knew a place and a distance and a path through the ferns, and none of that had been secret at the mill or in the village, and he gave them all of it.

The Gestapo raided the hideout on 14 August 1943.

Tadeusz Kowal was there. He was seventeen years old and he was asleep with two other young men in a bunker dug into a bank of ferns, and the Germans came at dawn and surrounded it and fired into the entrance. He ran. He got perhaps fifteen metres into the ferns before they shot him in the back, and he fell, and the ferns closed over him, and the other two were killed where they stood; and then the Germans burned the hideout and went away and left the three of them in the forest for the woodcutters to find on Thursday.

The Gestapo released him on 20 August, because they had decided he was a minor courier and not worth the paperwork. He walked out of the prison on the Castle Hill with two fingers splinted, his feet wrapped in cloth because he could not get his boots on, and a face the color of tallow, and he walked to the station and took a train to Puławy and walked the twenty kilometres to Brzóza, and got to the house in the evening. His mother opened the door. She looked at his face for a long moment and did not ask what had happened; she took him inside and sat him down and heated water and washed his hands and his feet and put salve on the burns, and fed him bread and milk, and did not ask. He did not tell her. He never told her. She was the first person he ever kept it from, and she was not the last.

The village found out about the raid. The People's Guard found out. They said that someone had betrayed the hideout. They said that the Gestapo had been told. They said that Janusz Wilga had been arrested and released. They said that he had talked. They said that he was a traitor.

Halina believed them.

She was in Puławy when she heard. She was sitting in a room with Marek and three other comrades. Marek told her. He said it gently. He said that Tadeusz was dead. He said that the hideout had been betrayed. He said that Janusz Wilga had been arrested and released. He said that the Gestapo did not release people unless they talked. He said that Janusz was a traitor.

Halina did not cry and did not scream. She sat very still in the chair in the room in Puławy with the other comrades looking at her and the kettle boiling on the stove, and after a while she said, "I know," which was not true, and which was the first lie she told for the revolution, and which she would remember longer than any of the others.

She knew because she had always known. She knew because Janusz was weak. She knew because he was a priest. She knew because he had chosen God over her. She knew because he had chosen the AK over the future. She knew because he had killed her brother.

Then she went home and cut off her hair. She had worn it in a braid since she was a girl; she took the kitchen knife and cut the braid off in one hand and put it in the stove and watched it burn with a feeling she would not have been able to name, and then put on a man's shirt and trousers and went back to work. She was nineteen. She became efficient. She became hard. She became, in the phrase the Party used in those years, reliable. She did not cry again for fifteen years.

What she did not know would have filled a book: that he had been tortured, and had broken, and had given up the location to stop them burning his mother's hands in front of him; that he had not known Tadeusz was in the bunker; that he had prayed for Tadeusz every day since, by name, and asked forgiveness for a death he had caused without knowing whose it would be. She did not know that he had loved her. She thought he was a traitor, and she hated him, and the hatred was a relief, because it was the only thing in the room that was warm.

---

Brzóza, July 1944.

The Soviets came in July.

The Germans left in the night, burning the manor and the mill behind them and taking the horses and the cows and shooting the dogs, and the Soviets came in the morning. They came on tanks, dirty and grinning, and gave the children chocolate and the women tinned meat, and put a red flag on the church tower. Father Grudziński took it down. They put it up again. He took it down again. They laughed and let him keep it down, which was the first and for a long time the only joke anybody in Brzóza heard about the new order.

Halina came back to the village in August 1944 in uniform, with a pistol on her hip and a red star on her cap, and the villagers looked at her and did not know what to say. Some were afraid of her and some were impressed by her and one old woman who had known her mother since before the war spat on the ground at her feet and walked away without a word. Góra was still the headman. He had changed sides; he was a socialist now; he greeted her as a comrade in the middle of the road, with both hands, and told her that he had always known she would go far. She knew what he had done. She knew he had sold Rutka and her mother for a sack of sugar and a good word with the Germans. She shook his hand anyway, and smiled, and asked after his wife, and it was the first time she understood that she had learned to do a thing she would not be able to unlearn.

She saw Janusz in the church.

He was at the altar in a black cassock, serving the Mass, and he looked up when she came into the doorway. He had entered the underground seminary in 1943, after his release; he was studying in secret in a room above a bakery in Lublin and coming back to the village to serve at the altar, and he had a breviary and a borrowed soutane and the beginnings of the manner he would have for the rest of his life. She stood in the doorway in her uniform with the pistol on her hip. He looked at her face, and then at the pistol, and then at her face again, and did not smile and did not frown; he looked at her the way a man looks at a thing he has been expecting for a year and had begun to hope would not come.

She turned and walked out.

Nor did she know the other thing, the small thing, which he would carry longer than the rest: that he had written her a letter in the seminary in the winter of 1946 and kept it in the pocket of his cassock for a year, and never sent it, and had prayed for her every day of his life since the birch tree.

She knew that he had killed Tadek. She knew that he was a traitor. She knew that he had chosen the church. She knew that he was on the wrong side. She walked out of the church and did not look back.

---

Lublin, February 1945.

The Urząd Bezpieczeństwa was on a side street in Lublin, in a gray building with a courtyard and a gate that was always shut. The corridors smelled of boiled cabbage and disinfectant. The offices were small and cold, and the men in them were young and serious and wore civilian clothes and carried pistols, and they were the new police and the new power, and they knew it, and they were careful with it, in the way that men are careful with a thing they have not yet had to use.

Halina worked in the press department. She wrote pamphlets, and slogans, and articles for the newspapers, and she was good at it: she had a clear mind and a sharp pen and a peasant's contempt for a sentence that was doing no work. She was promoted twice in a year and given an office with a window, and then a secretary, and then, in February 1945, a summons to the office of a man named Rydel.

He was older than her by perhaps fifteen years. He had a soft face and heavy lids and a gray suit that had been cut well before the war, and he offered her a cigarette and she said she did not smoke, and he lit one for himself and put the packet away and looked at her with an expression that she would come to know very well over the next thirteen years, which was the expression of a man who is about to give you a choice he has already made.

"Halina," he said. "We have a problem."

"What problem?"

"We have arrested a man. He is a member of the AK. His name is Janusz Wilga."

Halina's heart stopped. She did not show it. "I know him," she said.

"We know you know him. You were childhood friends. You were in the same village. We have a file."

"What do you want?"

"We want to know if he is a threat."

"He is a priest."

"He is a member of the AK. He is a threat."

"He is a seminarian. He is studying for the priesthood. He is not a threat."

"The AK is a threat. The AK is an anti-state organization. We are trying to build a new Poland. The AK is trying to restore the old Poland. We cannot allow that."

Halina looked at him. She saw the photograph of the girl with the ribbon on the wall behind his head, and the desk, and the file, and the glass of water she had not drunk; and she saw the man behind her chair, standing by the door with his pistol, not because he thought she would run but because that was how the room was arranged.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

"We want you to sign a report. We want you to tell us what you know about him. We want you to tell us who his commander was."

"I don't know his commander."

"You know Berezowski."

Halina closed her eyes, and saw the loft in the mill with the flour dust in the air, and Janusz's face at seventeen, and Tadek's face at thirteen, and the letter he had written her from the barracks at Dęblin in 1942 that she still had, and the hideout in the forest, and the ferns.

"He is a traitor," she said.

"Yes," Rydel said. "He is a traitor. We know that; we have the Gestapo report, and the Gestapo report is in German and signed, which in this office is the next best thing to a confession. But a report about a hideout in 1943 is not a network in 1945. We need the network. We need Berezowski. And you can help us, because you are from that village, and you know that name, and you have known it since you were a child."

"What will happen to Janusz?"

"He will be released."

"And Berezowski?"

"Berezowski will be arrested."

"And then?"

"And then he will be interrogated."

"And then?"

"And then he will be punished."

Halina looked at the glass of water. It was clear and ordinary and there was nothing in it, and she thought about the river, and the birch tree, and the boy who had kissed her under it, and the man who had killed her brother, and the future, and the Party, and the new Poland that was going to be built on all of it.

She signed the report.

The report said that Janusz Wilga was a member of the AK, pseudonym Sosna. It said that he was a dangerous element. It said that his commander was Berezowski, pseudonym Bór. It said that Berezowski was to be arrested and interrogated. It was signed H. Kowal.

Janusz was released in March 1945. He was never charged and never told why he had been arrested and never told who had signed the paper that sent the patrol to the hideout. He went back to the seminary, which had come up out of the ground with the spring, and was ordained in 1948, and became a priest, and was careful for the rest of his life with the word *confession*.

Berezowski was arrested three days after the report was signed. He was interrogated for six weeks. He was shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. He was forty-three. He had a wife and two children. His name was never mentioned in the newspapers. His family was told that he had died of a heart attack.

Halina never spoke of it. She never asked about Berezowski's family, or whether the wife in Otwock had been told, or where he was buried; she did not know his first name and never learned it. She had killed a man and had called it revolution, and the revolution had come, and the man was still dead, and the two facts sat in her at the same distance from each other as they had on the day it happened.

Warsaw, 1958.

Halina closed the file.

The snow was still falling and the Palace of Culture was still dark and the city was still there. She looked at the file and at the signature and at the photograph of the girl by the river, and it seemed to her, at four in the morning on a Tuesday in December, that she was both of them and would have to be both of them until she died, and that there was no arrangement of the facts that would let her be only one.

She put the file back in the briefcase and closed it and turned off the lamp and lay down again in the dark, and did not sleep, and watched the ceiling go from black to gray, and at six o'clock got up and made tea she did not drink and went to the office four hours early.

In the church on Brzeska Street, Janusz was kneeling at the altar rail. He had been there two hours, and his knees hurt and his back hurt and the two fingers the Gestapo had broken in 1943 had begun, as they did in cold weather, to ache in a way that was almost companionable. He was praying for Halina, and for Tadek, and for Berezowski and the man's wife and the man's two children, and for Rutka Kreiner, and for the village, and for the whole of Poland, which he had come to think of as a country full of people doing their best in a system designed to make their best impossible.

He knew now. That was the thing he could not put down: he knew. She had told him in the parish hall three weeks ago, in a voice with everything scraped out of it, that she had signed the paper that killed Berezowski, and that she had done it to save him, and that she had known what she was doing and had done it anyway. He had forgiven her before she finished the sentence, and she had refused the forgiveness, and she had been right to refuse it. Forgiveness was not a thing you handed a person like a coat. It was a thing you carried alongside them, in the same weather.

He was praying for her, and he was praying for Tadek, and he was praying for Berezowski, and he was praying for the woman in Otwock he had never met, and he was praying for the boy he had been in the cell in Lublin and the man he was now, and there was no difference between the two prayers and there never had been. He had loved her. He had lost her. He had found her again. None of it had been paid for. All of it had been given.

He prayed. The candle flickered. The snow came down past the window in the dark. At six the bell in the tower rang the Angelus, and the sound went out over the roofs of Praga, and he bowed his head.

He did not move.

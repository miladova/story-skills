# Chapter One: The Funeral of Brunon Kowalik

The crane cable had been frayed for months. The workers said so afterward, in the queue outside the factory gate, in the tavern on Brzeska Street, in the confessional of the Church of the Transfiguration, where a man can say things he would not say to his wife. The report said that Brunon Kowalik, crane operator, forty-two years old, had failed to inspect the cable before the morning shift. The report was typed on a single sheet of paper, signed by the factory director, stamped by the personnel office, and placed in a file that would never be opened again. The workers said the cable had been reported in June. They said the report had been lost. They said the crane had been scheduled for repair in the spring. The spring had come and gone. The cable had held until it did not.

Halina Sowa read the report in the back of a black FSO Warszawa as it crossed the Śląsko-Dąbrowski Bridge. The car was one of the small fleet assigned to the Central Committee, with a driver named Wiesław and a heater that made a sound like a man clearing his throat. The Vistula below was the color of dishwater. On the far bank, the towers of Praga stood in a gray mist, the church of St. Florian with its dark brick, the smaller roofs, the chimneys. The Palace of Culture was behind her, its silhouette in the rearview mirror, a needle stuck into the sky.

She read the report twice. Then she put it in her briefcase and looked out the window. The city was not beautiful in late October. It was a city of patched facades and empty lots, of new apartment blocks with windows like dark eyes, of trams that groaned around the corners. The war had been over for thirteen years, and still the gaps were there. Some had been filled with lawns and benches. Some had been left to the weeds. Some had been built on with the crude confidence of the new state. Halina had grown up in a village where the only stone building was the church. Now she lived in a city of stone, and she did not believe in the church.

"Comrade Sowa," Wiesław said. "The church is just ahead."

"I know."

"The workers are already inside. There's a crowd."

"I know."

He slowed the car into a narrow street of chestnut trees that had lost their leaves. On the corner a militiaman in a green cap stood with his hands behind his back watching the traffic, and a second one was writing something in a notebook, and they both turned to look at the Warszawa as it went past. The Church of the Transfiguration stood at the end of the street: baroque, with a tall tower and a facade the colour of old cream, and a darker patch of tile on the presbytery roof where a bomb had taken it off in 1944 and the parish had put it back. The church had survived. The parish had survived. The priest had survived. That was what the file said, and the file, for once, was accurate.

Halina stepped out of the car. The air was cold and smelled of coal smoke and wet leaves. She wore a black suit, a black coat, and a black hat with a small veil. She had dressed for a funeral, not for a meeting. She was not here to meet anyone. She was here to observe.

The church was full. The women sat in the front pews, their heads covered with dark scarves. The men stood at the back, in caps and work jackets, their hands folded in front of them. The children were quiet. The coffin stood on trestles in the center aisle, covered with a black cloth and a spray of chrysanthemums. The flowers were the color of rust. A portrait of Brunon Kowalik leaned against the coffin, a black-and-white photograph of a man with a heavy jaw and a half-smile, as if he had been caught between two thoughts.

Halina stood at the back, near the holy water font. She did not dip her hand. She had not touched holy water since she was a girl. She had not been in a church for anything but a state funeral in years. She knew the ritual. She knew when to stand and when to sit and when to lower her head. She had been raised on it. She could perform it the way a person can perform a song in a language they no longer speak.

The sacristan lit the candles. The organ played a low note, then a chord. The choir was small, mostly old women, and their voices were thin in the cold air. The priest came out of the sacristy.

Halina picked him out before she properly recognized him. He was tall, broad-shouldered, in a black cassock that was slightly too short at the ankles. He walked with his head down, his hands joined in front of him. His hair was dark, cut close. There was a scar above his left eyebrow, a pale line like a scratch in wood. He stopped at the coffin. He made the sign of the cross. He looked up.

Their eyes met.

For a moment it was not the priest in the pulpit at all but a boy of sixteen standing by the river with a fishing rod, a boy who could imitate the priest's cough so perfectly that the old women laughed into their hymnals, a boy who had kissed her under the birch tree and said, "I will love you until I am an old man." She saw the boy who had been arrested by the Gestapo and had come back with a scar and a silence. She saw the seminarian in a black cassock, in the village church in 1944, looking at her across the nave as if he had seen a ghost. She saw the man in the black cassock, the priest, the stranger.

He looked away first. He began the Mass.

The Latin was old and worn, like the stone of the church. Halina did not translate it in her mind. She listened to the sound of it, the rise and fall of the priest's voice, the responses of the congregation. She had learned the Latin as a child. She had loved the sound of it then. She had believed that the words were a door, and that behind the door was something bright. She had stopped believing in the door. But the sound still moved her. It was the sound of her childhood, of the village, of her mother's voice in the evening. She hated it a little.

The priest read the Gospel and stood in the pulpit with his hands flat on the wood, not looking at his notes. He looked at the congregation instead: the widow in the first pew, the children beside her, the workers standing at the back in their caps. He looked at Halina for a moment, and away.

"My friends," he said. "We are here to bury a man. We are not here to judge him. That is God's work, not ours. But we are here to ask a question. Why did he die?"

The church was silent.

"Brunon Kowalik was a crane operator. He worked at the Zakłady Mechaniczne for nineteen years. He was forty-two years old. He had a wife and three children. He was a good worker. He was a good man. He died because a cable broke."

Halina felt her chest tighten. She looked at the priest's face and understood that he was not shouting, and that his voice was low and even, and that he was not a demagogue working a crowd but a man stating a fact to a room full of people who had known the fact for years and had never heard it said aloud in church.

"The report says he was careless," the priest said. "The report says he failed to inspect the cable. I have read the report. I have also read the letter he wrote to the factory in June, asking for the cable to be replaced. I have spoken to the men who worked with him. They say the cable was frayed. They say the report was lost. They say the crane was scheduled for repair. They say the repair did not come."

A man at the back said, "That's true." His voice was rough. The priest waited until the man sat down.

"I am not a politician," the priest said. "I am not here to make a speech against the government. I am here to bury Brunon Kowalik and to ask why he died. The Gospel tells us that a man's life is worth more than the whole world. The Gospel does not say that a man's life is worth the plan. It does not say that a man's life is worth the quota. It does not say that a man's life is worth the report that says he was careless."

There were two men from the security service near the door: one writing in a notebook, the other watching the priest with a flat expression. She knew that expression. She had worn it herself.

"Brunon Kowalik was a worker," the priest said. "He was not a hero. He was not a martyr. He was a man who got up in the dark and went to work and came home in the dark and ate his dinner and played with his children. He was a man who loved his wife. He was a man who feared God, in his own way. He was a man who deserved to grow old."

The widow put her hand to her mouth. The children looked at her.

"The Gospel says that the last shall be first," the priest said. "It does not say that the last shall be a line in a report. It does not say that the last shall be a statistic. It does not say that the last shall be a number in a file that is closed and put in a drawer. The Gospel says that the last shall be first. If we believe that, then we cannot close the file. If we believe that, then we cannot say that Brunon Kowalik was careless. We cannot say that his death was the price of progress. Progress is not a god. It is a tool. A tool that kills its worker is not progress. It is a sacrifice. And we have had enough of sacrifices."

The church was very quiet. Halina could hear the candles hiss, and the traffic outside, and a tram bell, and somewhere at the back a man coughing into his hat, and her own heart going like a fist on a door.

The priest looked at the coffin. "Brunon," he said, "I did not know you. But I know your wife. I know your children. I know the men who worked with you. I know that you were not careless. I know that you were afraid. I know that you went to work anyway. That is what courage looks like. It is not a speech. It is a man getting up in the dark. May God forgive us for the times we have called that man careless. May God forgive us for the times we have closed the file."

He made the sign of the cross over the coffin. The organ began to play. The choir sang. Halina did not sing. She stood with her hands at her sides and looked at the priest. He was not the boy she had known. He was not the man she had imagined. He was a stranger with a scar and a voice that could make a room full of tired workers believe, for a moment, that their lives mattered.

She had spent twenty years learning how to make people believe things, and it had left her with an ear for it: she could tell when a speaker was lying, and when he was telling the truth, and when he was dangerous. The priest was dangerous.

After the Mass, the coffin was carried out into the street. The workers lifted it on their shoulders. The widow followed, her children holding her hands. The crowd moved toward the cemetery. Halina did not go to the cemetery. She stood in the churchyard, under the bare chestnut trees, and waited.

The rain began while she was standing there: a thin cold rain of the sort that gets into the bones and stays for the winter. She pulled her coat tighter. Wiesław had come round from the driver's side with an umbrella, and she waved him away, and he went back and stood by the car with the umbrella over himself and his collar up, and did not look at her.

The priest came out of the church twenty minutes later. He had removed his vestments. He wore the black cassock and a dark overcoat. He stopped at the top of the steps when he saw her.

"Halka," he said.

The name went through her like a needle. Nobody had called her that in years. Her mother had called her Halinka; her colleagues called her Comrade Sowa; the Party called her comrade, in the flat way the Party used when it wanted you to know it had a card with your name on it. The only person who had ever called her Halka was the boy who had kissed her under the birch tree, and he had been dead, as far as she was concerned, since about 1945.

"Ksiądz," she said.

The word was a door closing. He understood it and did not argue with it, and came down the steps towards her, and she saw that he was taller than she remembered, and leaner in the face, and that the scar above his eyebrow showed white in the grey light.

"You came," he said.

"I came for the funeral."

"You came for me."

She did not deny it. "I came for the funeral," she said. "I also came for you."

He looked at the wet street. "I saw you at the back of the church. I thought I was imagining it."

"You were not."

"Twenty years," he said. "Almost twenty."

"Nineteen," she said. "Since the autumn of 1939. We were seventeen."

"You remember the year."

"I remember everything."

He looked at her. His eyes were dark, with a tiredness that had not been there when he was a boy. "So do I," he said. "That is the problem."

The rain fell on the church steps. The bell in the tower rang the Angelus, a slow, bronze sound. Halina did not bow her head. Neither did he. They stood there, two people who had once been children in the same village, who had once loved each other with the absolute certainty of children, who had been separated by a war and an ideology and a river of years.

"I read your sermons," she said.

"I know. The Department keeps a file."

"You know about the file?"

"I know about the file."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you say you're here. I don't know why you're really here."

She almost smiled. "You always did ask the wrong questions."

"And you always did answer them with another question."

"I'm not a girl anymore, Janusz."

"No," he said. "You're not."

The use of his name was deliberate, and she watched him flinch. She had not called him by his first name in nineteen years, and it felt strange in her mouth, like a word in a language she had stopped speaking on purpose.

"The accident at the factory," she said. "The cable. The report. I'm here to investigate."

"You're here to manage it."

"I'm here to investigate."

"Is there a difference?"

"There is always a difference."

He looked at the church, at the people coming out, at the widow getting into a car. "The report is a lie," he said. "The cable was frayed. The workers reported it. The factory knew. Brunon Kowalik is dead because someone decided that the plan was more important than his life. You can put that in your report."

"I don't write reports like that."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I was told to come."

"By whom?"

"By the people who tell me to do things."

He nodded. "And what do they want you to do?"

"They want me to find out if you are a problem."

He smiled. It was not a warm smile. "And what do you think?"

"I think you are a problem."

"Good," he said. "Then we understand each other."

A black car turned the corner and slowed. Halina saw the two men from the security service in the front seat. The car stopped across the street. The driver did not get out.

"I have to go," she said.

"I know."

"Janusz."

He waited.

"I'm sorry about the man," she said. "Brunon Kowalik. I'm sorry."

"So am I."

She turned and walked to the car, and Wiesław opened the door for her, and she got in without looking back, and felt him on the steps behind her the whole way — the church, the tower, the dark windows, the altar where he had said the Mass; and behind all of that the village, and the river, and the birch tree, and the boy.

In the car she opened the briefcase and took out the report and read it again, and the words were the same words they had been that morning. The cable was not in it. The workers were not in it. The letter from June was not in it. It was a clean report, and it was a lie, and those two things had never seemed to her so exactly the same size.

She put it back in the briefcase and closed it. The car crossed the bridge. The Palace of Culture was ahead, its windows lit against the gray sky. Halina thought about the sermon, and about the line that had gone through the church like a struck bell: *We have had enough of sacrifices.* It was a dangerous line. It was a true line. It was the kind of line that could bring a man to the attention of the Central Committee. It was the kind of line that could end a career.

She had a career, and a flat on Marszałkowska, and a driver, and an office in the Central Committee with her name on the door and a file of her own in the registry downstairs. She had learned how to survive, which was the first thing she had ever been good at. She had learned that the truth was a luxury and that a lie was a currency, and that the state was not a monster but a machine, and that a machine does not hate anyone; it simply does not care, and will process you as it processes everything else, in order, at the speed of the queue.

But the priest's voice had been different. It had not been the voice of a machine; it had been the voice of a man who believed that a single life mattered more than a plan, and she had heard that voice before, in the village, in the forest, in the dark, and had believed it then too. She had heard it in her own chest, before she had learned to silence it.

The car stopped at a red light on Marszałkowska and Halina looked out of the window at a woman crossing in front of them with a child, and the child was holding a red balloon on a string, and the balloon bobbed in the wind and pulled at the child's hand, and the light changed, and the car moved.

Halina thought about the boy who had kissed her under the birch tree. He had been sixteen. He had been beautiful in the way that a boy is beautiful when he does not know it. He had told her that he wanted to be a priest. She had laughed. She had told him that priests were old men in black dresses. He had said, "Not all of them." She had said, "You'll be a terrible priest. You'll tell the bishop what you really think." He had said, "Maybe that's what the Church needs." She had said, "The Church doesn't need you. I do." He had kissed her. She had believed him.

She had been wrong about everything. She had been wrong about the Church. She had been wrong about the Party. She had been wrong about the world. But she had not been wrong about the boy. He had become a priest who told the truth. He had become a man who stood in a cold church and said that a worker's life mattered. He had become a problem.

The car reached the Central Committee. Wiesław stopped at the gate. Halina sat for a moment. The building was gray, with rows of windows. A red flag hung from the roof, limp in the rain. She thought about going in. She thought about her office, her desk, her telephone, her files. She thought about the report she would write. She thought about the line she would have to cross.

She got out and the guard saluted and she went in through the tall doors into a corridor that smelled of floor wax and cigarette smoke, under the portraits of men who had been dead a long time and were still, in the way of such portraits, watching the corridor.

At her office, she took off her coat and hat. She sat at her desk. She took out a sheet of paper. She wrote the date: 27 October 1958. She wrote the title: "Report on the Funeral of Brunon Kowalik." She wrote: "The funeral was held at the Church of the Transfiguration, Praga-Północ, at 10:00 a.m. The church was full. The congregation consisted mainly of workers from the Zakłady Mechaniczne and their families. The priest, Janusz Wilga, delivered a sermon that was critical of the factory administration and, by implication, of the Party's economic policy. The sermon was received with sympathy by the congregation. Two officers of the security service were present. No incidents were reported."

She stopped and read it through. It was accurate, and it was a lie, and the whole trick of the thing was in the gap between those two facts. It did not say that the sermon had made her feel something. It did not say that the priest was the boy she had loved, or that the cable was frayed, or that the report she had read in the car that morning was a lie. It did not say that she had stood in the rain and spoken to him. It did not say that he had called her Halka.

She put the report in a folder and the folder in her drawer and locked it, and then sat at her desk with her hands flat on the blotter and looked at the wall for a while.

The wall was bare. There was a portrait of Marx on the wall of the office next door. In her office, there was nothing. She had taken down the portrait years ago. She had told herself that it was because the wall was damp. The truth was that she could not stand the eyes.

She heard a knock at the door.

"Come in," she said.

The door opened. It was Rydel.

He was wearing a gray suit and a gray tie. He had a soft face, with heavy eyelids and a small mouth. He looked like a professor who had been asked to teach a class he did not believe in. He closed the door behind him.

"Halina," he said. "I hear you went to the funeral."

"I went to the funeral."

"Good. Did you learn anything?"

"I learned that the priest is a problem."

Rydel smiled. He took a cigarette from his pocket and lit it. He did not offer her one. He knew she did not smoke in her office.

"Then we have something to talk about," he said.

He sat down in the chair across from her desk. He did not take off his coat. He looked at the bare wall behind her, as if he were looking for something that was not there.

"Tell me about him," he said.

Halina looked at the report in the drawer, and thought about the boy by the river, and the man on the church steps, and the line in the sermon that had gone through her like a nail: *We have had enough of sacrifices.*

"He is a priest," she said. "He is young. He is popular with the workers. He is ambitious. He is a candidate for the episcopate. And he is in love with me."

Rydel raised an eyebrow. "In love with you?"

"That is what the file says," she said. "The file is usually right."

"The file is usually right," Rydel said. "But it is not always complete."

He leaned forward. He put his cigarette out in the ashtray. He looked at her with his heavy eyes.

"Halina," he said, "I have a job for you."

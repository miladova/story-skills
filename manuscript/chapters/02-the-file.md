# Chapter Two: The File

The alarm went off at six-thirty. Halina reached for it in the dark and knocked it off the nightstand. It landed on the wooden floor with a crash that sounded like a shot. She lay still for a moment, her heart beating fast. Then she got up.

The apartment was on Marszałkowska, on the fourth floor of a building that had been put up in the early fifties. It had two rooms, a kitchen, and a bathroom with a hot-water heater that worked three days out of seven. The windows faced east. In the morning, the light came through the thin curtains and made a pale rectangle on the wall. From the window, she could see the Palace of Culture, its spire like a needle, and the rooftops of the city, and the smoke rising from the chimneys.

She had lived here six years and had never hung a picture. The furniture was the standard issue of the housing office — a bed, a wardrobe, a table, two chairs, a bookcase — and the bookcase held Marx and Lenin and the collected speeches of the First Secretary and four novels that had come to her in the way such things came: a gift from a delegation, a prize for a course, a book left behind by a man who had not stayed the night. Behind the pamphlets on the bottom shelf, out of sight and out of habit, was a copy of *The Brothers Karamazov* that she had bought in a secondhand shop on Nowy Świat in 1952 and could not have explained to a single person in her building. She did not believe in God. She did not believe in the soul. She read the novel every winter, the way other people took a tonic.

She went to the bathroom and washed her face. The water was cold. She looked at herself in the mirror. She was thirty-six. She had a good face, she thought, not beautiful, but strong. Her hair was ash-blonde, cut short. Her eyes were gray. There was a line between her eyebrows that had appeared in the last two years. She had a habit of pressing her lips together when she was thinking. She did not look like a woman who had been in love with a priest. She looked like a woman who worked in an office.

She made coffee on the gas ring. The coffee was ersatz, made from roasted barley, with a spoonful of real coffee on top. She drank it standing by the window. The radio was on. The announcer read the news in a voice that was calm and certain. The seven-year plan was being drafted. The workers of the Stalin Steelworks had exceeded their quota. The American imperialists were continuing their aggression in the Middle East. The Catholic bishops had issued a statement. The announcer did not say what the statement was. He said it was "a matter of internal Church affairs." Halina knew what it was. The Jasna Góra raid. The Cardinal had called it a violation of the law. The Party had called it a routine inspection. The truth was a matter of vocabulary.

She turned off the radio. She went to the bedroom and opened the wardrobe. Her clothes were arranged by color: black, gray, dark blue. She took out a dark gray suit, a white blouse, and a pair of low-heeled shoes. She dressed carefully. She put on a small gold watch. She looked at herself in the mirror. She looked like a comrade. That was the point.

On the top shelf of the wardrobe, behind a stack of scarves, there was a wooden box. She had not opened it in months. She reached up and took it down. The box was small, made of dark wood, with a brass latch. Inside there was a photograph of a boy in a white shirt, a girl with a braid, and a wooden cross. The boy was Tadeusz. He was seventeen in the photograph. He had a wide grin and a cowlick. The girl was Halina. She was sixteen. She was not smiling. The cross was her mother's. It had been blessed by the priest in the village. Halina did not believe in the blessing. But she had kept the cross.

She looked at the photograph for as long as she could stand to, which was about a minute, and then put it back in the box and the box back on the shelf and shut the wardrobe door.

The driver was waiting at the entrance. He was a young man with a round face and a permanent cold. He held the door open for her. She got in. The car smelled of gasoline and wet wool. The heater was broken again.

"Rakowiecka," she said.

"Yes, comrade."

The city passed outside the window: women going to work with their shopping bags over their arms, men in caps waiting for the tram, a milk truck, a woman carrying a child on her hip because there was nowhere else to put the child. It was gray and it was alive. She had grown up in a village where the street was empty after dark, and Warsaw was never empty; it was a city of people who had survived, and they walked with their heads down, and she had decided some years ago that they were not looking for anything they had lost but keeping their eyes on the pavement out of a habit the war had taught them, and she did it too, and did not think it was the same thing.

The Ministry was a gray building on Rakowiecka behind a long driveway and a gate, and the guard at the gate looked at her identity card for exactly as long as it took to decide she was not going to be any trouble. The car stopped at the entrance. Inside, the building smelled of disinfectant and boiled cabbage and something underneath them both that she had never been able to name — not fear, exactly; the place where fear had been and gone and left its coat. The corridors were wide and empty and the portraits were of men in uniform, and the lift was 1930s, with a brass gate that creaked as it rose.

Rydel's office was on the third floor. The door was open. He was sitting at his desk, reading a newspaper. He looked up when she came in. He did not get up.

"Halina," he said. "Sit down."

She sat down. The chair was hard and too low for the desk, which was a thing she noticed because it was the sort of thing she noticed, and the office was larger than hers, with a map of Poland on the wall and a photograph of a girl of about twelve in a frame on the desk, with a ribbon in her hair and a smile for whoever was holding the camera.

"Your daughter?" Halina asked.

Rydel glanced at the photograph. "Yes. Agnieszka. She is at school."

"She looks like you."

"She has her mother's face," he said. "God rest her."

He put down the newspaper. He folded his hands on the desk. He looked at her with his heavy eyes. He had a soft voice, a soft face, and a soft way of moving. He was the most dangerous man she knew.

"I asked you to come because we have a problem," he said. "The problem is a priest. Janusz Wilga. The rector of the Church of the Transfiguration in Praga. You know him."

"I know him."

"You knew him. Before the war."

"I knew him before the war."

"He is a candidate for the episcopate. The Primate likes him. The Vatican is considering him. He is young, he is popular, and he is not ours. We cannot allow him to become a bishop. A bishop is a prince of the Church. A bishop has a voice. A bishop can make trouble for twenty years. We need to stop him."

"And what do you want me to do?"

"I want you to talk to him. I want you to persuade him to cooperate with us. There is a commission of priests that has agreed to work with the government. It is called the Commission of Priests. It is a patriotic organization. It supports the People's Republic. It opposes the reactionary elements in the Church. We want him to join."

"He won't."

"He might. If the right person asks him."

She understood. "You want me to ask him."

"You are the right person."

"I haven't seen him in nineteen years."

"You saw him yesterday."

She did not ask how he knew. The security service had been at the funeral; they had seen her on the church steps and had probably photographed her and had certainly written a note about it, and she had said nothing that could be used against her, because she had been careful since 1945. But Rydel did not need the funeral. He did not need words at all. He had the past, which was cheaper than a microphone and never went out of date.

"There is something else," he said.

He opened a drawer and took out a thin gray folder and put it on the desk between them. It had a red stamp on it, *Tajne*, and a number in the corner, and he did not open it, because the folder was not information; it was furniture, and he wanted her to sit and look at it.

"This is a copy of a file," he said. "It is a file we have had for fifteen years. It concerns Janusz Wilga. It concerns you. It concerns your brother."

Halina felt a coldness in her stomach. "My brother?"

"Open it."

She opened the folder.

The first page was a Gestapo report typed in German with a Polish translation attached, on paper thin enough to read the watermark through. The stamp at the top was the black eagle and the swastika. She had seen that stamp before: on the papers that had come after her father was taken to Germany in 1939, and on the notice that had ordered the Jews of the village into the square, and once, for a reason nobody had ever explained, on the door of the church. She had not known, until she turned the page and saw it again, that a stamp could live in a person's body for fifteen years and come up at the sight of it, like a scar in cold weather.

She read the translation.

"On 12 August 1943, at 4:30 a.m., the criminal investigation police arrested the Pole Janusz Wilga, born 22 November 1922 in Brzóza, son of the blacksmith Franciszek Wilga, on suspicion of activity for the communist bandit organization. During interrogation, the accused confessed that he was a courier for the organization. He gave the location of a hideout in the forest near Brzóza, approximately three kilometers from the village, used by the bandit group. The hideout was raided on 14 August 1943. Three bandits were killed. Among them was Tadeusz Kowal, born 4 March 1926, brother of the communist functionary Halina Kowal. The accused was released on 20 August 1943, as he was considered a minor figure."

The paper blurred. Halina read it again. She read it a third time. The words did not change.

She looked at Rydel. "He gave them the location."

"He gave them the location," Rydel said. "Under interrogation. The Gestapo had a way of asking questions."

"He was tortured."

"He was tortured. But the result was the same. Your brother is dead. The priest is alive. He has been a priest for ten years. He has heard confessions. He has given communion. He has buried the dead. He has never told anyone what he did. He has never been punished. He is about to become a bishop."

Halina put the paper down. Her hands were steady. She had learned to keep her hands steady. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because I want you to understand the kind of man he is."

"He was a boy."

"He was a man. He was twenty. He made a choice. He gave them the hideout. He killed your brother."

"He didn't know Tadeusz was there."

"He knew someone was there. He knew the hideout was used by the AL. He knew the Gestapo would raid it. He gave them the location. That is all that matters."

Halina looked at the folder. There was a second page. She reached for it. Rydel put his hand on it.

"Not yet," he said.

"Let me see it."

"You will see it. But first, I want you to understand something. The file is not complete. There is another report. It is from 1945. It is a report from the Urząd Bezpieczeństwa. It is about a man named Berezowski. It is signed by you."

Halina's mouth went dry. "That file is closed."

"No file is ever closed," Rydel said. "You know that. You have worked in the Party long enough to know that. The file is closed when we say it is closed. It is open when we need it to be open."

He lifted his hand. She took the second page.

The paper was a thin carbon copy. The type was faint. At the bottom, in blue ink, there was a signature: *H. Kowal*. The signature was hers. She had written it when she was twenty-three. She had written it in a small office in Lublin, with a glass of water on the desk and a man from the security service standing behind her. She had written it because she was afraid. She had written it because she wanted to save Janusz. She had written it because she wanted to save herself.

The report said that "the named person, Janusz Wilga, son of Franciszek, was a member of the anti-state organization Armia Krajowa, pseudonym Sosna. He is to be regarded as a dangerous element. The information was provided by the informant H. Kowal, who has proven reliable in the past. In addition, the named person, Berezowski, pseudonym Bór, was the commander of the unit to which Wilga belonged. Berezowski is to be arrested and interrogated."

Halina read the words and did not remember writing them, and remembered everything else: the office, the man behind her, the glass of water she had not drunk, and the arithmetic she had done in her head at seventeen minutes past four on a February afternoon in 1945 — *I will give them Berezowski. I will not give them Janusz. They will let him go. They will never find Berezowski. It will be all right* — which had been correct in two places out of four.

Berezowski had been arrested three days later. He had been interrogated for six weeks. He had been shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. Halina had heard about it from a friend. She had not asked for details. She had never spoken of it again.

She had killed a man. She had killed him to save another man. She had killed him because she was afraid. She had killed him because she was young and believed that the revolution was worth a life. She had killed him because she had not known what death was.

She looked up at Rydel. "You have kept this for fifteen years."

"I have kept this for fifteen years."

"Why?"

"Because it is useful. Because you are useful. Because one day I might need you to do something that you would not otherwise do."

"And now you need me."

"Now I need you."

He stood up. He walked around the desk. He stood by the window, with his back to her. The light from the window made his gray suit look almost white.

"Halina," he said. "I am not a monster. I know what it is like to be afraid. I was in a Sanacja prison before the war. I was in the Soviet Union during the war. I have seen what happens to people who do not cooperate. I have seen what happens to people who do. I am trying to keep this country from falling apart. The Church is a power. The Party is a power. They have to live together. The priest is a threat to that balance. If he becomes a bishop, he will use his power against us. He will preach against us. He will organize the workers. He will make martyrs. We cannot allow that."

"So you want me to destroy him."

"I want you to save him. If he joins the commission, he will be protected. He will have a future. He will be a bishop. The state will approve him. The file will stay closed. He will never know what you did. He will never know what he did. He will live a long and useful life. That is salvation, Halina. That is what we offer."

"And if he refuses?"

Rydel turned around. His face was sad. He looked like a man who was about to bury a friend.

"Then the file will be opened," he said. "The Gestapo report will be sent to the newspapers. The 1945 report will be sent to the Party. You will be exposed. He will be exposed. The Church will drop him. The Party will drop you. You will lose your career. He will lose his vocation. You will both be destroyed. That is not what I want. But it is what will happen."

Halina stood up and found that her legs were steady, and was surprised by it, having expected the weakness that follows a decision; but it was the strength of a person who has been given a clear choice, which is not like other strength and does not last.

"I will talk to him," she said.

"That is all I ask."

"I will not promise anything."

"I am not asking you to promise. I am asking you to try."

She picked up the folder. She put it in her briefcase. She closed the briefcase. She looked at Rydel.

"You said the file is not complete," she said. "What else is in it?"

Rydel smiled. It was a thin smile, like a crack in a wall. "There is always something else," he said. "But that is enough for today."

She left the office and went down through the empty corridor to the elevator, and the guard at the door saluted her the way he saluted everyone above the rank of section head, and out in the street the car was already waiting with its engine running and Wiesław holding the rear door open against the cold.

"Where to, comrade?"

"Drive," she said.

He drove, and she did not know where she wanted him to drive to, so he drove the long way along the river and she looked out the window at the trams and the people and the gray buildings, and thought about the boy in the photograph and the man in the church and the report. And Berezowski. She had never known his first name. He had been "Bór," the commander, a word on a page, a man with a wife and, she thought, children, though she had never asked and had never been told. She had killed a man and did not know his name, and now she was expected to kill the same man again with better paperwork.

The car crossed the bridge. The Vistula was gray. The ice was beginning to form at the edges. The Palace of Culture was on the left, its spire in the clouds. The city looked like a black-and-white photograph. Halina had lived in it for fourteen years. She had never loved it. She had never hated it. It was the place where she worked.

"Stop the car," she said.

Wiesław pulled over on the embankment without being asked, as he always did at that stretch of the river, and she got out and walked to the railing. The Vistula was below, dark and slow and going about its business. The wind came off it cold. She stood there a long time with her coat pulled around her, and Wiesław sat in the car with the engine running and the heater on and did not look at her, which was why she kept him.

A man in a cap walked past. He looked at her. She looked away. She did not want to be recognized. She was a comrade, a deputy department head, a woman with a driver and a briefcase. She was also a woman who had killed a man. She had been twenty-three. She had been afraid. She had thought she was saving Janusz. She had not known that Berezowski would be shot. She had not known that death was so quick. She had not known that guilt could last so long.

She thought about Tadeusz. He had been seventeen. He had been in the hideout because he believed in the revolution. He had been killed because Janusz had talked. She had hated Janusz for fifteen years. She had hated him with a hatred that had kept her warm on cold nights. It had been a kind of love. It had been the only thing left of the boy by the river.

Now Rydel had shown her the file. He had told her that Janusz was a traitor. He had told her that Janusz had killed Tadeusz. He had told her that Janusz was about to become a bishop. He had told her that she could save him or destroy him. He had told her that she was a killer too.

She had always known that she was a killer. She had known it since 1945. She had buried the knowledge. She had built a career on top of it. She had built a life on top of it. She had told herself that it was necessary. She had told herself that Berezowski was a soldier, that he would have been killed anyway, that the revolution needed sacrifices. She had told herself that she was not a murderer. She was a functionary. She was a survivor. She was a woman who had done what she had to do.

But the file was in her briefcase. The file was a piece of paper with her signature on it. The file was a man named Berezowski, who had been shot in the back of the head in a forest near Lublin. The file was a boy named Janusz, who had been tortured by the Gestapo and had given up a hideout. The file was a girl named Halina, who had signed a report to save him.

She turned away from the river. She got back into the car.

"Home," she said.

The car took her home to Marszałkowska. She hung up her coat, put the kettle on out of habit, and then sat down at the kitchen table with the briefcase in front of her and did not open it for a long time, and looked at it, and listened to the kettle come to the boil and go on boiling until it began to spit.

The telephone rang. She jumped. She picked it up.

"Halina?"

It was Maryla. Maryla Zawadzka, her friend from the village, now a doctor at a clinic in Mokotów. They had known each other since they were children. Maryla had been the one who had told her about Rutka. Maryla had been the one who had invited her to Christmas Eve every year. Maryla was the only person who called her at home.

"Yes."

"You didn't come to the meeting yesterday. I was worried."

"I was busy."

"You are always busy. I am coming over. I have a cake."

"Now?"

"Yes. I am downstairs."

Halina looked at the door. She heard footsteps in the corridor. She opened the door. Maryla was standing there with a cake in a tin and a bottle of wine. She was a short woman with a round face and graying hair. She wore a red coat and a knitted hat. She looked like a woman who had spent her life taking care of other people.

"You look terrible," Maryla said.

"Thank you."

"I mean it. You look like you have seen a ghost."

"I have."

Maryla came in with snow in her hair, took off her coat and hat, put the cake on the table, and opened the wine without being asked. She did not ask what ghost. She had known Halina since they were six years old and had learned, in that time, that Halina would tell her when she was ready and not one minute before, and that pushing produced the opposite of the desired result.

They sat at the kitchen table and Maryla cut the cake, which was a poppy-seed cake of the kind Halina's mother had made on the great feast days, and Halina took a piece and ate it slowly because it tasted of a kitchen in Brzóza in about 1934, and because a woman who has spent the day being blackmailed is entitled to a piece of cake.

"The children are well," Maryla said, and went on with the list because it was the kind of list that got her through an evening. "Ewa is at the university, studying medicine, and wants to be a doctor like her mother, God help her. Marek is in the army, and hates it, and says the food is terrible and the officers are worse, and wants to come home. He writes me three pages about the food. Three pages, Halina. I have raised a boy who thinks a potato is a human rights question."

"Tell him to stay."

"I told him. He doesn't listen to me."

"He is young."

"Young people don't listen. We didn't listen either."

Halina smiled. "No. We didn't."

Maryla looked at her. "What is it, Halina?"

"What is what?"

"You have that look. The look you get when you are about to do something stupid."

"I always look like that."

"No. You look like you have seen something you cannot forget."

Halina looked at the briefcase on the table and thought about the file in it, and the photograph of Tadeusz in the wardrobe, and the man in the church on Brzeska Street, and the three of them would not leave her alone long enough for her to sleep.

"Do you believe in God?" she asked.

Maryla put down her fork. "What kind of question is that?"

"A simple question."

"Of course I believe in God."

"Why?"

"Because I was raised to believe. Because I have seen too much to believe in nothing. Because I have a daughter who is alive, and a son who is alive, and a husband who is alive, and I am grateful. Gratitude is a kind of belief, I think."

"I don't believe in God."

"I know."

"I believe in the Party."

"Do you?"

Halina was silent.

Maryla reached across the table and took her hand. "You are not a bad person, Halina. You are a person who has been asked to do bad things. There is a difference."

"Is there?"

"I think so."

"I don't know anymore."

Maryla squeezed her hand. "Come to Christmas Eve. Bring a bottle. We will sing the old songs. We will be bored. We will be normal. You need to be normal for one night."

Halina looked at her friend across the table and felt the thing she always felt with Maryla, which was not love exactly, or not only love: something older than love and less forgiving, the memory of a village and a river and a church and two girls with braids who had believed that life would be a certain way.

"I'll come," she said.

"Good."

Maryla left at nine, with her tin and her coat and her hat and a long look at the briefcase that she did not comment on. Halina washed the dishes, put the cake in the cupboard, poured herself a glass of wine she did not want, and sat down at the table, and opened the briefcase.

The file was inside. She took it out and put it on the table and looked at it without opening it, the way a person looks at a letter from a doctor.

She read the Gestapo report again, and then the 1945 report, and then looked at her own signature at the bottom of it. It was a young signature, with a flourish on the H that she had practised in 1945 because it had seemed to her that a person who was going to sign documents should have a signature worth signing; and she had been proud of it, and had thought it looked like the signature of a woman who mattered, and it had.

She closed the file and put it back in the briefcase. Then she went to the wardrobe and took down the wooden box from the top shelf, and opened it, and took out the photograph of Tadeusz. He was seventeen in it, laughing at something the camera could not see, with his cowlick standing up and the small white scar on his chin from the summer he fell off a borrowed bicycle into the ditch by the mill. He was her brother. He was dead. She had known both of those facts for fifteen years and they had never once, in all that time, arrived at the same moment.

"Tadek," she said.

The word sounded strange in the empty apartment. She put the photograph back in the box. She put the box back on the shelf. She closed the wardrobe.

She went to the telephone and dialed the parish number and it rang for a long time, long enough for her to begin composing what she would say, and then a woman answered — Pani Genowefa, out of breath, with the wireless going behind her.

"Parafia Przemienienia Pańskiego."

"This is Halina Kowal," she said. "I would like to speak to the priest."

"Which priest?"

"Father Wilga."

There was a pause. "Who shall I say is calling?"

"Halina Kowal."

Another pause. The woman's voice changed. "I will tell him."

Halina waited, and heard footsteps in the passage and then a door, and then the priest's voice close to the receiver, saying her office's number aloud to somebody in the room.

"Yes."

"It's Halina."

"I know. They told me."

"I would like to see you."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow. In the afternoon."

"Come to the rectory. At three."

"Thank you."

"Halina."

"Yes?"

"Are you all right?"

She looked at the briefcase on the table. "No," she said. "But I will be."

She hung up and turned off the light and lay down in the dark, and the city was quiet the way a city is quiet at midnight on a Tuesday, and the Palace of Culture was a dark shape against a sky with no stars in it, and she thought about the boy by the river, and the man in the church, and the file, and the choice, and she was still thinking about them when the trams started again at half past four.

She slept.

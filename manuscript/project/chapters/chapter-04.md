# Chapter 4: The Sermon

## Chapter Text

On the last Sunday of November the Church of the Transfiguration was full an hour before Mass, which had not happened since Easter. The workers came from the factory with their coats brushed and their hair wetted down. The women came from the courtyards with their heads covered and their hands full of flowers, and the children came from the catechism class scrubbed and polished and warned, in the way children are warned, to sit still and not to stare at anybody. The church smelled of wet wool and incense and candle wax. The bell rang. Then it rang again, because the crowd was still coming in, and Pan Tadeusz kept ringing it until the porch was empty, which took a quarter of an hour.

Halina sat at the back, in the last pew, in the gray coat and the gray hat she had worn to the funeral a month before, which was not a coincidence and which she had chosen not to examine. She had come alone again. Wiesław was in the car with the engine running because she had told him to keep it running. She had told herself she was there to hear the sermon in order to report it, and had known, walking up the steps, that this was the third or fourth most convincing lie she had told herself in a career built out of them.

The coffin was not there; Brunon Kowalik had been in the ground a month. But the workers had put a memorial in the centre aisle anyway — a table with a red cloth on it, his photograph in a frame, his helmet, a pair of his work gloves, a candle — and the women had brought flowers and the children had brought a drawing of a crane with a sun in the corner of it. The drawing was crooked and the sun was much too large and it was the most beautiful thing in the church.

Janusz stood at the altar in a black vestment and a white stole with a face the color of the candles, having not slept. He had rewritten the sermon four times and had considered not preaching it for reasons that were all good reasons: the parish, the youth club, the permit, the file, the black car across the street. He had prayed. He had decided. He had discovered, somewhere around the third rewrite, that he had decided weeks ago and had been looking ever since for a reason that would let him un-decide.

He read the Gospel. It was the passage from John: "You shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." He read it slowly. He read it as if he were reading it for the first time. Then he climbed the pulpit.

"My friends," he said. "We have heard the words of Christ. 'You shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.' It is a beautiful sentence. It is also a dangerous sentence. Because the truth does not make us free immediately. First it makes us miserable. First it shows us what we have done. First it takes away our excuses."

The church was quiet. At the back, by the door, one of the two security men had stopped writing and was holding his pen above the page without moving it, and the other had turned his head to look at the priest's face.

"A month ago I buried Brunon Kowalik," Janusz said, "and I said then that he was not careless. I want to say something harder today, and it is not about the director of the works. The director of the works is a frightened man with a plan to fulfil. I could tell you his name and it would not help you." He put both hands on the edge of the pulpit. "The report on Brunon Kowalik was typed by a typist. It was stamped by a clerk. It was filed by a filing clerk, in a registry, in a building on a street you all know, by a woman or a man who went home afterwards and ate supper. That is what I want to say. There is no monster in this story. There is a chain of ordinary people doing one small wrong thing each, because the wrong thing was easier, and every one of them went home and ate supper. And I am one of them, and so are you."

A man at the back said, "That's true." His voice was rough. Janusz waited.

"I am not here to make a speech against the government," he said. "I am a priest, and I am here to say what a priest is for. You are afraid. I know you are afraid, because I am afraid, and because I have been hearing confessions in this parish for seven years and there is not a man in this parish who has not told me so in the dark. We are afraid of losing our jobs and our flats and our ration cards, of being called an enemy of the people, of being taken off a tram in the night. And so we close the file. We say the man was careless. We say the cable was inspected. We say the report was lost. And then we come here on Sunday and we say the Creed, and we say *I believe*, and we go home, and those two things cannot both be true in the same person for very long."

Halina sat with her gloved hands folded in her lap and the leather damp against her palms, and thought: I have written that report. I have written it a hundred times, in a hundred versions, for fifteen years, and I have never once written it because I was afraid. I wrote it because I believed it. That is the thing I will have to answer for, and I would rather it had been fear.

"Christ says that the truth will make us free," Janusz said. "He does not say that the truth will make us comfortable. He does not say that the truth will make us happy. He says it will make us free. And freedom is not the absence of fear. Freedom is the ability to speak the truth in the face of fear. Freedom is the ability to say: this man was not careless. This man was murdered. This cable was frayed. This report is a lie. This file must not be closed."

The congregation had gone silent in the way a room goes silent when everyone in it has stopped breathing at the same moment: the old women crying, the workers straightened up, the children checking their parents' faces to see what was allowed, and the two men by the door writing again, one of them steadily, in shorthand, like a man taking dictation from history. Halina felt her heart going. She knew the sermon would be reported and the priest called in and the parish put under a glass. She knew the state would not tolerate it. She knew she should get up and go out and stand on the steps and be seen to be leaving. She did not move.

"There is a widow in this parish," he said, "sitting four rows from the front, with three children and a pension that will not keep them and a letter from the works that says her husband was careless. She has shown me that letter. She asked me a question I could not answer. She asked me whether the Church was going to say he was careless too, because if the Church said it as well there would be nobody left in the world who did not say it." He looked at the front pews. "So I am saying it now, in this church, in front of the parish and in front of whoever is taking notes. Brunon Kowalik was not careless. The cable was frayed and it was reported in June, and if any man here saw the crane and said nothing, he knows it, and I will not ask him to stand up."

He paused, and turned to the memorial table: the helmet, the gloves, the drawing of the crane with the sun in the corner.

"Today I ask you to do something dangerous," he said, "and it is not to riot and not to hate, and it is not to stand in the street with a placard. I ask you to be willing to be a witness. Not a martyr. A witness. A man who saw the cable, and can say when he saw it, and to whom he reported it. That is all. It is a small thing and it will be written down and it will make somebody in an office very uncomfortable, and one or two of you will come to me afterwards and say Father, not my name."

He came down the pulpit steps and went to the memorial table and picked up the candle and held it up, and the flame bent in the draught from the door and then stood straight again.

"Brunon Kowalik," he said, and his voice went, and he had to stop and start again. "Brunon Kowalik. Servant of God. A man who got up in the dark. A man who was afraid and went anyway, which is the whole of what we mean when we say a man was brave, and which we have spent a month pretending was not enough. May God forgive us the month. May God forgive us the file. May God give us the grace to be a witness and not a martyr, because there are too many martyrs in this country already and not enough people willing to say what they saw."

He put the candle down and made the sign of the cross over the coffin, and the organ began to play, and the choir came in a beat late, and the whole congregation stood up at once with a scrape of chairs and boots that went through the church like a wave.

Halina stood with them and did not sing. She looked at the priest and thought that he was not the boy she had known, and not the man she had imagined either — that he was a man who had stood up in a church full of frightened people and said the true thing at the cost of his parish and his career and possibly his liberty, which was a thing she had once believed in and had not believed in for fifteen years. He had chosen. She had not chosen anything since 1945. She had only chosen to survive, over and over, in a series of small decisions that had added up to a life.

After the Mass the workers gathered in the parish hall in the way they always gathered after a funeral, which is to say without anybody having arranged it, and stood around in their good coats being angry and frightened and wanting something done; and Janusz stood at the front of the room and let them talk and did not interrupt, because a room full of men who have just heard the truth needs a quarter of an hour to find out what it wants to do with it.

"We should go to the factory," said a young man named Zbyszek. He was twenty-two, with a red face and a loud voice. "We should stop the line. We should demand an investigation."

"If we stop the line, they will arrest you," Janusz said.

"Then they will arrest me."

"And then what? Will that bring Brunon back?"

"No. But it will show them that we are not afraid."

"They know you are not afraid. They are the ones with the guns."

"We can petition the government," said an older man. "We can write to the Sejm. We can write to the newspapers."

"The newspapers will not print it."

"Some of them will."

"Some of them will be closed."

"Then we will write to the Cardinal."

"The Cardinal is not a factory inspector."

"Then what do we do, Father?"

Janusz looked around the room at their faces, at the anger in them and the fear and the small stubborn thing that was not hope exactly but would do instead. He wanted to tell them to fight, and to be brave, and that the truth would win. He knew it was not true. The truth did not always win; in his experience it was usually buried, and often killed, and now and then forgotten so completely that people began to speak of it as a thing that had never existed. But silence was not the answer either. Silence was what the state wanted, and what the report wanted, and what the closed file was for.

"We do what we can," he said. "We help the widow. We help the children. We write the letter. We keep the memory. We do not let them close the file. And we do not let them make us hate them. Because if we hate them, we become like them."

Zbyszek looked at him. "That is not enough, Father."

"It is not enough. But it is something."

"Something is not enough."

"No. But it is what we have."

The meeting ended and the workers went home through the rain, and Janusz stayed in the parish hall and sat down at the table and put his head in his hands. He was tired and afraid and not at all sure he had said the right thing, or that there was a right thing to have said.

The door opened. It was Halina.

She was still in her gray coat, with her face pale and her eyes red, and he understood with a small shock that she had been crying — she, who had been angry and cold and ironic and exhausted in his company for twenty years, and whom he had never once seen cry.

"Halina," he said.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say anything. Just listen."

He stood up. She came toward him down the side aisle and stopped a few feet away with her hands clenched at her sides and her eyes red, and he understood that she had been crying in the car, and that she would have hated anyone to know it.

"The security service is going to arrest you," she said. "Not today, and probably not tomorrow, because they will want to see what the sermon does in the works first. But soon. They have enough already — the sermon, the meeting in the hall, the petition with four hundred names on it — and if they want more they will invent it. They will say you are inciting the workers, and a reactionary element, and an agent of the Vatican; and one of those three will stick, because it always does, and because there is no man in this country who cannot be made to sound like a foreign agent if the sentence is written by a man who is paid to write it."

"Let them."

"Let them? Do you know what happens to priests who are arrested? Do you know what happens in those rooms? Do you know what they do?"

"I know."

"You don't know. You think you know. You think you are prepared. You are not prepared. No one is prepared."

"I am a priest."

"You are a man."

"I am a priest."

"You are a man who has been tortured once. Do you want to be tortured again? Do you want to be broken again? Do you want to give them another name? Do you want to kill another man?"

The words hit him like a slap. He stepped back. She saw the pain in his face. She did not apologize.

"That is what you are afraid of," she said. "Not the arrest. Not the prison. You are afraid that they will break you again. You are afraid that you will give them another name. You are afraid that you will kill another man. That is why you preach. That is why you hide behind the truth. Because you are afraid of what you will do when they put the wire around your testicles again."

Janusz sat down. He put his head in his hands. He felt sick. He felt old. He felt the pain in his fingers, the old pain, the pain that never went away. He heard the Gestapo officer's voice. He heard his own voice, cracked, begging, giving the location. He heard the sound of the door. He heard the silence after the door.

"Halina," he said.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I didn't mean—"

"Yes, you did."

"Yes. I did."

He looked up at her. "You are right," he said. "I am afraid. I have always been afraid. I have been afraid since 1943. I have been afraid every day. I have been afraid of the Gestapo. I have been afraid of the UB. I have been afraid of the SB. I have been afraid of the bishop. I have been afraid of the cardinal. I have been afraid of God. I have been afraid of you. I have been afraid that I am not a good priest. I have been afraid that I am a coward. I have been afraid that I will be broken again."

"Then why do you preach?"

"Because I am a priest."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have."

Halina sat down across from him. She put her hands on the table. "Janusz," she said. "I am not here to save you. I am here to save myself. I have a file. Rydel has a file. The file has my name on it. If I don't bring you to him, he will destroy me. If I bring you to him, he will destroy you. I don't know what to do."

"He wants you to persuade me to join the Commission."

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"He will release the file. The 1943 report. The 1945 report. The one with my name on it."

"The one about Berezowski."

Halina looked at him. "You know about Berezowski?"

"I know his name. I know he was my commander. I know he was arrested in 1945. I know he was shot. I did not know you were involved."

"I was involved."

"What did you do?"

She told him. She told him about the office in Lublin. She told him about the man behind her. She told him about the glass of water. She told him about the signature. She told him about Berezowski. She told him that she had done it to save him. She told him that she had not known Berezowski would be shot. She told him that she had killed a man.

Janusz listened and did not interrupt and did not judge, and looked at her face in the light of the sacristy lamp, and saw the girl by the river and the woman in the gray coat and the murderer and the victim, all of them printed on top of one another like a photograph taken twice on the same plate, and the last of them, always, was the person he loved.

"I have hated you for fifteen years," she said. "I have hated you because I thought you killed Tadek. I have hated you because I thought you were a coward. I have hated you because you became a priest and I became a communist. I have hated you because you were good and I was not."

"I am not good."

"You are better than I am."

"No. I am not."

"You are."

"Then why did I give them the hideout?"

"Because you were afraid."

"Yes."

"And I was afraid."

"Yes."

"And we both killed someone."

"Yes."

They sat in silence. The rain had stopped, and the church was doing the thing a church does after a crowd has left it, which is to hold the last of the warmth and the last of the noise and give them back slowly. A tram bell rang out on Brzeska Street and went away.

"Halina," Janusz said.

"Don't."

"I forgive you."

She looked at him. "You don't know what you are forgiving."

"I know enough."

"You can't forgive me. I haven't asked."

"You don't have to ask."

"I don't want your forgiveness."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want to stop being afraid."

He reached across the table and took her hand, which was cold, and held it, and she did not pull away. They sat like that in the parish hall with the tea going cold between them, two people who had been broken by the same war in two different places, and who had spent fifteen years being angry at the wrong person.

"I can't stop being afraid," he said. "But I can be afraid with you."

She looked at him. Her eyes were wet. "That is the most romantic thing you have ever said."

"It is not romantic. It is theology."

"It is a bad theology."

"It is the only theology I have."

She laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was real. She wiped her eyes with her glove.

"Janusz," she said.

"Yes."

"I have to go."

"I know."

"Rydel is waiting."

"I know."

She stood up and let go of his hand and walked to the door, and stopped there with her hand on the frame and her back to him.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For not lying."

"I lied to you once."

"I know."

"I am sorry."

"I know."

She left. He sat at the table and looked at his hand, which was still warm where she had been holding it, and thought about the kiss under the birch tree in 1939 and the girl with the braid and the woman in the gray coat, and the file, and the sermon, and the fear, and the truth, and God. He did not know what to do, or what to say, or how to pray. So he sat at the table, and waited, which is what a man does when he has done the thing he was afraid of and has not yet found out what it will cost.

The next morning, Halina went to the factory.

The Zakłady Mechaniczne im. Nowotki was a vast complex of brick sheds and smokestacks on the east bank of the Vistula, and at seven in the morning the gates were open and the shifts were coming in through them in a river of caps and bicycles. The air smelled of coal and iron and hot oil, and the noise was a single continuous roar with the clang of metal somewhere inside it and the hiss of steam somewhere else and men shouting under both. Halina went through the gate with her identity card in her hand; the guard saluted her, and looked at the card, and saluted again, and she walked into the works the way she had walked into a hundred works in the last ten years, and hated it a little more than usual.

The personnel office was on the second floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled of rubber and carbon paper. The director, inż. Ryszard Sokół, was waiting for her: a tall man with a gray face and a nervous smile, in a dark suit with a Party badge on the lapel that he touched, she noticed, the way a man touches a lucky coin.

"Comrade Sowa," he said. "This is an honor."

"Is it?"

"We don't often get visitors from the Central Committee."

"You have one now."

He smiled. He had the smile of a man who has been smiling for so long that he has forgotten what it means.

"The Kowalik accident," she said. "Tell me about it."

Sokół opened a folder. He read from it. His voice was flat. "On the morning of 14 October, at 7:20, the crane operator Brunon Kowalik was killed when a load slipped from the crane hook and fell on him. The investigation found that the cable was in working order and that the accident was caused by operator error. The worker failed to inspect the cable before the shift."

"Who investigated?"

"The factory safety commission."

"Who was on the commission?"

"The chief engineer, the personnel director, and the Party secretary."

"Was any worker on the commission?"

"No."

"Was the widow interviewed?"

"No."

"Was the letter that Kowalik wrote in June included in the report?"

Sokół's smile flickered. "There was no letter."

"There was a letter."

"Who told you that?"

"The priest."

"The priest is not a factory inspector."

"No. He is a priest. He hears confessions."

Sokół looked at her and put down the folder very carefully, the way a man puts down a glass when his hand has begun to shake. "Comrade Sowa," he said. "I have been the director of this factory for six years. I have fulfilled the plan every year. I have been decorated twice. I have a wife and two children and a flat on Waszyngtona with a telephone in it. I am not a criminal. I am a man who was given an old crane and a plan and no cable, and I reported the cable, and I have the copies of the letters in this folder to prove that I reported it, and nobody in this city will read them, because the only thing anyone will want to know is why the director did not stop the crane."

"I did not say you were a criminal."

"You are here because of the priest. He has been stirring up the workers. He has been telling them that the factory killed Kowalik. He has been telling them that the report is a lie. He has been telling them that I am a murderer."

"Are you?"

"No."

"Then you have nothing to fear."

Sokół's face had gone the color of the ash in the tray. "The cable was old," he said. "I knew it was old. I reported it in April and again in June and the replacement did not come, because the plan came first and the allocation came second and the cable came a long way third, and I signed the inspection because if I had not signed the inspection the works would not have made the quarter and there would have been a new director by Friday who would have signed it instead. I did what I could. I know what that is worth. I have known since the fourteenth of October what it is worth."

"Did you?"

"I did what I could."

"That is not the same."

"What do you want me to do? Do you want me to go to the workers and tell them that the factory killed their friend? Do you want me to tell them that the plan is more important than their lives? Do you want me to tell them that I am a coward? Do you want me to lose my job? Do you want my children to starve?"

"No."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want the truth."

"The truth is that the cable was old. The truth is that I reported it. The truth is that the replacement did not come. The truth is that Brunon Kowalik is dead. The truth is that I am afraid. The truth is that we are all afraid."

Halina looked at him. She had come into the room expecting a monster, and had found a man who had made a compromise somewhere back down the line and learned to live inside it, which was a thing she understood so exactly that it was almost unbearable. He was a man like her.

"Thank you," she said.

"Is that all?"

"That is all."

She left the office and walked out through the works, through the noise and the heat and the long sheds with their belts turning overhead, and found the men from the crane gang in the yard by the foundry, and talked to them. They were afraid to speak at first. Then an old man named Mieczysław came forward, sixty years old and bent, with a face like a dried apple and hands that had been in the trade since before the war.

"The cable was frayed," he said. "I reported it in June. I wrote a report. I gave it to the safety commission. The report was lost."

"Do you have a copy?"

"No."

"Do you have anything?"

He reached into his pocket. He took out a piece of metal. It was a cable strand, frayed at the end, about ten centimeters long.

"I kept this," he said. "I knew someone would come."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the first one who came."

Halina took the cable. It was cold and heavy. She put it in her bag.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," Mieczysław said. "Thank God. If you believe in Him."

"I don't."

"Then thank the priest. He is the one who told us to remember."

Halina left the factory in the middle of the afternoon and went to Brzeska Street, to a tenement with a broken gutter and a smell of coal in the stairwell, and up four flights to Wanda Kowalik's room. It was one room, clean and poor: a bed, a table, two chairs, a stove, a shelf with four books on it and a photograph. The three children were sitting on the bed — Ewa twelve, Marek nine, Tomek six — and they were quiet with the particular quietness of children who have learned that quietness is the safest thing to be.

Wanda was at the stove with her back to the door. She was thin, with dark hair going gray at the temples and a face that would have been pretty if it had not been so tired; the tiredness was recent, and it sat on her features like a coat that did not belong to her. She poured Halina a glass of tea without asking and set it down, and Halina sat.

"I am sorry about your husband," Halina said.

"Thank you."

"I am investigating the accident."

"The investigation is over."

"Not this one."

Wanda looked at her. "Are you from the factory?"

"I am from the Central Committee."

"The Central Committee."

"Yes."

"My husband wrote to the factory in June," Wanda said. "To the director. He asked for the cable to be replaced. He got no answer. He wrote again in August, and to the union, and to the Party committee, and he got no answer from any of them, and he was not a troublemaker, he was a good worker, he had been at that crane eleven years. He just wanted the cable replaced. He said it was dangerous. He said it could kill someone." She looked at the table. "It killed him."

"Do you have the letters?"

"I have one."

Wanda went to the shelf and took down a book — a school reader, with a picture of a stork on the cover — and opened it, and took out a letter that had been folded twice and flattened under the weight of the other books. She gave it to Halina without a word. It was written in pencil on a page torn from a notebook, in a careful hand, the hand of a man who had left school at fourteen and had never quite stopped being proud that he could write.

*Dear Comrade Director,*
*I am writing to report that the cable on crane number 4 is frayed. I have reported it to the safety commission. I ask that it be replaced before an accident occurs. I have a wife and three children. I do not want to die.*
*Respectfully,*
*Brunon Kowalik*

Halina read the letter. She put it in her bag with the cable.

"Thank you," she said.

"What will you do?"

"I will write a report."

"A report."

"Yes."

"Will it say what happened?"

"Yes."

"Will they listen?"

"I don't know."

Wanda looked at her. Her eyes were hard. "You people always promise," she said.

"I am not them."

"You are in their car."

"I am in their car. But I am not them."

"Then prove it."

Halina looked at the three of them. Ewa was holding a book with her finger in it to keep the place. Marek was holding a wooden car with one wheel missing. Tomek was holding his mother's hand. They looked back at her with the flat evaluating gaze of children who have learned that adults who arrive with briefcases bring news of the sort that is discussed after they are sent to the neighbours.

"I will prove it," she said.

She left the apartment and went back across the river to the Central Committee and sat down at her desk and took out a sheet of paper and wrote the truth. She wrote about the cable and the letter and the director and the safety commission, and about the workers in the yard who had been afraid to speak until an old man named Mieczysław spoke first, and about the widow and the three children and the school reader with the stork on the cover; and when she had written it all, which took an hour and a half, she read it. She knew that if she sent it, it would be buried. She knew that if she sent it, she would be buried. She knew that if she sent it, the priest would be right, and the state would be wrong, and she would have to choose.

Then she took out another sheet of paper and wrote a shorter report, in the flat language of the office, saying that the accident had been caused by a combination of factors, including the age of the equipment and the possibility of operator error; that the works had taken steps to improve safety; that the widow would receive a pension; and that the matter was closed. It took her eleven minutes. She signed it.

She put the first report in her drawer. She put the cable and the letter in an envelope. She put the envelope in the drawer. She locked the drawer. She put the second report in her outbox.

She went home and poured a glass of wine and sat at the table and looked at the wooden box on the shelf without opening it, and then at the Palace of Culture through the window, with its lights on and its spire floodlit, going up into a sky that had no more stars in it than a ceiling.

"Tadek," she said. "I'm sorry."

She drank the wine. She went to bed. She did not sleep.

In the church on Brzeska Street, Janusz knelt at the altar. The candle was burning. The statue of the Virgin was above him, her face pale in the dark. He prayed for Brunon Kowalik. He prayed for Wanda and the children. He prayed for the workers. He prayed for Halina. He prayed for himself. He prayed for the courage to be honest. He prayed for the grace to be faithful. He prayed for the strength to be afraid.

The snow began to fall. It fell on the church, on the courtyard, on the statue, on the city. It fell on the Palace of Culture, on the Vistula, on the rooftops. It fell on the village of Brzóza, on the church of St. Michael, on the grave of Tadeusz Kowal. It fell on the world.

Janusz finished his prayer and stood up and blew out the candle and went to bed, and lay for a while in the dark with his hands folded on his chest like a man laid out, and then turned over and slept, and dreamed of nothing at all.

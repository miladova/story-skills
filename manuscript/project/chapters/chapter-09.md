# Chapter 9: The Vigil

## Chapter Text

Otwock was a town of pines and peeling villas and sanatoriums for people with lungs, and in December the snow lay deep under the trees and the air smelled so sharply of resin that it made Halina's eyes water as she came down the station steps.

She had been there once before, in 1951, on a ministry errand to a rest home where a writer was being dried out. She remembered nothing of it. Now she walked up the road past a fence of larch posts and a clinic with a sign saying *Zakład Leczniczy*, and turned into a street of small wooden houses with carved verandas, and stopped by a gate with the paint gone.

The house was small and well kept. There were lace curtains in the windows, and a runner of homemade carpet on the step, and a wreath of dried leaves on the door, the kind people make in the villages out of what is left in the garden.

She stood at the gate for perhaps a minute. Snow fell on the shoulders of her coat and did not melt, it was so cold. Then she pushed the gate and went up the path and knocked.

The woman who opened the door was small and upright, in a gray wool dress and an apron, with her gray hair pinned back. She had the face of a woman who has spent thirteen years being careful with her face. She looked at Halina and did not know her.

"Yes?"

"Pani Berezowska."

"Yes."

"My name is Halina Sowa. I work — I worked — at the Ministry of Internal Affairs." She had rehearsed it for a week and it came out flat and stupid. "I have come about your husband."

The woman's hand went to the door frame. She did not step back. She did not invite her in. Behind her, from the depth of the house, came the sound of a radio playing a carol, and the smell of cabbage and mushrooms and something sweet.

"What about my husband?"

"May I come in? It will take some time."

"What about my husband?"

Halina looked at her. There was no way to do this in the snow at the gate, and there was no way to do it anywhere else either.

"I signed the paper that killed him," she said. "In Lublin, in February 1945. I gave his name to the security service. I have come to tell you so. I have not come to ask you for anything."

The woman stood in the doorway. Her face did not change at all. That was the worst of it: it did not change, as though she had been waiting so long in one position that a new blow simply confirmed the shape she was already in.

"Come in," she said, "before the neighbors see you crying, and then we will close the door."

Zofia Berezowska's kitchen had a stove with two rings, a table covered with a checked cloth, a shelf of crockery, and a clock with a brass pendulum. On the wall was a crucifix, a photograph of a man in a Home Army uniform with his cap under his arm, and a small picture of the Black Madonna cut from a magazine and framed.

Halina sat down where she was told. The woman put a glass of tea in front of her and sat down opposite with her hands folded.

"Say it," she said.

So Halina said it. She said it without excusing herself, because she had rehearsed that too. She said that in February 1945 the security service had arrested Janusz Wilga, and that Rydel had brought her a report to sign, and that she had known that Wilga was a seminarian and that he had been in the Home Army, and that Rydel had told her that if she gave him a name, Wilga would be released. She said that she had given him the name of Wiktor Berezowski, whom she knew as Bór, the commander, because the whole village knew the commander's name and she had thought it would cost him an interrogation and no more. She said that he had been held for six weeks and shot in a forest near Lublin, and that she had learned of it from a colleague, and that she had never asked a single question about it in thirteen years, and that she had known the widow's address since 1949 and had walked past the end of the road twice.

"And why did you not come before?" the woman said.

"There is no answer to that which will not make you angrier than you are."

"Try."

"Because I am a coward," Halina said. "Because I had made a life out of not being the person who did it. Because every year I did not come made the next year harder, and thirteen years of not coming is not thirteen years of forgetting. It is thirteen years of knowing." She put her hands around the glass. "And because on the thirtieth of November a priest stood up in a church in Praga and told his whole parish what he had done in a cell in 1943, and I understood that I was going to have to do the same, or stop calling myself a person."

The woman looked at her for a long time. The radio in the next room finished the carol and a man's voice began to read a list of figures about coal production.

"You are a Party member," she said at last. "You have a card."

"Since 1945. Since I was twenty-three."

"And now?"

"I mean to give it back."

"And they will let you?"

"They will take it. They will be delighted to take it. Then they will decide how much of me to break, and there is a chance they will decide that the cheapest thing is to let me disappear."

"And my husband? What do they say about my husband, in your office? That he was a fascist? That he was a reactionary? That he was an enemy of the people who died of a heart attack?"

"They say he was a heart attack," Halina said. "They said it for thirteen years and they said it so well that his daughter-in-law probably believes it. That is what they are good at. It is not the killing. It is the sentence afterward."

Zofia Berezowska got up. She went to the window and stood with her back to the room, and her shoulders were quite straight, and the light came through the lace curtain and made a pattern on her apron.

"Everybody said it was a heart attack," she said. "His mother said it. My mother said it. The priest at the funeral said it. They let me have the funeral, you know: a coffin, an open coffin. There was nothing in it that I was not allowed to see. They had washed him. His hands were folded. He had a mark under his jaw where the rope had been, or where he had fallen, or where something had been done, and nobody would say, and I put a lace handkerchief over it, and I said to my son, 'Your father died of his heart.'" Her voice did not break. "My son was nine. He is a man now. He works at the depot in Warsaw. He does not come home very often. When he comes, he stands in the doorway of this kitchen and he does not sit down. He says, 'What was my father really like, Mother?' and I say, 'He was a good man, he was a soldier, he liked to sing,' and he listens to me with the face of a man who has been lied to all his life and has decided not to mention it.

"Now you come and you tell me that a woman signed the paper. That is a thing I have been waiting for. Not for the name. For the paper." She turned around. "Do you understand? I have not been able to say to my son, 'They killed him on purpose,' because I did not know. I thought perhaps they did it for no reason at all, the way weather happens. And now I know it was done with a pen and a signature, and there is a person who did it, and I want to be sick, and I want to thank you, and I want you to leave my house and I want you to have another glass of tea."

"I will have another glass of tea," Halina said.

She had it. They sat for twenty minutes. The woman asked questions and Halina answered all of them: the office, the smell of it, the cigarette, the glass of water, the man standing behind the door with a pistol because there had been a man behind the door with a pistol. She named Rydel. She described the form. She described the tea and the flat, kind voice in which she had been told that the man would be interrogated, which she had chosen to understand as would be released.

At a quarter past one the front door opened and the son came in.

He was twenty-two, a broad young man with his father's face in a photograph taken before he was born, in a railwayman's coat and a good wool scarf, and he stopped short in the kitchen doorway with his cap in his hands.

"Mother. There is a woman in the front garden gate. She left a car on the road." He looked at Halina. "Who are you?"

"Sit down, Andrzej," his mother said.

"Who is she?"

"She is the woman who signed the paper."

The son stood in the doorway. He did not sit down. Halina looked at him, and thought, with a strange detachment, that she had never in her life seen anyone come so close to killing someone with his eyes.

"You," he said.

"I have told your mother everything," Halina said. "I will tell you the same, if you want it. It is not a long story and I will not dress it up. I signed a report, in Lublin, on the twentieth of February 1945, giving your father's name to the security service. It was typed, it was formally correct, and I was told by the officer who took it that it would put an end to the questioning of another man. Your father was arrested three days later."

"And you have lived," he said. "All this time. You have lived, in an apartment, on a salary paid by the state that killed him."

"Yes."

"And now you have come to tell us so that you can feel better."

"I have come to tell you so that you know. Whether I feel better is my business and it is not going in the ground with your father." She stood up, because she was not going to be seated while he stood over her. "You may put me out of this house. You may go to the militia and tell them that a Party official has confessed to a political killing in 1945; they will laugh at you, or they will file it, and either way it will cost you something. You may write to *Życie Warszawy*. Or you may do nothing at all, which is what most people do, and I would not blame you for it."

The son looked at his mother. And Zofia Berezowska, who had been careful with her face for thirteen years, said: "Sit down, Andrzej. In this house nobody gets up from a table on Christmas Eve."

"It is not Christmas Eve, Mother. It is the twenty-third."

"It is near enough. Sit down."

He sat down. He sat down slowly, on the chair nearest the door, and put his cap on his knee, and looked at the floor.

"Tell him," his mother said. "Start at the beginning and tell him."

So Halina told it again, from the beginning, in the kitchen, in the light that came through the runner blinds, while the clock ticked and the son sat with his cap on his knee and his jaw working, and did not once look up at her, and did not once tell her to stop.

When she had finished the son said, to the floor: "Did they hurt him?"

"They interrogated him for six weeks. That is the phrase that was used to me. Six weeks is not a phrase that means nothing."

"And he told them things."

"I don't know. I have wondered. He was a commander; he had men in the forest. He would have had things to tell."

"My father would not have told them anything."

"Then it was six weeks of nothing, and he died with nothing to give them, and it cost him six weeks of pain to do it. Or he gave them something, and they killed him anyway. You will never know which, and it is not a shame either way. It is only men in cells. Nobody is themselves in a cell." She heard how harsh her voice was and did not soften it. "I am sorry. That is the truth as I know it."

The son nodded, once, still looking at the floor.

Then he said: "I have to go and see to the car, Mother. The radiator is leaking and if I leave it, it will freeze." And he got up and went out, and stood in the front garden for a long time with his back to the house, in the snow, until his mother went out to him and put her hand on his arm, and Halina, from the kitchen window, watched the two of them standing in the garden among the pines, and took her hat and coat and let herself out of the back door and away.

She came back to Warsaw in the dark, on the slow train, and sat in a compartment with three soldiers who slept with their mouths open, and looked at the black window and at her own reflection in it.

On the morning of Christmas Eve she did two things, and she did the smaller one first.

The smaller one was at the ministry. She came in at eight, before the corridors filled, and went up to the fourth floor with a sealed envelope, and put it in the tray of the Office for Religious Affairs: a copy of the factory investigation she had written and not sent on the second of December — the frayed cable, the June letter, the safety commission that had met twice and minuted nothing, the crane that had been scheduled for repair in the spring and was still lifting in October. She had typed it herself on Sunday on Maryla's borrowed machine, because the ministry's typists read what they typed. At the bottom she had written: *Respectfully submitted, H. Sowa, formerly deputy head, Propaganda and Agitation Department. A copy of this document has been lodged with the district prosecutor's office and with the editors of* Życie Warszawy *and* Express Wieczorny*.* That last sentence was not true, and it was the sort of untruth that a woman who has spent thirteen years in the machine can tell without any difficulty at all: three copies in three envelopes, all of them in the same tray, and the office would spend a week trying to find out who else had one.

She walked out of the building without being stopped, because nobody stops a woman in a gray coat who looks as though she is late for something.

The larger one was at the Control Commission, where she handed in a signed statement, three pages long, which named the widow and the son, described the office in Lublin and the man behind the door with the pistol, and said that she would repeat every word of it at any investigation the Party wished to hold.

She waited in the corridor for two hours. Nobody came out to her. At noon a young man with a briefcase told her that the commission was in session and that her case would be heard on the second of January and that she might consider herself suspended from duty in the meantime, on full pay, which was not a kindness but a procedure.

Then she walked out of the building into the snow. It was the twenty-fourth of December, 1958. She was thirty-six years old, she had no family, she had no Party, she was shortly to have no job, and at six o'clock she was expected at Maryla Zawadzka's flat in Mokotów with a bottle of vodka, a poppy-seed cake, and a priest.

She was, she discovered to her own surprise, happy.

Maryla's building was on Puławska, a pre-war block with a courtyard and a wide staircase and the smell of a hundred families cooking at once. Halina climbed to the third floor carrying the cake in its tin and the bottle in a paper bag and found the door already open and Maryla in a red dress with flour on her hands.

"You are early," Maryla said, kissing her. "You are never early. Who are you and what have you done with Halina Sowa?"

"I am standing on the landing. Let me in."

The flat was full. There was a great deal of furniture and a great deal of food and a great deal of noise. Maryla's husband, Kazimierz, who was an engineer at the waterworks and had the calm voice of a man used to disasters, took her coat. Their daughter Ewa, who was in her third year of medicine and had her mother's round face and none of her mother's patience, was arguing in the kitchen with Maryla about the salt. Their son Marek, home on leave from the army in a sweater with a hole in the sleeve, was sitting on the arm of the sofa reading a newspaper and pretending to be idle.

And in the corner by the window, in a black suit with a face that had been freshly shaved and nicked in three places, sat Father Janusz Wilga, with a glass of tea in his hand and Pani Genowefa's instructions on his hat, which he had written on a piece of paper and put inside the band.

Maryla had done it deliberately. She had invited Wanda Kowalik and the three children as well; they were not there yet. She had put the two of them in the same room with twenty minutes to spare before the guests arrived, and she had then retreated to the kitchen with a bang of the door and left them to it.

Halina stood in the doorway with the cake in her hands.

"You are here," she said.

"On the third floor, at the top of the stairs, with a cake in my hat," Janusz said, standing up. "I have been here for eighteen minutes, and a small child from the first floor has asked me whether I can turn water into wine."

"Can you?"

"I told him that the trouble is not turning it into wine, it is getting people to stop."

Halina laughed. She put the cake on the sideboard and took off her coat and hung it in the hall and came back and sat down on the sofa beside him and then, a moment later, realizing that they were alone in the room and that Maryla had engineered it, she leaned over and kissed him on the cheek.

It was a small, plain, domestic kiss, delivered to a man with a cut on his chin, and it landed on him like a stone in still water.

"Halina—"

"Do not get theological about it. It is Christmas Eve." She stood up. "Give me the tea and tell me about Otwock."

"I was going to ask about Otwock."

"I asked first."

So he told her: about the cardinal and the sacristy and the knees and the arithmetic; about Prefect Bochenek, who had come to the rectory on the twenty-second in a good overcoat with a parcel of books, and had said, at the end of a half-hour, "When I am consecrated, will you pray for me? Not for the mitre. For the thing the mitre is supposed to be," and had then gone out into the snow and turned around on the step to say, "The books were mine when I was a student. They are better than you deserve, but you will need them in Wyszków"; about the parish council, who had met on the twenty-first and voted to petition the curia for him to stay and had been told, correctly, by the curia that the curia had already decided; about the youth club, closed on the twenty-second by the office, its table-tennis table and its books and its six bats taken away in a van while Pani Genowefa stood on the steps with her arms folded and watched them, and the boys stood along the wall of the presbytery, and nobody said anything, and one of them cried, and it was not the youngest.

Halina told him about Otwock: the pines and the lace curtain and the tea and the son standing in the doorway. She told it in the compressed way she told things, and when she came to the part where the mother had said *sit down, in this house nobody gets up from a table on Christmas Eve*, her voice did something that she had to stop and drink tea over.

"You have not done a small thing," Janusz said.

"I have done a very small thing, thirteen years too late, to one widow out of the many."

"That is not how it works."

"You are always telling me how it works."

"I am a priest. It is my trade." He set down his glass. "Halina. When I am gone to Wyszków, and you are in some office in Warsaw with a new job and a smaller salary, and the file is in Rydel's drawer and the year has turned — what is to become of you?"

"I have not the faintest idea," she said. "I have spent my whole life knowing exactly what would become of me. It is restful."

"Will you write to me?"

"If I have anything to say."

"That is not a yes."

"It is a yes with my conditions in it." She looked at him. "I will not write you letters about the weather, and I will not write you the other kind of letters, and you would not want either. I will write when there is something true to write. And you will write back the same way, and if nine months pass in silence, nobody is to be hurt about it."

"Agreed."

"Say it as if you mean it."

"I mean it," he said. "I will be very lonely in Wyszków."

"I know," she said. "So will I. There is a kind of comfort in the arithmetic."

The doorbell rang, and Maryla came out of the kitchen at a run, and Wanda Kowalik came in with snow on her headscarf and Ewa beside her carrying a covered dish, and Marek behind her carrying Tomek, who was seven and asleep on his feet. There was a great business of boots and coats and exclamations. The children, once awake, went about the flat at a speed that made the furniture seem to move. Kazimierz put the bottle on the sideboard and set out the glasses. And then Maryla took command, in the way of a woman who has been cooking for two days, and everyone was put where she wanted them and the lights were turned off.

The table was set for eleven. There was hay under the white cloth, a handful of it, in the old way, and a little pile of straw at the corner, and Maryla's children pretended to be embarrassed by it and were, in fact, pleased. There was carp in aspic and herring in oil and beetroot soup with mushroom dumplings and cabbage with mushrooms and pierogi and dried-fruit compote and a great deal of bread, and the extra place laid at the end of the table where nobody sat, with a candle beside it.

"Who is that for?" asked Tomek, who was seven, at the top of his voice.

"For the people who are not here," said Maryla, without a pause. "Now sit up straight and do not put your elbow in the compote."

There was a wafer at each place. Maryla rose and went around the table with hers in her hand and broke it with each person in turn, and said a thing to each, quietly, so that the others would not hear, and when she came to Halina she said, "No more of the old life. We are doing the new one now," and Halina, who had been expecting something sentimental, found that her eyes had gone hot and that she could not answer.

Then Kazimierz went around, and Wanda, and Ewa the medical student, and Marek the soldier, being extremely correct with the wafer and very shy.

And then Janusz stood up, and went around the table with the wafer in his hand, and came at last to Halina, and they stood facing each other over the hay and the twelve dishes and the candle for the absent.

"Halina," he said, very low. "I have nothing to forgive you for. That is the first thing. The second thing is that I have loved you for twenty years, and it is not a sin, and I am not going to pretend any more that it is; I am only going to be careful with it. The third thing—" and here his voice failed him, and he stopped, and looked at the wafer in his hand, and then said it anyway, "—the third thing is that I am glad, in a way that I cannot justify before God or man, that it is you who are standing here and not someone else."

"Janusz," she said. "Will you be all right in Wyszków?"

"I will be all right in Wyszków."

"Will you be all right in ten years?"

"I do not know."

"Neither do I," she said. "That is my whole answer to everything. I do not know." She reached out and broke the corner of his wafer and put it in her mouth. "But I am glad it is you I am not knowing it with."

He broke her wafer and ate a piece of it, and they sat down, and Maryla said grace, and everybody began to talk at once.

After supper they sang. Ewa played the piano, badly and with great enthusiasm, and Marek sang in a fine, embarrassed baritone, and Wanda wept a little at *Bóg się rodzi* and pretended that it was the onions, and Tomek climbed onto Janusz's knee and asked him whether he was really a priest, and when Janusz said he was, Tomek asked whether he had ever seen God, and Janusz said, "Not with my eyes," and Tomek considered this and said, "That's all right. I haven't either," and went to sleep against his shoulder.

At half past ten the children were carried down to the car that Kazimierz had borrowed for Wanda and the family, and Wanda kissed Halina on both cheeks and said, "You are different," and Halina said, "I am the same," and Wanda said, "That is what I mean."

At eleven, Halina and Janusz came out of the building together and stood under the archway in the snow.

The street was quiet. There was a light in a window across the road and a dog barking somewhere. It was snowing the way it snows on Christmas Eve, slowly, in large flakes, as though somebody had been given the job of decorating and had taken it seriously.

"Walk with me to the corner," she said.

They walked. The snow creaked under their feet. At the corner of Puławska and the side street, a black car was standing at the kerb with its engine running and a light in the cabin, and in the front seat sat a man in a gray coat with a cigarette, and in the back a girl of about twelve, in a red hat, looking out of the window at the lights.

Rydel got out. He stood by the car with his hand on the door, and he had not put on a hat, and the snow was settling on his hair.

"Halina," he said.

Janusz stopped and stood still and did not go forward or back.

Rydel looked at him and then at her. "I am not here for the office," he said. "I am taking Agnieszka to see the lights on the Old Town. She has been asking all evening. She is twelve, and she has decided that she believes in the baby Jesus, and she has asked me whether I do, and I have been driving around this city for an hour and a half trying to think of an answer." He looked at the car; the girl was still looking out of the window; he raised a hand to her, and she waved back with the enormous condescension of the young. "So when I saw you—" He stopped. "I read your statement, you know. The one you left with the commission this morning. It came to me at noon because my office is copied on everything that touches 1945, and I read it in this car while my daughter was buying chestnuts, and then I sat in the car for twenty minutes and did not send it up."

"I know," she said. "I have been waiting for you to send it up."

"I have not sent it up," Rydel said, "because it says nothing that the Party does not know, and because you wrote it in the third person and signed it as if you were someone else, and I do not know what to do with a document written by a woman who wants to be punished and has gone about it in a way that makes the Party look ridiculous." He lit another cigarette off the old one. "It will be heard on the second of January. I know what the recommendation will be. Suspension for one year, expulsion, no prosecution. The Party does not put its own in prison for 1945. The Party puts its own in a room and explains to them that they have disappointed the working class, and then they are given a pension and a very small flat and they are told that if they keep quiet they may keep both. That is the punishment. It is untidy but it is not nothing."

"I know," she said again.

"I have hated you for a week," Rydel said. "That is a strange thing to say to a woman I have known for thirteen years. I have hated you because you have taken away the only thing I had that made me sure that what I was doing was necessary. If you can be a murderer and a good woman, then what am I? If the man in the file can stand up in his own church and confess, then what is the file for?" He smoked. "I do not want an answer. I am not going to ask you for one. I am going to get back in this car and take my daughter to look at the lights."

"Rydel."

He stopped.

"Your daughter asked you whether you believe in the baby Jesus," Halina said. "Tell her you do not know. Do not tell her that she is too young to ask, and do not tell her that it is a fairy tale, and do not tell her what to believe. Tell her that you do not know, and that your father did not know, and that there are men who die for not knowing and men who die for knowing, and that she must decide which kind of person she is going to be. She is twelve. She can take it. She is your daughter."

Rydel looked at her. Then he looked at Janusz, over her shoulder, and something passed between the two men that Halina did not try to read.

"Good night, Father," Rydel said.

"Good night," said Janusz.

Rydel got into the car. The window came down and the girl leaned out and said, "Good night!" to them both with great cheerfulness, and the window went up again, and the car pulled away from the kerb and went off down Puławska toward the center of the city with the snow following it.

Halina and Janusz stood at the corner for a while.

"Well," she said at last.

"Yes."

"He will not send it up."

"No."

"He will not send it up," she said, "and in ten years he will be in a better flat, and his daughter will be a woman with a face like his, and I will be a person that the Party does not speak of. That is what the file comes to. It is not justice. It is not even a settlement. It is just that the machine goes on, and a few people get out of it, and a few people are pushed out, and it keeps grinding."

"You are out of it," Janusz said.

"Yes," she said. "I am out of it."

He walked her to the tram stop on Puławska. The tram came; it was almost empty. She got on and stood in the doorway and looked down at him, standing in the snow with his hat in his hand and no coat collar turned up because he never did turn it up.

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"It is Christmas."

"It is Christmas."

"Go home," she said. "Say your Mass. And when you go to Wyszków in February, go without all this. It is finished. It is over. The file is closed and you were never the man in it."

The tram bell rang. The doors began to close.

"I was the man in it," he said.

"Yes," she said. "So was I. That is why it is closed."

The doors shut. The tram pulled away. She stood in the doorway with her hand on the rail and watched him standing under the streetlamp until the tram turned the corner and the street and the light and the man were gone.

The second of January came. The hearing took forty minutes.

Halina Sowa was expelled from the Polish United Workers' Party for moral misconduct and conduct contrary to the principles of socialist morality, and suspended from her post, and granted, by a decision taken at four o'clock on the same afternoon by a deputy minister who had never met her, a disability allowance of six hundred and forty złoty a month, which was rather more than the average wage and rather less than she had been earning, and which was accompanied by a letter that said, in its second paragraph, that the decision was taken in recognition of her many years of service and in the expectation that she would continue to observe the discretion required of a former officer of the department.

She read the letter in the corridor. She read the second paragraph twice.

Then she folded it and put it in her handbag and went out into the snow, and walked across the city to Marszałkowska, and began, at last, to pack.

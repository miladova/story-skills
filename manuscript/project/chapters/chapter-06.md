# Chapter 6: The Bishop's Cross

## Chapter Text

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the second week of December, and it was not a letter. It was a telephone call from the curia at half past eight in the morning, and the voice on the line was the cardinal's secretary, who said only that His Eminence would receive Father Wilga at eleven, and that the matter was not to be discussed with anyone.

Janusz shaved badly and cut himself under the chin. He stood in the little bathroom off the rectory kitchen with a towel pressed to the cut and the water going cold in the basin, and he thought: *So it has come.* He had been expecting it ever since the sermon. He had expected it in the shape of a black car and two men in gray coats, and he had prepared himself for that. He had not prepared for the curia.

Pani Genowefa gave him tea and a piece of bread with dripping, and watched him eat it with the expression she reserved for the seriously ill.

"You are going to see the cardinal," she said. It was not a question. In the rectory, nothing was a secret for more than an hour.

"I am."

"Wear the good cassock."

"This is the good cassock."

"It has a shine on the elbows."

"Then I will sit with my arms down."

She sniffed, which was her way of saying that the Church had gone to the dogs and no one consulted her. Then she went to the cupboard in the hall and came back with a small jar of black cherry jam, which she had been saving, and put it on the table in front of him, and went out again before he could thank her.

He took the tram to Castle Square. It was a cold morning, the kind of cold that comes off the river and settles in the lungs, and the snow on the roofs of the Old Town was the color of an old coin. The tram was full of people going to work: women in felt boots with their heads wrapped in scarves, men in caps with their collars turned up, a boy with a satchel eating a roll. Nobody spoke. Somebody's breath smelled of onions; somebody's of plum brandy. A woman in a headscarf read a prayer book with her lips moving.

He got off at the bottom of Miodowa Street and walked up past the Primate's palace, and the cold came in at the tops of his shoes where the leather had given up.

The palace had been shelled in 1944 and rebuilt, and the rebuilding was still not finished: one wing was covered in scaffolding, and the courtyard smelled of lime and wet plaster. The secretary took his coat and hat and hung them on a peg that had not been there in the autumn. He was a young man with a smooth, careful face, the kind of face that has never had to make a decision, and he led the way up the stairs without a word.

The corridor was long and cold. The portraits of dead bishops hung in a row on the left-hand wall, and as he walked past them Janusz felt the peculiar sensation of being reviewed. There was the cardinal's door, with its black leather padding and its brass handle worn smooth by hands that had been at it for a hundred and fifty years. The secretary knocked.

"Come in."

The study was warm. That was the first thing: the warmth of it after the corridor. A tiled stove stood in the corner with its door open, and the fire inside it was the color of a poppy. There was a desk with a green lamp, and books stacked on the floor because the shelves were full, and on the wall a photograph of Pius XII with a black ribbon on the frame, and below it a map of Poland with the new western border drawn in red ink by somebody's hand. On the desk was a bowl of apples, a stack of typewritten pages, a magnifying glass, and a small notebook bound in black cloth, and beside the notebook a pen lying exactly parallel to the edge of the desk.

The cardinal was standing at the window with his back to the room. He was fifty-seven, but the years of the internment had settled into him like damp into a wall. He was tall, and he had the big, heavy hands of a country priest's son, and when he turned around Janusz saw again what he always saw: the eyes. There was nothing tired about the eyes.

"Father Wilga. Sit down."

"Eminence."

Janusz sat. The cardinal did not sit. He walked along the bookshelves with his hands behind his back, and he began, in the way of a man who has spent his whole life being listened to, in the middle of a thought.

"When I was a young priest," he said, "I was sent to Przasnysz. It was a small town, and there were not many educated people there, and so the young people came to me. I started a circle for them. We read, we sang, we had discussions. It was not a parish of intellectuals. It was a parish of farmers and mechanics and girls who worked in the post office. And the late countess came to me one day — she was the patroness of the parish, she had the right of presentation, an old woman with a face like a knife — and she said to me: 'Father, why do you trouble yourself with them? They will not read the books. They will not understand.'" He turned. "Do you know what I said?"

"No, Eminence."

"I said: 'They are not reading the books, Countess. The books are reading them.'"

The cardinal smiled, briefly, as at a memory he did not entirely trust, and then the smile went away.

"You have a youth club at the Transfiguration," he said.

"We have a table-tennis table and a library."

"Books reading them."

"I hope so."

"Twenty-two young people last month. Fifteen the month before. A boy with a criminal record and a girl whose father was in the AK and a young man who works at the Nowotki works and is not a fool, and who, I am told, argues with you about Marxism."

"Zbyszek," Janusz said. "He is a good boy. He is angry."

"Anger is not the worst thing in a young man. Apathy is worse. The Party has the angry ones. We have the apathetic ones. That is a bad exchange."

"It is changing."

"Yes. It is changing. That is why they are frightened of you."

The cardinal came and sat down at last, across the desk from Janusz, and folded his big hands on the blotter. On the desk, the notebook lay closed. Janusz looked at it and then away, because it would not do to be seen looking.

"In July," the cardinal said, "the security police went into Jasna Góra and broke open the archives. They took the files of the Institute. They beat the archivist. They beat the priests who were there. They went into my own office and pulled the drawers out onto the floor and took what they wanted and left the rest." He said it in a level voice, as a man reports weather. "You know this."

"I read the communiqué."

"You read what the bishops let you read. The rest you can imagine." He picked up an apple and turned it in his fingers and put it down again. "They did not find what they were looking for, because it was not there. What they were looking for was that we are in contact with the Vatican, which we are, and with the West, which we are, and that we are conspiring against the state, which we are not. They will not be able to prove it, so they will not stop looking. That is the situation. We are not being destroyed. We are being squeezed. Every month, a little less air."

"And the answer?"

"The answer is priests." He looked at Janusz. "The answer is men who can stand in a pulpit in Praga and say what you said in November and not be destroyed by it. The answer is men who can talk to a boy like your Zbyszek, and who can also be received at the ministry without being humiliated, and who can hold a parish together when the money runs out and the roof leaks and the state has taken the catechism out of the schools for the third time." He paused. "The answer is a bishop."

The stove crackled. Out in the corridor, someone dropped something metal and swore and then was silent.

"Eminence," Janusz said, "you cannot mean me."

"I mean exactly you. The Holy Father has been consulted. There is no obstacle. There will be an auxiliary see in this archdiocese, and when it is vacant I will present one name to the office, as I am permitted to do, and the name will be yours."

"I am thirty-six."

"Karol Wojtyła in Kraków is thirty-eight, and they consecrated him in September, and he is a poet and a philosopher and he climbs mountains. The state consented to him because they believe that a mystic cannot organize a strike. Do not let the age trouble you. Let the state."

"I am not Wojtyła."

"No. You are not." The cardinal's voice was dry. "Wojtyła is a gentle man. He will spend thirty years becoming a saint, and the state will not lay a finger on him, because he does not frighten them. You frighten them. That is not a defect in you. It is a fact about them."

"Then they will not consent."

"They will not consent." The cardinal said it flatly, without drama. "That is likely. They will refuse you, and they will appoint a man of their own, and I will have to accept it, or I will not, and then we will be in the newspapers for a month. That is the calculation I have to make, and I am asking you to help me make it."

"By consenting."

"By being willing. If the Church asks, you will say yes, and the asking and the answering will become a fact that the state must deal with. Or you will say no, and I will not ask again." The cardinal leaned back. "I am not offering you a mitre, Father Wilga. I am offering you a cross. I want that to be clear between us. The last bishop who was consecrated in this country was released from prison to be consecrated, and the one before him was not."

Janusz looked at the fire. He was aware of his own heartbeat, and of the shine on the elbows of his cassock, and of the smell of the coal.

"Eminence," he said, "there is something you should know."

"If it is about the war, I know."

Janusz looked up. For a moment he could not speak.

"I was in the AK," the cardinal said. "Do not look so surprised. Half the secular clergy of this province were in the AK. I know about the Lublin province, and I know that in 1943 you were arrested, and I know that men under interrogation do not behave like statues of the martyrs, because they are not made of stone, they are made of nerves and flesh and a mother's photograph." He picked up the apple again. "If you want to tell me the rest, tell me. If you do not want to tell me, do not. I am not your confessor. But do not think that I am asking you to be a bishop because I believe you have never broken. I am asking you because you have broken and have gone on."

Janusz sat with his hands in his lap. He could have said it then, in that warm room with the fire ticking behind its iron door. He could have said: I gave them the hideout. Three men were killed, and one of them was the boy I had loved like a brother, whose sister I have loved since I was sixteen. He could have said it, and the cardinal would have heard it, and perhaps the cardinal would have said something wise. But the cardinal had said he was not his confessor, and it was not a thing he could put into the air of that office and leave there, between the apples and the map.

"I did break," he said. "I have been to confession about it for fifteen years."

"Then it is between you and God, and between you and the man you have made your peace with." The cardinal's mouth was gentle and unsparing at once. "One thing. If you say yes, and they refuse you, they will dig. They will find whatever is in the files and they will put it in the papers, and they will not print it with any sympathy for a young man with a broken hand in a cell in Lublin. They will print it as the story of a traitor who was made a bishop by reactionaries. And every man in the Church who has ever been ashamed of his war will see his own face in yours." He set the apple down. "If you cannot bear that, say so now, and I will ask a better man. There may be one. I am not sure."

"I can bear it."

"Say it again."

"I can bear it, Eminence."

"Good." The cardinal stood, and Janusz stood, and the interview was over as quickly as it had begun. The cardinal held out his hand and Janusz kissed the ring, and the cardinal held his hand a moment longer than the ceremony required. His grip was warm and dry and very strong. "Pray about it for a week. Then come back. And Father Wilga — do not tell the newspapers."

"No, Eminence."

"Not even the friendly ones."

"No, Eminence."

The corridor was colder on the way out. In the antechamber a man was sitting on the bench with his hat on his knees: a priest of about forty-five, thick-set, well-fed, in an overcoat of good cloth. He had the look of a man who has been kept waiting exactly long enough to be angry about it and has decided not to be.

"Father Wilga," the man said, standing. "Bochenek. We met at the clergy conference in 1956. You gave a paper on the lay apostolate."

"I remember. You disagreed with it."

"I disagreed with the last page of it." Bochenek smiled, and there was something agreeable in the smile, which was worse than if there had not been. "I agreed with the first four, which nobody has ever forgiven me for."

"His Eminence is expecting you."

"He is." Bochenek did not move. He turned his hat slowly in his hands. "May I ask you something, Father? Not as a priest. As a man."

"You may ask."

"Do you think you are being used?"

Janusz looked at him. "By whom?"

"By all of us. That is the honest question, isn't it. The cardinal wishes to make a point to the state. The state wishes to make a point to the cardinal. There are six hundred priests in this archdiocese and most of them are tired and most of them are frightened and most of them have no idea what to do with a nineteen-year-old who has been told since he was six that the Church is a superstition for old women. And you are to be the answer to that, because you speak well and you were in a cell and you are young." Bochenek turned the hat again. "If they refuse you, the cardinal will be humiliated and you will be a parish priest in Praga for the rest of your life. If they accept you, you will have the mitre and the state will have a bishop who owes them nothing and can therefore be destroyed at any time with a clear conscience. Either way, someone else's point is being made."

"And you, Father? Whose point will you be making?"

Bochenek put his hat on. "I am a practical man," he said. "I have been a parish priest in Nowy Dwór for eleven years, and I have built a church there with money from the government, and I have a youth club and a kindergarten and a football team, and the Party secretary's wife comes to my Christmas Mass. I am not ashamed of it. When your bishop is in prison, someone must keep the parish open." He opened the door for himself. "Do not mistake my questions for hostility, Father Wilga. I would rather it were you than me. I am simply telling you that I have been in this antechamber for an hour, and I have counted the apples in the bowl, and I have prayed about it, and I have concluded that I am not the man." He nodded and went in.

Janusz walked down the stairs and out into the cold. The snow had begun again, small and hard, the kind that stings. He walked as far as the column of Sigismund and stopped and stood with his hands in his pockets, looking at the empty square, and he found that he could not feel anything except the cold. Not fear. Not exaltation. The cold, and beneath it a small flat certainty like a stone in the shoe: *They will refuse. And then what?*

He thought of Zbyszek, who had asked him in September whether the Church had any answer to the fact that the works canteen had no meat and the Party did. He thought of old Mieczysław, who had given Halina the cable strand. He thought of the boy he had been at seventeen, standing on the bridge at Brzóza, certain that the world was about to be remade and that he would be one of the men who remade it.

He walked all the way back to Praga. It took him an hour and a half and it was foolish, and by the time he came up Brzeska Street his feet were numb and his cassock was wet to the knees.

The church was cold. The boiler had failed again on Sunday, and the men from the coal depot had promised to come on Thursday, which meant Friday. The old women came anyway, in their coats, with their rosaries, and sat in the front pews and prayed, and the breath went out of them in small clouds. Pani Genowefa had put a paraffin stove in the porch, and it stank, and it was a mortal sin against the fire regulations, and it kept the worst of the frost off the holy water.

He went into the church and knelt for a while at the side altar, where the plaster Christ stood with his hand raised, and he said the office and then he stopped saying anything and simply knelt.

That was where Halina found him, an hour later, at three o'clock in the afternoon, when the light was already going.

She came down the aisle in her gray coat with the collar turned up. She had come from the ministry; he could tell by the shoes. She stood beside him and waited until he crossed himself and got up, and her face was white with the cold and with something else.

"The cardinal," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I am in the ministry and the cardinal is not a rumor." She sat down in the pew and pulled off her gloves, and her hands were red. "Rydel called me in this morning. They know about the see. They know the name. They had it before the curia was told, which means one of your priests has a tongue, or one of the secretaries does." She looked up at him. "They will refuse you, Janusz."

"I know."

"You do not know. You have guessed. I am telling you."

"Then tell me why."

"Because you spoke against them in a pulpit in November and the note of the sermon went to the department and then to the Central Committee. Because you are thirty-six and you can hold a room. Because they have Bochenek, and Bochenek is everything they want: a builder, a practical man, a man who has never said a word that could be printed on the front of *Trybuna Ludu* with a photograph of a bishop smiling." She said it all quickly, in the flat, competent voice she used in offices. "And because Rydel has a file."

Janusz sat down beside her. The cold of the pew came up through his cassock. Ahead of them, at the altar, the red lamp burned in its little cup of oil.

"Tell me about the file," he said.

"You know what is in it."

"Tell me what he will do with it."

"He will give you a choice." She was silent for a moment, and then she said, very evenly, in a voice from which every trace of tenderness had been removed, as a surgeon removes a growth: "The state will consent to your appointment if you join the Commission of Priests. You will sign a declaration. You will sit on the commission and you will say what it says about peace and the German danger and the Church's place in socialist society, and the file will stay closed, and you will be a bishop, and the Gestapo report will never see daylight. That is the offer."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the report goes to the newspapers. And my report goes to the Party." She folded her gloves in her lap and looked at them. "He did not say that. He did not have to. We are one file, Janusz. My signature is on the second page."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere at the back of the church a door opened and closed, and the draft came down the aisle and touched the back of his neck.

"Halina."

"Don't."

"I have to say it."

"Don't say it in a church."

"If not in a church, where?" He turned to her. "I have loved you since I was sixteen years old. I have loved you through the war and through the peace and through every year of it, and I have said Mass for you and buried people for you and I have had a photograph in my breviary for nineteen years. I know what I am. I know what I cannot be. I have not asked you for anything and I will not ask you for anything. But I will not stand in the snow outside a ministry and pretend that what I feel for you is Christian charity. It is not. It is love, and it has been love since the birch tree, and I am too old to lie about it."

Halina did not look at him. She looked at the altar, and her jaw was set, and her eyes were shining in the light of the lamp.

"Say something," he said.

"You are a priest."

"I know that. I have never once been permitted to forget it. Not by the seminary, not by the cardinal, not by myself at three in the morning. Say something else."

"I am a divorcée of the Party. I am thirty-six years old and I have killed a man and I have a card in my pocket that says I am trustworthy. If they put you on the front page with me beside you, they will not call it love. They will call it the proof." She turned to him at last. "You want me to say that I love you. I have said it. I said it in this room—" she stopped herself, and started again. "I said it in the parish hall. I have not unsaid it. But I will not have my love used as the instrument of your destruction. That is what it would be. That is what they are offering us. Not a life. A scandal."

"That is not what I asked for."

"What did you ask for?"

"To be told the truth by you. That is all. I am a priest; I am not a fool. I know what the world will do to a bishop who loves a woman. I have read the papers. I know what they did to Father K." He looked at the altar. "But I have also read the Gospel, and it says that the truth sets us free, and I have begun to think that it means something less comfortable than we tell the children. It means the truth costs. It means you cannot be free and keep your file."

"Then we are both bankrupt."

"Then we are both bankrupt." He almost smiled. "At least we are solvent in the same currency."

She laughed. It came out of her wrong, a wet, broken sound, and she put her hand over her mouth and looked horrified at herself.

"Genowefa is right about you," she said.

"What does Genowefa say?"

"That you are too clever for the parish and not clever enough for the world."

"That is the most accurate assessment of my character anyone has ever made."

She stood up. She put her gloves on, one finger at a time. The church was darkening around them, and the windows over the altar were turning from blue to black.

"Rydel wants you at Rakowiecka on Friday," she said. "Ten o'clock. He will make the offer in person. He likes to do it in person."

"Then I will go."

"Janusz."

"Yes."

"Whatever you decide," she said, "do not decide it for me. I have spent fifteen years being the reason men did things. I am tired of it."

She walked up the aisle. At the door she stopped, in the parallelogram of gray light, and crossed herself with the water from the stoup, quickly, the way she had done as a girl, before she remembered not to. Then she was gone.

He stayed in the church until the Angelus rang, and then until the altar boys came in to light the candles for Benediction, and then he went into the sacristy and took off his stole.

The telephone in the rectory rang at eleven that night.

It was Pani Genowefa, from the hall, and her voice was so changed that for a moment he did not know her.

"Father. They have taken Father Piotr."

"Taken him where?"

"The militia. Two men in a car. They came to the door and asked for him by name and he went with them. They would not say where. He asked if he could take his coat and they said no. Father, it is below zero and he went out in his cassock—"

"Genowefa." He was already reaching for his own coat. "Genowefa. Listen to me. Go and put the kettle on. And then go to your room and lock the door and do not open it for anyone but me."

"But Father—"

"Go. I am coming."

He put the receiver down. He stood for a second in the dark hall with his hand still on the telephone, and he thought, with a clarity that frightened him: *They have begun with the boy. They always begin with the boy.*

Then he took his hat and his scarf and went out into the snow.

# Chapter 10 — Iron and Velvet

*Warsaw — Late November 1943*

---

When Krystyna entered the white drawing room of Foksal 14, Viktor was standing by the Bechstein piano in his shirtsleeves, a glass of Danzig Goldwasser in his hand, watching the wet snow slap against the dark garden glass. He turned as the door closed, and his cold blue eyes went immediately from the snow on her sable collar to the flush on her cheekbones.

She let him feel the rapid beat of her pulse and made him believe he was its cause. She measured the risk by the certainty that Viktor had sensed the change in her skin temperature within five seconds of touching her throat, though the thought of her instructor's rule that the best hiding place for an emotion is directly inside its opposite returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of wet loden wool, anise liqueur, birch smoke, and the lingering warmth of her own flushed skin hung in the draft alongside the soft, wet slap of the first November snow against the garden windows until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. It was in small things like the tiny flecks of real gold leaf swirling at the bottom of Viktor's crystal glass that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond stepping straight into the circle of his arms and pressing her cold fingers against the warm linen of his shirtfront and a shivering, petulant intimacy that demanded his heat, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when inside her dressing-table drawer lay the flat tin of soft grafting wax she had prepared that morning for his Brühl Palace key.

The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

"You were out past the four-thirty curfew bell, Marion," he said. His tone was quiet—the dangerously quiet voice he used before reprimanding a regimental commander.

"The gates of the Saxon Garden were jammed with some bureaucratic parade, and then I stopped at the Europejski for marzipan," Marion said, tossing the white confectioner’s box onto the marquetry table and unfastening her damp loden cape. "Feel my hands, Viktor—it is snowing already, and it is only November. Does the sun ever shine in this wretched town?"

As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Through the lens of her cover identity, the silver button of his left tunic pocket gleaming on the chair ten feet away was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the draft stirred the white marble fireplace where the birch logs blazed, the white confectioner's box of marzipan lying unopened on the card table remained fixed in place, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. With the pliant, heat-seeking compliance of a woman coming in from the storm governing the motion of leaning her forehead against his collarbone with a low, contented sigh to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. "You smell of snow and pine," Viktor murmured, his fingers tangling in the damp curls at her nape. She did not allow herself to forget that while his hands moved down her spine to warm her, her eyes measured the exact distance to his discarded tunic on the library chair, though every detail of the Brühl Palace key with its double-bitted ward that Phillip had described in the conservatory remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that the master safe key never left the buttoned inner pocket of his field-gray tunic except when he slept.

She held out her bare hands to him deliberately. If you hide from a predator, he hunts; if you walk straight into his reach, he smells only what you offer him.

Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Past the iron railings of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. Measuring the room one last time, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Viktor set his Goldwasser glass down on the piano lid and took both her hands in his. Her fingers were cold from the walk down Nowy Świat, yet as he drew her closer and laid his palm against the side of her neck, his brow furrowed. "Your hands are ice, Liebchen, and your throat is burning like a stove. Who did you see at the Europejski?"

She answered the silence with unbuttoning the top three buttons of her wool jacket as she walked backward into the bedroom, letting a slow, challenging smile over her shoulder that dared him to follow serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. He followed her across the threshold and kicked the bedroom door shut behind them. Between the doorway and the threshold of the master bedroom where the porcelain stove glowed, Viktor's field-gray tunic draped over the back of her father's leather armchair marked the half-lit interval, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She let her attention brush past the heavy iron poker resting beside the porcelain stove, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. There was no escaping the conclusion that something in his animal instinct knew that she was brighter, more electric tonight than she had been in weeks—and it drove his desire to a pitch of frenzy, while the contrast with the feather-light brush of Phillip's mouth in the dark glasshouse ninety minutes ago made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the distant rumble of an Ostbahn freight train crossing the Vistula bridge at the same moment she breathed in hot porcelain tiles, French perfume, and the gathering storm of Viktor's appetite steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray.

"A dozen fat majors’ wives in hideous Berlin hats, and a laundry maid who scorched my slip," she answered without missing a beat, tilting her head so her damp gold hair brushed his wrist. "Unless you are jealous of the pastry cook, Viktor, take me in by the fire and warm me."

* * *

In the firelit bedroom of Foksal 14, Viktor came at her like a man trying to stamp his own seal into hot wax before another hand could touch it. He did not let her finish unbuttoning her wool suit; he stripped the jacket and silk blouse from her shoulders, his rough palms sliding over her bare ribs and breasts as if checking every inch of her flesh for foreign contraband.

She lay beneath his crushing weight in the dark, her heart intact behind its wall of iron. Nothing about the red imprint of his thumb at the hollow of her throat escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the harsh, ragged rhythm of Viktor's breath and the rattle of snow against the glass while the room held the aroma of sweat, anise liqueur, scorching linen, and bruised skin that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Across the crimson-draped Empire bed in the firelit master bedroom, her silk blouse lying torn at the cuff beside the footboard rested in the quiet shadow, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. As she completed arching her spine off the mattress and locking her heels behind his scarred back, she drew the raw, scorching abandon of Marion Herschlag in the dark around herself like silk, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. Her training insisted that every stroke of his body had been an interrogation without words, and her body had answered without a single stammer, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the sacred, silent chamber behind her ribs where Phillip's touch remained untouchable by any violence.

In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. Against the harsh, possessive storm of Viktor's appetite, every bruise on her flesh bought three uninterrupted minutes with his dispatch case. In the amber half-light, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

"Look at me," he growled, catching her chin in his hand as he backed her against the footboard of the Empire bed. "Not at the fire. At me."

Marion looked straight into his hard blue eyes, her pupils wide in the orange glow of the stove, and reached down to unbuckle his belt herself. It was the cruelest part of her war: less than two hours after Phillip Brandt had cradled her face like sacred porcelain and whispered her childhood name in the Saxon Garden, she had to open her body to the harsh, bruising storm of the man who commanded him.

Where Viktor sought proof of his dominion in the heat of her skin, she let him believe he conquered the woman while she harvested the officer. Within the mirror-lined rooms of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. With her eyes clear and level, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Within the bedside table where the silver water carafe stood, her gaze returned briefly to Viktor's heavy arm flung across her naked waist as sleep claimed him, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. A subtle trace of cooling sweat, birch ash, and the faint camphor of the winter blankets hung in the draft alongside the wind howling down the Vistula valley and rattling the garden shutters that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. At one-fifteen in the morning, she slid inch by inch from beneath his arm onto the cold parquet. Against the quiet of the room, the steady rise and fall of the gray chest hair above his Verdun scar stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the absolute stillness of a hunter waiting for the quarry's eyes to close with the quiet motion of waiting twenty full minutes after his breathing slowed before moving a single muscle of her leg, she gave the room only what it expected to see, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she did not even need chloral tonight; his own exertion had drugged him deeper than any draft.

Viktor lifted her onto the crimson damask counterpane, his heavy, scarred body coming down between her thighs with an almost savage possessiveness. He hooked one arm beneath her knee, pushing her leg high against her chest so he could drive deeper, his mouth grinding against hers while his free hand gripped the base of her throat—measuring her pulse, her breath, the involuntary arch of her spine. Krystyna locked her jaw against the ache in her soul and gave him the dark, feral fire he demanded: raking her nails across the Verdun scar on his ribs, meeting the relentless, battering rhythm of his hips with a fierce heat that was half survival and half desperate self-forgetting.

Behind the double-bolted doors of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Beneath the ruthless weight of the older man's scarred hands, passion became a blade that drained his vigilance before the clock struck three. With unhurried grace, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

"Say it," Viktor rasped against her ear as the bedstead hammered against the wall, his fingers tightening in her damp gold hair until tears stung her eyes. "Tell me what this city is to you."

"Nothing!" she gasped against his scarred shoulder, her body clenching around him as she drove him over the precipice. "Nothing—only a barracks until you take me back to Paris—"

With a hoarse, triumphant groan, Viktor buried himself to the hilt and collapsed heavy and shuddering across her breasts.

* * *

Barefoot in the dark between the bedroom and the library, Krystyna did not turn on a single lamp. The orange glow from the porcelain stove grate was enough. From the bottom drawer of her dressing table she took a flat silver pomade tin filled with softened yellow beeswax mixed with a drop of olive oil and lampblack so it would leave no shiny grease on metal.

Under the magnifying lens of her compact, the wax mold was flawless to atenth of a millimeter. Beside the darkened threshold between the bedroom and the Foksal library, the outline of the flat silver pomade tin containing lampblack-tinted beeswax marked the stillness, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She was acutely aware of the steady, deep snore of Viktor from the bedroom and the hiss of snow on the windowpane and the faint drift of benzine solvent, beeswax, cold leather, and old book bindings pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that all fourteen asymmetric wards of the Brühl Palace master safe key were now preserved in the palm of her hand, though every detail of watching Tadeusz Kowalski cut a precision brass key by micrometer in the back of the Chmielna clock shop remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, pressing the double-bitted steel blade straight down into the wax without rocking the shaft reinforced the quiet authority of the surgical steadiness of fingers trained never to slip in the dark, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, if a single microscopic crumb of wax remained in the notches of Viktor's key, the safe lock at the Brühl Palace would stick tomorrow morning, whatever price it demanded.

In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Beyond the shuttered windows of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Steeling her resolve once more, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

In the library doorway, Viktor’s field-gray tunic hung over the back of her father’s leather armchair. Moving without a sound, she unbuttoned the left inner breast pocket and drew out the flat leather key-wallet.

Inside, on a separate steel split-ring from his apartment keys, hung the three-inch double-bitted Chubb-and-Zeiss safe key stamped *OKW-WARSCHAU 01*. It was the master key to the personal wall safe in his Brühl Palace office.

From the sentry-guarded gates of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Letting the silence work for her, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Her senses caught the chime of the Holy Cross clock muffled by the falling snow outside at the same moment she breathed in benzine evaporating from silk and the dry heat of the porcelain stove an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. She slid back into bed beside Viktor before the clock struck two, her hands warm and her work done. The sight of the white blanket of fresh snow covering the courtyard cobbles below brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted wiping each tooth of the steel key twice with benzine-dampened silk before slipping the wallet home and the familiar armor of silent, invisible precision in the dark to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that by noon tomorrow Wanda would carry this silver pomade tin out of the Hotel Europejski in a basket of linen, while the contrast with the winter of 1937 when the snow had drifted four feet high against the gates of Foksal 14 made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when once Tadeusz cast the duplicate key in hardened nickel-brass, she and Phillip would have the key to the W-Netz cipher vault.

The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

She opened the silver tin on the edge of the marquetry table, pressed the flat side of the key straight down into the darkened beeswax until the teeth bottomed against the tin, lifted it cleanly by the bow, and turned it over to press the reverse side into the opposite half of the cake. Under the faint orange light from the stove, the negative impression of all fourteen asymmetric notches was as crisp as a die stamp.

She wiped the steel blade of the key with a drop of benzine on silk so not a molecule of wax remained in the grooves, buttoned the key-wallet back into his tunic pocket, and locked the silver tin inside her jewel case.

Outside the tall windows, the first blizzard of the Warsaw winter was turning Ulica Foksal white.

* * *

By Friday morning, Warsaw lay buried under six inches of fresh white snow that muffled the clatter of the German patrol wagons and turned the blackened ruins along Nowy Świat into jagged teeth of charcoal against a bridal sheet. At eleven o’clock, while Viktor was inspecting the Ostbahn marshaling yards at Praga across the frozen Vistula, Krystyna walked to the clock shop on Ulica Chmielna herself—carrying Viktor’s gold hunting-case pocket watch, whose crystal she had deliberately cracked against the edge of the marble washstand.

She held fast to the recognition that passing the wax mold openly as 'silver polishing paste' three feet in front of a German railway sergeant was the safest way to defeat surveillance, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her father's old hunting watch that had stopped at 4:15 on the morning the Gestapo arrested him. From where she stood in the snow-bright window of the Chmielna 22 clock shop, Viktor's cracked gold pocket watch and the silver pomade tin on the green baize counter was just visible, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. The railway sergeant even held the shop door open for her and saluted as she stepped back out into the snow. In the presence of the three subtle pencil dots Tadeusz placed beneath the date on the receipt, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the jingle of the shop door bell and the stamping of snow from the railway sergeant's boots while the room held the aroma of wet wool greatcoats, melting snow on sawdust, and watchmaker's oil as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. Before the night was over, by four o'clock Saturday afternoon she would hold the hardened nickel-brass duplicate of Key 01 in her glove, and she intended to be ready.

Along the damp paving stones of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. While the night deepened outside, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

In the narrow shop, while a German railway sergeant waited near the door having a strap fitted, Krystyna leaned over the counter and spoke to Tadeusz in crisp, imperious German: "Oberst von Harenberg requires a new crystal fitted to this hunting watch before Monday, Herr Kowalski. And here is the silver polishing paste Hanke promised you for the case."

The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. In that narrow pause between beats, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

She set the cracked gold pocket watch and the silver pomade tin openly on the green baize pad under the railway sergeant’s nose.

In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. As the shadows lengthened, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond walking briskly through the snow with her chin tucked into her silver-fox collar and the bright, winter-flushed composure of a woman enjoying the first snow, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of crisp frost, coal smoke, and the clean cold air blowing off the Vistula hung in the draft alongside the jingle of sleigh bells from a droshky passing a column of gray Wehrmacht trucks sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. Everything was converging on Monday night under the roof of the Brühl Palace. Against the muted backdrop of the snow-covered perspective of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the rows of bare linden trees heavy with fresh November snow drew her eye, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. No illusion could soften the fact that on Monday evening Governor Fischer was hosting a chamber concert on the upper floor of the Brühl Palace—directly above Viktor's ground-floor office, though the thought of skating down the frozen paths of Ujazdowski Park when she was sixteen with Phillip holding her mittened hand returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when Monday night's chamber concert would give her the twenty-minute window she needed on the ground floor of the Brühl Palace.

"Jawohl, gnädige Frau," Tadeusz said, bowing his cropped gray head as his three-fingered left hand closed over the silver tin. "I will have the crystal fitted and the case polished by tomorrow afternoon at four."

As he wrote the receipt, his pencil point tapped three times against the bottom line of the ticket—their code that the Home Army’s High Command had accepted her voucher for Hauptmann Phillip Brandt, and that the duplicate safe key would be ready in twenty-four hours.

* * *

On Sunday afternoon at five, the German Governor of the Warsaw District hosted a formal reception at the Hotel Bristol to celebrate the completion of the autumn eastern supply quota. Marion stood near the tall crystal mirrors of the Malinowa Hall in a gown of dark garnet velvet, holding a glass of champagne in her right hand and her long suede gloves in her left.

Where the shadow fell across the mirrored Malinowa Hall of the Hotel Bristol, her long garnet suede gloves resting on the marble console ledge remained half-hidden, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Viktor stood three feet away talking to a brigadier and never even glanced down at the gloves. It was in small things like the slight, imperceptible pressure of Phillip's thumb confirming the brass key was secure that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. With cold clarity she reckoned that in less than two seconds under the noses of four Generalstab officers, the duplicate key to Viktor's Brühl Palace safe had passed into Phillip's palm, though every detail of the sleight-of-hand card tricks Phillip used to perform on rainy afternoons at Konstancin remained as sharp as cut glass. With the haughty superiority of a woman barely tolerating a junior officer's courtesy governing the motion of taking the empty suede gloves back from Phillip with a cool, unsmiling nod of dismissal to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the buzz of a hundred German officers and wives talking beneath the crystal sconces and the faint drift of champagne, Floor wax, and the warm suede of the glove passing from his hand to hers until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing.

The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Inside the middle finger of her left suede glove rested the hardened nickel-brass duplicate of Viktor’s Brühl Palace safe key, delivered by Wanda in a hatbox two hours earlier.

With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Anchored by her silent oath, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

When Hauptmann Phillip Brandt approached with three other Abteilung Ic officers to pay their formal respects to Oberst von Harenberg, Marion shifted her champagne glass to her left hand and casually dropped her folded suede gloves onto the marble ledge of the mirror console beside Phillip’s elbow.

"You dropped your glove, Fräulein Herschlag," Phillip said in cool, formal German, stooping to pick up the suede gloves. In the fraction of a second his fingers closed around the soft leather, his thumb slid the nickel-brass key out of the middle finger into his own palm before handing the empty gloves back to her with a stiff bow.

"Thank you, Herr Hauptmann," Marion said coldly, remembering their staged hostility for any watching eyes. "Try not to spill coffee on them this time."

# Chapter 8 — The Streetcar in the Rain

*Warsaw — November 1943*

---

Ten yards south of the Holy Cross church on Nowy Świat, the morning quiet shattered without warning. Two shrill blasts of a police whistle cut through the rain; from both ends of the block—at Ulica Świętokrzyska and Ulica Ordynacka—gray-green trucks of the Ordnungspolizei and the SS screeched to a halt across the tram tracks, blocking the street from curb to curb.

She knew beyond doubt that old Hanna had just spoken her real name aloud six feet in front of an approaching SS corporal with a Schmeisser submachine gun, though every detail of Hanna braiding her hair by the nursery fire on Ulica Foksal and singing lullabies from Mazovia remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the shadow fell across the cordoned pavement of Nowy Świat during the street roundup, the canvas-topped police trucks blocking the tram tracks at both ends of the street remained half-hidden, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Every drop of blood in Krystyna's body seemed to turn to lead as the SS corporal spun toward them. Against the quiet of the room, Hanna's trembling,arthritic hands reaching out instinctively toward Krystyna's sleeve stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the savage barking of German Shepherd dogs, police whistles, and women screaming and the faint drift of wet wool, diesel exhaust, crushed potatoes in the gutter, and the metallic stench of raw terror that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Whatever happened next, she knew that if the SS trooper understood Polish or saw a flicker of recognition between them, both she and Hanna would be thrown into the Pawiak truck within thirty seconds.

Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

*"Łapanka!"* someone screamed in Polish—a street roundup.

Instantly the pavement exploded into panic. Men and women dropped their string shopping bags and bolted for courtyards, only to be clubbed back by schupos in long green greatcoats swinging submachine guns and rubber truncheons. A red streetcar ground to a halt with its iron brakes shrieking; soldiers with barking Alsatians surrounded the rear Polish platform, dragging young men and girls out into the gutter by their collars while orders barked through megaphones: *"Alle stehenbleiben! Ausweise vorzeigen!"*

She answered the silence with stepping back sharply and snapping at the old woman in loud, furious Strasbourg German, letting the outraged arrogance of a high-ranking German officer's mistress touched by a street peasant serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the crunch of the SS corporal's boots as he cocked his submachine gun and shouted 'Was ist hier los?' at the same moment she breathed in wet dog fur, gun oil, and the crushed stems of the white chrysanthemums pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. "Keep your hands off my coat, you crazy old woman!" Krystyna cried in German, shoving a twenty-zloty note at the ground so Hanna had to stoop. Under the dim lamp near the wet gutter beside the stalled red streetcar, Hanna's spilled potatoes rolling between the jackboots of the Ordnungspolizei cast a narrow shade, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She measured the risk by the certainty that there was only one way on earth to save old Hanna's life in front of a German soldier: she had to strike the old woman away as a mad beggar, while the contrast with Hanna weeping at the gate of Foksal 14 when the Gestapo took Count Ignacy away made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where if she showed a single grain of mercy, the corporal would check Hanna's papers and haul her into the van for accosting a German lady.

Outside the tall casements of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Measuring the room one last time, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Krystyna was caught fifty paces from the corner of Ulica Foksal, her arms full of white chrysanthemums. Around her, terrified Polish women pressed against the shuttered shopfronts as two SS troopers in coal-scuttle helmets strode down the line, shoving people toward the open tailgate of a canvas-topped Buda truck—the trucks that went straight to Pawiak Prison or the Auschwitz transports.

Then, three paces to her left, an old woman in a faded brown shawl dropped her basket of potatoes on the wet stones, looked straight at Krystyna’s face beneath the Parisian hat, and gasped aloud in Polish: *"Matko Boska—Panienka Krystyna!"*

It was Hanna Nowakowa, the sixty-five-year-old housekeeper who had raised her from the cradle at Foksal 14.

* * *

The SS corporal saw the silver Wehrmacht eagle on Marion’s lapel and the purple seal of the Oberfeldkommandantur inside her leather pass-folder; he snapped his heels together instantly. "Verzeihung, gnädige Frau! Did this Polish hag trouble you?" He raised the steel butt of his Schmeisser above Hanna’s bowed white head.

By the threshold of the wet pavement between the SS corporal and the stalled streetcar, the steel butt of the Schmeisser submachine gun poised six inches above old Hanna's shawl stood out in sharp relief, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Inside her glove, Krystyna's fingernails had cut four red crescents into her own palm. The sight of the look of dawning awe and forgiveness in old Hanna's tear-filled eyes as she fled brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She did not allow herself to forget that by invoking the cleanliness of her Parisian furs, she had stopped the rifle butt in mid-air without arousing a shadow of suspicion, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Hanna nursing her through three nights of fever when she was ten years old. As she completed lifting her skirt daintily with two fingers as if afraid the wet pavement would soil her hem, she drew petulant, aristocratic fury at the inconvenience of a street roundup around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the roar of Viktor's Mercedes engine cutting through the police whistles while the room held the aroma of wet silver fox fur, diesel smoke, and the cold sweat trickling down Krystyna's spine an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place.

Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Along the quiet curbstones of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

"No!" Marion snapped, stepping directly between the rifle butt and the kneeling old woman with an expression of fastidious disgust. "Do not spatter blood on my furs, Corporal—I have just had this coat cleaned in Paris! She is sixty years old and half-blind; she mistook me for her niece and begged for bread. Let her pick up her potatoes and get out of my sight before I complain to Oberst von Harenberg that I cannot walk two blocks without tripping over your cordons!"

Down the block, the iron gates of Ulica Foksal swung open, and Viktor’s black Mercedes staff car—summoned by the Foksal gate sentry at the sound of the whistles—rolled through the police barrier with its Wehrmacht pennants fluttering on the fenders. Viktor himself stepped out onto the running board, his hand on his holster, his cold blue eyes sweeping the roundup.

A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Down the wind-scoured length of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. With unhurried grace, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

"Your SS friends are animals, Viktor," she said sharply. "That idiot nearly ruined a twelve-thousand-franc coat in the gutter." There was no escaping the conclusion that Viktor had watched the final ten seconds of the exchange from the running board of his car, though the thought of her training instructor's voice: 'After a close call, let your anger do the talking before the enemy asks a question' returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of dry wool, cigar smoke, and the sudden, suffocating silence of the car interior hung in the draft alongside the thud of the heavy car door closing out the cries from the street as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. In the presence of Viktor's cold blue eyes studying the pulse in her throat as he sat beside her, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the flushed irritation of a pampered woman whose morning walk had been ruined with the quiet motion of throwing her hat back against the headrest with an exasperated sigh as the car backed into Ulica Foksal, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that if Viktor thought she was rattled by pity for the Poles, he would begin to wonder about her nerves.

At the sight of a full Wehrmacht Colonel and his staff car, the SS corporal lowered his submachine gun immediately and kicked Hanna’s basket toward the alley. "Mach, dass du fortkommst, Alte!" he barked, waving the old woman through the cordon into the side passage before saluting Viktor.

Hanna scrambled to her feet, clutching her shawl to her mouth, her wet blue eyes meeting Krystyna’s for one last, shattered instant of understanding before she vanished down the alley. Krystyna stepped across the gutter and let Viktor hand her into the Mercedes.

* * *

Viktor watched the police trucks pull away down Nowy Świat loaded with silent, standing figures behind their wire grilles, then turned his gaze back to Marion as the Mercedes rolled into the courtyard of Foksal 14. "Ten Wehrmacht soldiers were ambushed near Wawer last night," he said coldly. "For every German soldier shot in Warsaw, the SS shoots a hundred hostages from the streetcars. That is the arithmetic of this city, Marion. And I told you not to walk Nowy Świat without Hanke."

To anyone else, the four tiny half-moon cuts bleeding silently inside her right kid glove might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Past the edge of the first-floor marble landing of Foksal 14 beneath the tall stair window, Viktor's ungloved hand tilting her chin into the gray November light waited in the gloom, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Without haste, holding his blue gaze with cool, petulant defiance until his thumb released her chin reinforced the quiet authority of the unbroken porcelain surface of Marion Herschlag, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Only when the heavy front door bolted shut behind Viktor did she peel off the blood-spotted glove. Her training insisted that a single tear in her eye on this staircase would have betrayed fifty years of history in this house, though every detail of sliding down this same marble banister when she was seven while Hanna scolded her from the hall below remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, she had survived the first lethal test of walking Warsaw's streets: recognition to her face in the middle of an SS roundup, whatever price it demanded.

Above the slated mansard roofs of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. With her eyes clear and level, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

"I was only going as far as the Europejski to fetch my mended slips and buy flowers for your drawing room," she replied, stepping out of the car into the shelter of the porte-cochère. "What did that old woman want? She grabbed at my hem babbling some Slavic nonsense."

"They all babble when they see the trucks," Viktor said, though as they walked up the marble stairs of Foksal 14 together, he caught her chin in his hand on the first landing and turned her face toward the tall stair window. The gray November light fell full across her eyes. "You are pale, Liebchen."

Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

She trusted throwing the blood-spotted white kid glove into the heart of the birch fire and watching the leather curl into black ash and the familiar armor of the solitary, silent composure of a woman burning the evidence of her own heart to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She watched the white leather glove blacken in the flames until nothing remained but gray soot. Set against the white drawing room beside the Bechstein piano, her mother's cut-crystal vase filled with cold water and white chrysanthemums held a quiet, insistent weight, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. It was in small things like the dispatch slip from Tadeusz still tucked safely inside the waistband of her skirt that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She forced herself to accept that old Hanna would never betray her—Hanna would light a candle at Saint Cross and guard the secret to her grave—but Warsaw's streets were a tightrope over an abyss, while the contrast with Hanna's rough, warm hand making the sign of the cross over Krystyna's forehead on the night she fled Poland made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the crackle of the birch fire in the grate and the distant clang of a streetcar on Nowy Świat at the same moment she breathed in chrysanthemum stems, woodsmoke, and the faint scent of her own blood on the inside of her glove until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing.

"I am furious," she corrected him smoothly, meeting his stare without a blink. "And freezing. Send Hanke back to the Brühl Palace if you must go, Viktor, and come home early for dinner."

He studied her for a heartbeat longer, then kissed her hard on the mouth, satisfied that her pallor was merely temper and cold. Yet when the front doors closed behind him ten minutes later and Krystyna stood alone in the white drawing room putting the chrysanthemums in her mother’s crystal vase, she had to brace both hands on the edge of the Bechstein piano until the violent trembling in her knees subsided.

* * *

In the locked dressing room behind her bedroom, Krystyna unfolded the tiny onion-skin slip Wanda had hidden inside the cardboard backing of her mended slip. Tadeusz’s cipher was brief and urgent:

She tested the secret panel behind the armoire once; the oiled hinges still opened without a sound into the dark garden stair. Through the lens of her cover identity, the hidden wooden latch behind the shoe shelves of the dressing-room armoire was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the rush of cold tap water in the porcelain washbasin carrying the black ash down the drain while the room held the aroma of burned paper, sulphur match-heads, and the cold draft from the hidden service shaft behind the armoire filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Not three paces from her mother's dressing table in the locked inner chamber of Foksal 14, the burning scrap of onion-skin cipher paper held between her thumb and forefinger lay in the amber half-light, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the choreography of the room, watching the last gray flake of ash swirl down the brass drainpipe flowed seamlessly into the cold, tormented resolve of a woman caught between her country's executioners and the man she loved, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that Tadeusz's liquidation squads were already measuring Phillip Brandt for a bullet on his walk from the Brühl Palace, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Phillip at twenty-two swimming across the Jeziorka river with the sun on his wet shoulders.

A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Near the sandbagged corners of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Letting the silence work for her, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

*London and KG AK confirm: Fall Weichsel films invaluable. New priority: we need the exact location and cipher schedule of Oberfeldkommandantur 'W-Netz' underground cable repeater hub before November 15. Also report on Hauptmann P. Brandt (Abt. Ic): is he vulnerable to compromise or elimination?*

She read the last line three times beneath the shaded lamp of her mother’s dressing table: *is he vulnerable to compromise or elimination?*

Framed by the geometry of the tall Venetian window of the drawing room as November dusk fell, the black iron railings of the Foksal courtyard glistening in the rain seemed almost innocuous, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. A subtle trace of wet soot, coal smoke, and the approaching winter night hung in the draft alongside the six o'clock curfew siren wailing across the rooftops from the Prudential building steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She sat down on the bench of her mother's Bechstein and played a single, silent chord on the wood without pressing the keys. She let her attention brush past the brass pendulum of the mantel clock swinging steadily toward seven, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond drawing the heavy crimson blackout curtains across the garden glass with both hands and the quiet, disciplined readiness of an operative on the eve of a decisive contact, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when whatever Phillip had become, she could not let him see how completely his voice still owned her soul.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Under the starless canopy above the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. As the shadows lengthened, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

If Tadeusz and the *Kedyw* liquidation squads believed Hauptmann Phillip Brandt was an effective hunter of the Polish Underground at the Brühl Palace, they would shoot him down on the street the way they had shot Bürckl and Kretschmann in September—unless she proved to them, or to herself, what lay beneath his uniform.

She held a match to the corner of the onion-skin slip, let it burn down to her fingertips over the porcelain washbasin, and flushed the black ash away with cold tap water.

Tomorrow was Thursday. Four o’clock at the glass conservatory behind the Saxon Garden fountain.

* * *

When Viktor returned at eight that evening, he brought his locked calfskin dispatch case and two junior colonels from the Ostbahn engineering corps for brandy in the library. Marion played the hostess to perfection—pouring their Courvoisier, laughing at their dry Prussian jokes about Russian mud, and lingering by the library door just long enough as she refilled the cigar humidor to hear Colonel Reinecke mention the new *W-Netz* armored telephone cable.

She smiled warmly as the two colonels bowed over her hand at the door, having already stolen the heart of their conversation. Where the draft stirred the half-open oak doorway of the Foksal 14 library, the silver water pitcher and crystal cigar humidor on her tray remained fixed in place, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She was acutely aware of the low rumble of the three colonels' voices and the hiss of rain in the chimney and the faint drift of Havana cigar smoke, aged Courvoisier, and wet wool tunics that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She held fast to the recognition that the W-Netz cable repeater vault lay beneath the old sorting cellar on Ulica Nowogrodzka, and the two schedules were held by Viktor and Phillip, though every detail of walking past the gray stone telegraph building on Ulica Nowogrodzka on her way to school remained as sharp as cut glass. With the invisible, graceful attentiveness of a hostess who understood not a word of military engineering governing the motion of setting the silver pitcher silently beside the brandy decanter without looking toward the maps on the desk to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Whatever happened next, she knew that if she could get the Nowogrodzka schedule from either Viktor's safe or Phillip's hands, the Home Army could sever German communications across the Vistula at the hour of revolt.

"The repeater vault is thirty feet beneath the old postal sorting cellar on Ulica Nowogrodzka," Reinecke said, tapping his cigar ash into a silver tray. "Even a five-hundred-kilogram bomb cannot crack that concrete. And Harenberg holds one of the only two master inspection schedules in his safe at the Brühl Palace."

"And the other?" asked the second colonel.

Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. In that narrow pause between beats, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

Her senses caught the distant chime of the Holy Cross church bells striking two through the fog at the same moment she breathed in warm linen, cigar ash, and the cold November rain beating against the shutters that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Outside in the rain, Warsaw waited in the dark, and Krystyna counted the hours until four. Against the quiet of the room, the faint tick of her gold Patek Philippe wristwatch on the marble nightstand stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with lying utterly still beneath the weight of Viktor's arm while her eyes traced the dark molding overhead, letting the steady, unhurried breathing of a woman feigning deep sleep serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. No illusion could soften the fact that between the man sleeping beside her and the man waiting tomorrow in the Saxon Garden lay the entire fate of the Warsaw Underground, while the contrast with the quiet river at Konstancin where the water ran deep and dark beneath the alder roots made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where fifteen hours remained until four o'clock at the Saxon Garden conservatory.

"In Abteilung Ic's counter-sabotage vault," Viktor said, raising his glass. "Under Hauptmann Brandt's lock and key."

Standing outside the half-open library door with the silver water pitcher in her hands, Krystyna felt the pieces of the board click into place with chilling symmetry. Two copies of the *W-Netz* cable schedule in all of Warsaw: one in Viktor’s safe, and one in Phillip Brandt’s.

After the two colonels took their leave at eleven, she went to bed with Viktor without a murmur of complaint, lying awake long after his heavy arm pinned her waist in sleep, counting every chime of the Holy Cross clock through the rainy dark until Thursday afternoon arrived.

* * *

At two o'clock in the morning after the roundup on Nowy Świat, the rain turned to sleet against the tall windows of Foksal 14. Unable to sleep while the memory of old Hanna’s terrified blue eyes burned behind her eyelids, Krystyna slipped out of the bedroom in her wool dressing gown and stood in the dark drawing room beside her mother’s Bechstein piano.

With cold clarity she reckoned that touching that half-inch scratch in the wood reminded her that she was not an impostor in this house—Viktor was, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of twelve-year-old Janek laughing under the piano while their mother pretended not to see him hiding from his Latin tutor. Across the dark drawing room of Foksal 14 at two in the morning, the tiny wooden cross scratched by her dead brother beneath the bass frame of the Bechstein rested in the quiet shadow, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She let him lead her back to the Empire bed, her fingertips still tingling from the wood her brother had carved. She observed Viktor's bare, scarred chest dimly visible in the light from the bedroom stove with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the rattle of sleet against the garden panes and the creak of the parquet under Viktor's bare feet while the room held the aroma of cold beeswax, wet sleet, and the warm wool of her dressing gown pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that in fourteen hours she would walk into the Saxon Garden conservatory and stand face to face with Phillip Brandt.

Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Within the lamplit precincts of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Anchored by her silent oath, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

She did not dare light a candle. Instead, she reached beneath the bass end of the keyboard frame—where the wood was unvarnished oak—and ran her fingertips over a tiny, rough-carved cross half an inch long. Her brother Janek had scratched it there with his penknife in 1931 when he was twelve years old.

The wood was still there. The cross was still there.

"Why are you standing in the dark, Marion?" Viktor’s voice came quietly from the bedroom doorway behind her.

She turned without a start, letting the wool robe fall slightly open at her throat. "The wind in the chimney sounded like voices," she said in German, walking back toward his tall silhouette in the doorway. "This house is full of drafts, Viktor. Come back to bed and hold me until it stops."

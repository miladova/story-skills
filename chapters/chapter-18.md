# Chapter 18 — The Seamstress's Needle

*Warsaw — Late April 1944*

---

Half-carrying, half-dragging the bleeding nineteen-year-old girl through the narrow iron fire-door onto the first-floor ash balcony overlooking the service alley of Ulica Ossolińskich, Krystyna tore her own silk scarf from her throat and packed it hard into the bullet hole beneath Wanda’s left collarbone.

Beneath the shadowed trees of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Keeping her pulse steady, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

She trusted wiping a smear of Wanda's blood from her own wrist with a handful of wet alley snow before sliding into the passenger seat and the familiar armor of the icy, unblinking composure of Marion Herschlag seated beside a Wehrmacht captain to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She opened her gold compact as the car rolled toward the roadblock and calmly powdered her nose. Set against the narrow service alley behind the Hotel Europejski kitchens, the open luggage boot of Phillip's gray Wehrmacht Adler sedan lined with field blankets held a quiet, insistent weight, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. To anyone else, the faint crimson drop of blood on the cuff of Phillip's right gauntlet which he tucked swiftly beneath his thigh might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She held fast to the recognition that if Krystyna had been seen on the second floor by the SD gunman in the steam, only the steam and her hat veil had hidden her face, while the contrast with carrying Wanda on her back across the shallow ford of the Jeziorka when the girl had sprained her ankle at twelve made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the clang of the iron fire-ladder and police whistles shrilling from the front entrance of the hotel at the same moment she breathed in coal dust, kitchen grease, hot exhaust, and Wanda's fresh blood on Krystyna's silk scarf sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

Down the long colonnades of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Steeling her resolve once more, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Down in the narrow cobbled alley behind the hotel kitchens, a gray Wehrmacht Adler staff sedan with *Abteilung Ic* fender pennants backed swiftly between the coal bins. Hauptmann Phillip Brandt jumped out of the driver’s seat, popped open the rear luggage boot of the sedan, and caught Wanda in his arms as Krystyna lowered her down the iron ladder.

Through the drifting night mist of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. With her eyes clear and level, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

"Inside the boot, Sarna," Phillip whispered in Polish, laying the half-conscious girl onto the folded army blankets in the trunk and pressing a roll of field gauze into her hand. "Bite the blanket and don't make a sound when they stop us at the square. Krystyna—get in the passenger seat!"

Across the cobble-paved expanse of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Measuring the room one last time, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

"Sonderauftrag Abteilung Ic," Phillip barked in clipped Prussian tones. "Oberst von Harenberg's household! Clear the barrier!" It was in small things like the SS Rottenführer's eyes flicking from Phillip's captain's bars to Marion's Parisian furs that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the low rumble of the Adler's four-cylinder engine and the muffled thud of Wanda's shoulder against the trunk wall behind the seat while the room held the aroma of wet asphalt, petrol, and the copper smell of blood seeping through the rear seat-back until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. Not three paces from the SS Feldgendarmerie checkpoint at the corner of Plac Piłsudskiego, the red-and-white metal halt disk held up by an SS Rottenführer in a steel gorget lay in the amber half-light, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. In the choreography of the room, leaning across the gearshift with an arrogant, impatient glare through the windshield at the SS sentry flowed seamlessly into the haughty fury of Oberst von Harenberg's mistress delayed on her way to the dressmaker, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. No illusion could soften the fact that if Wanda groaned once in the trunk or if a single drop of blood dripped from the rear bumper onto the cobbles, all three of them would be shot against the Saxon wall, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the street roundup on Nowy Świat where Viktor's rank had saved old Hanna.

Beside the stone balustrades of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Through the narrow archways of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. In the hush that followed, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Past the shuttered shopfronts of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. As the minute hand crept on, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Before the Gestapo cordon at the front of the Hotel Europejski could seal the rear alley, Phillip slammed the trunk lid down to the first catch so air could enter, vaulted behind the wheel, and swung the Adler out onto Plac Piłsudskiego—straight into the teeth of a mobile SS Feldgendarmerie roadblock set up across the corner of Ulica Królewska.

* * *

The SS Rottenführer saluted the *Abteilung Ic* metal disk and the furious blonde woman in the passenger seat, waving his squad back from the wooden barrier. "Passieren, Herr Hauptmann!"

A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. As the minute hand crept on, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

Framed by the geometry of the walled orchard courtyard of the Sisters of Charity convent in Żoliborz, the unconscious, blood-soaked figure of nineteen-year-old Wanda lifted from the trunk of the Wehrmacht car seemed almost innocuous, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. A subtle trace of ether, carbolic acid, damp orchard earth, and blood hung in the draft alongside the rustle of the nuns' starched white habits and the urgent whispered Polish orders filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Stitch by stitch, Krystyna drew the girl's flesh together with catgut thread until the bleeding stopped. Through the lens of her cover identity, Phillip standing guard by the basement door with his Luger drawn while holding the operating lamp higher for her stitches was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond stripping off her silver-fox coat and rolling her silk sleeves above the elbow to scrub in the enamel basin and the steady, blood-cooled hands of a wartime nurse who had seen a hundred shrapnel wounds, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when if Wanda survived the night in the convent cellar, Tadeusz could move her to a country estate near Milanówek in a vegetable cart.

A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. While the night deepened outside, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Phillip did not speed—speed drew eyes. He drove at a measured forty kilometers an hour northward past the blackened rubble wall of the former Ghetto and across the Gdański Viaduct into the quiet, tree-lined streets of Żoliborz. Only when he turned the Adler into the walled orchard courtyard behind the Convent of the Sisters of Charity on Ulica Głogowa and shut off the engine did Krystyna let out the breath she had been holding since Plac Piłsudskiego.

A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. Letting the silence work for her, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Two nuns in white cornettes—both sworn nurses of the Home Army’s underground medical service—ran out from the laundry door with a stretcher as Phillip unlocked the boot. Wanda was unconscious now, her lips blue-white, the silk scarf soaked dark crimson, yet her fingers were still locked around the bundle of cipher slips.

A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. With unhurried grace, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

"She will live," Sister Urszula said softly, blessing them both as they stepped back out into the courtyard. Where the draft stirred the whitewashed basement recovery cell beneath the convent chapel, Wanda's bandaged shoulder resting quiet beneath a rough convent blanket remained fixed in place, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She was acutely aware of the faint singing of the nuns at Sext prayers filtering down through the stone vault overhead and the faint drift of iodoform gauze, beeswax candles, and the clean scent of scrubbed skin steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She knew beyond doubt that looking across Wanda's narrow iron cot at Phillip—whose field-gray sleeves were stained with the Polish girl's blood—she knew no boundary of nation existed between them anymore, though every detail of Wanda whispering 'May God keep you both until a free Poland' in the attic on Christmas Eve remained as sharp as cut glass. With silent, overwhelming tenderness as their eyes met over the basin of red-tinted water governing the motion of washing the blood from Phillip's wrists and hands in the enamel basin with a warm linen cloth to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Whatever happened next, she knew that they had thirty minutes to return to the city center before Kessler finished searching the Hotel Europejski.

The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Without a backward glance, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. Drawing a slow breath, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

In the basement surgery beneath the convent chapel, while Sister Urszula administered ether and probed for the 9mm slug lodged against the girl’s scapula, Krystyna rolled up the sleeves of her Parisian blouse, scrubbed her hands in carbolic, and held the retractors under the operating lamp—and then took the curved surgical needle herself to stitch the torn muscle closed, just as Wanda’s mother had once taught them both to stitch fine linen.

* * *

Outside the convent gates, the sky over the Vistula had turned the color of bruised gunmetal, and the first heavy drops of an April cloudburst were striking the dust. Phillip had washed the rubber mat of the Adler’s trunk at the convent pump and burned the bloodstained blanket in the boiler furnace.

Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Within the mirror-lined rooms of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Her senses caught the sudden deafening crack of spring thunder and the drumming of rain on the steel roof at the same moment she breathed in hospital ether clinging to her silk blouse, wet wool, and ozone that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Without a word, Phillip swung the wheel of the Adler through the side service gate of the old Royal Botanical Gardens. Nothing about the wrought-iron gates of Łazienki Park looming ahead through the curtain of rain on Aleje Ujazdowskie escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with unbuttoning the throat of her ether-dampened blouse as she looked out at the torrential downpour, letting the practical, rapid calculation of an operative erasing the trail serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She measured the risk by the certainty that Viktor knew the smell of field-hospital ether and iodoform better than any man—he had lain in a Verdun dressing station for six weeks, while the contrast with the smallest sensory oversight—a whiff of carbolic on silk—undoing the bravest rescue made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where they needed thirty minutes in a sheltered place with rain and green earth to wash the hospital scent from their clothes and skin.

Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Past the iron railings of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

"Kessler will have every laundry girl at the Europejski lined up in the courtyard by now," Phillip said as they drove south from Żoliborz through the rising rain. "When they find 'Sarna' missing and blood on the back stairs, he will know the courier escaped—but he will never guess she rode through his cordon in the boot of an Abteilung Ic car."

Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Above the slated mansard roofs of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. In that narrow pause between beats, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

Krystyna looked down at her hands in her lap. Beneath the edge of her manicured nails lingered a microscopic crescent of carbolic and Wanda’s blood; her silk blouse beneath the silver-fox coat was damp with sweat and ether.

Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Outside the tall casements of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Along the darkened tramlines of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Beneath the looming cornice of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. With practiced serenity, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the long shadow cast by the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. In the amber half-light, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

"I cannot walk into Foksal 14 smelling of hospital ether, Phillip," she said quietly as lightning forked across the river ahead of them. "Viktor is back from Wawer by now. If he kisses my neck and smells ether and carbolic, he will ask which hospital I have been in."

* * *

At three o'clock that afternoon, after returning from Łazienki Park, Hauptmann Phillip Brandt walked directly into Sturmbannführer Kessler’s office on the ground floor of the Brühl Palace and laid a signed counter-intelligence file on his desk.

Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. From the sentry-guarded gates of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. In the amber half-light, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

She did not allow herself to forget that by pinning the Wawer derailment and Wanda's trail onto a railway clerk who was already dead in the morgue, Phillip closed the investigation with a tidy corpse, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the meticulous bureaucratic thoroughness that always satisfied German headquarters. Across Sturmbannführer Kessler's ground-floor office in the Brühl Palace, the falsified Bahnschutz patrol report tying the Wawer leak to a dead railway clerk rested in the quiet shadow, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Reluctantly, Kessler stamped 'ABGESCHLOSSEN'—Closed—across the Wawer file, though his eyes remained cold. Against the quiet of the room, Kessler's thin forefinger tracing the dead clerk's photograph on the dossier stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the ticking of the wall regulator and the rustle of typed dossier sheets while the room held the aroma of chlorine ink, black uniform wool, and the faint scent of spring rain that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that Kessler could not dispute a signed Bahnschutz shooting report without accusing the Wehrmacht railway police of incompetence.

In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Behind the double-bolted doors of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

"We have identified the Hotel Europejski laundry girl 'Sarna' and her accomplice, Herr Sturmbannführer," Phillip said in crisp, businesslike German. "Her name was Janina Kowalska, recruited by a Polish railway clerk in Praga named Wróbel—who was shot dead by our Bahnschutz patrol two nights ago while cutting a signal wire. Both are now out of the game."

Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. Near the sandbagged corners of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Anchored by her silent oath, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

Kessler looked up from the file, his gin-pale eyes narrowing as he studied Phillip’s calm, unblinking face and the dry, spotless cuffs of his spare tunic. "And the girl who was shot on the laundry stairs this morning, Hauptmann Brandt? Where did she go?"

Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Down the wind-scoured length of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. As the shadows lengthened, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Beyond the guarded courtyard of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Without haste, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Within the high-walled gardens of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Keeping her pulse steady, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

"Into the Vistula sewer outfall at Karowa, according to the blood trail our dogs followed at noon," Phillip lied smoothly, handing Kessler a signed patrol report from a Wehrmacht dog-handler who owed Phillip his transfer off the Russian front. "No one survives a chest wound in sixty feet of freezing river water."

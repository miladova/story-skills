# Chapter 14 — The Blood on the Snow

*Warsaw — January 1944*

---

The Christmas Eve raid was the worst humiliation the Warsaw Gestapo had suffered in two years. Forty-two trucks had rolled out of Aleja Szucha at eleven o'clock in twenty-degree frost; forty-two doors had been battered down from Żoliborz to Ulica Chmielna; and not a single Home Army commander, radio transmitter, or printing press had been found. At Chmielna 22, Tadeusz Kowalski had vanished two hours before the locks were shot off, leaving only forty broken cuckoo clocks ticking on the walls.

Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Along the damp paving stones of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Drawing a slow breath, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

A subtle trace of roast pork, caraway, cigar smoke, and the icy draft from Viktor's greatcoat hung in the draft alongside the hiss of the porcelain stove and the scrape of snow-shovels in the courtyard below until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. "Then let Hauptmann Brandt deal with him," Marion said coolly. "You told me Brandt was your sharpest bloodhound." It was in small things like the deep vertical furrow between Viktor's silver eyebrows that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining calm, domestic disdain for the SD's incompetence with the quiet motion of stitching a thread of green silk through her embroidery hoop without a single pause in her needle, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Her training insisted that Kessler had connected the Paris Siedlce leak with the Warsaw Christmas Eve leak—and realized the common denominator had traveled from the Majestic to the Brühl Palace, though the thought of Kessler's gin-pale eyes watching her across the table at Lapérouse and at the Gare de l'Est returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that only two people on earth had traveled from Viktor's inner circle in Paris to the Brühl Palace: Viktor himself, and Marion Herschlag—while Phillip held the Warsaw end of the file.

Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Down the long colonnades of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. In the amber half-light, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

By the second week of January 1944, Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler had moved a permanent SD counter-espionage detachment into the ground floor of the Brühl Palace, directly across the hall from Abteilung Ic.

Along the quiet curbstones of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. With her eyes clear and level, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

On the morning of January tenth, Viktor stormed into the Foksal 14 dining room for luncheon with his jaw clenched like iron. "That Saxon ferret Kessler has Governor Fischer’s ear," Viktor said, throwing his gloves onto the sideboard. "Because forty Polish rats smelled his trucks coming on Christmas Eve, he claims the leak is inside the Brühl Palace—just as he claimed the Siedlce train leak was at the Hôtel Majestic in Paris!"

The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Outside the tall casements of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. With unhurried grace, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

Marion, seated by the porcelain stove with her embroidery frame, looked up with a cool frown. "Did you remind him that half of Warsaw works as janitors and telephone girls in that building, Viktor?"

Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beyond the guarded courtyard of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

"He has already arrested twelve Polish janitors," Viktor said grimly. "And now he is auditing the four officers who handled the December twenty-fourth liaison file—including Major Kleist and Hauptmann Brandt."

* * *

That afternoon, Krystyna knew that she and Phillip had to sever any possible thread of perceived sympathy between them in front of both Viktor and Kessler before Kessler thought to check the pre-war property registries of Konstancin-Jeziorna.

She forced herself to accept that by staging an ugly, arrogant scene and provoking Phillip into a sharp retort, she made it psychologically impossible for Kessler to imagine them as accomplices, though every detail of the theatrical arguments she and Phillip used to stage as teenagers at Konstancin to fool her governess remained as sharp as cut glass. Beside the palm-lined officers' tearoom of the Hotel Europejski, the outline of the overturned porcelain coffee cup spreading a dark brown stain across the damask cloth marked the stillness, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. "Leave us, Hauptmann," Marion said coldly, not even looking at Phillip as he clicked his heels and strode out of the tearoom. Through the lens of her cover identity, Kessler's thin fingers steepled beneath his chin as he watched the hostility between them was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the sharp gasp of nearby tables and the slam of Viktor's palm on the wood and the faint drift of scalding ersatz coffee, wet sable fur, and the acrid tension of a public humiliation filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, the scalding coffee had burned Phillip's wrist, yet his cold, angry reply had been pitch-perfect, whatever price it demanded.

Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Beneath the shadowed trees of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. With practiced serenity, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Her chance came at five o'clock in the tearoom of the Hotel Europejski, where Viktor had asked her to meet him after his afternoon staff conference. When she walked into the palm-lined tearoom in her sable hat and coat, Viktor was sitting at the corner table with Sturmbannführer Kessler and Hauptmann Phillip Brandt.

Under the hooded blue lamps of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Steeling her resolve once more, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

As Phillip rose to pull out her chair, Marion deliberately let her elbow strike his forearm, spilling half a cup of hot black coffee across the white tablecloth and onto the cuff of Phillip’s field-gray tunic.

She trusted stirring two lumps of beet sugar into her fresh teacup with a brisk, annoyed clink of the spoon and the familiar armor of lingering irritation at an insolent subordinate to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the low murmur of conversation resuming around the potted palms at the same moment she breathed in Turkish cigarettes, lemon tea, and the cold satisfaction beneath her ribs steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. "Your Captain Brandt has entirely too high an opinion of himself, Viktor," she complained, and watched Kessler smile thinly. In the corner of the corner table of the Europejski tearoom after Phillip's exit, she noticed a fresh white linen napkin laid over the coffee stain by a trembling Polish waiter, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that Kessler was a man who believed in vanity and resentment; seeing her humiliate Phillip in public drove a wedge straight through his hypothesis, while the contrast with Tadeusz's new safe house on Ulica Wspólna where the clock shop equipment had been moved after Christmas made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when now Kessler would look at Major Kleist rather than suspecting a conspiracy between Viktor's haughty Frenchwoman and his proud Prussian captain.

The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Above the slated mansard roofs of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Letting the silence work for her, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

"Do watch where you are standing, Herr Hauptmann!" Marion snapped in loud, cutting Strasbourg German, snatching her sable hem back as half the officers in the tearoom turned to look. "Have you forgotten how to hold a chair since you left Berlin, or did five years among the Poles ruin your manners entirely?"

Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Down the wind-scoured length of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

Phillip stood rigid as a post, dark red spots of color rising in his cheeks as he dabbed the scalding coffee from his wrist with a napkin. "My apologies, Fräulein Herschlag," he said in a voice clipped with cold, formal hostility. "I did not realize you required as much room as a supply wagon."

Viktor’s hand slammed down on the table. "Mind your tongue, Brandt! Apologize to Fräulein Herschlag properly, or take yourself back to your desk!"

Across the stained tablecloth, Sturmbannführer Kessler’s pale eyes darted from Marion’s haughty, curling lip to the muscle jumping in Hauptmann Brandt’s jaw—and for the first time since Christmas Eve, a look of slight uncertainty crossed the SD officer’s face.

* * *

Yet Kessler was not finished. Two days later, a young AK courier boy named Marek—captured with an uncoded street pass near the Gdański Station—was taken to the interrogation cellars at Aleja Szucha. And on the morning of January fifteenth, Kessler sent a polite invitation to Oberst von Harenberg and Hauptmann Brandt to attend an inspection of the new counter-sabotage archive at the Gestapo headquarters on Aleja Szucha—adding that since Fräulein Herschlag was driving with the Colonel to the Belweder park afterward, she was welcome to wait in the Upstairs Officers’ Casino over a glass of Malaga.

Across the darkened perspective of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Keeping her pulse steady, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

From where she stood in the wide, snow-swept boulevard of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the modern modernist facade of Aleja Szucha 25 looming behind its barbed-wire barriers was just visible, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She stepped out of the Mercedes onto the pavement of Aleja Szucha with her head high and death pinned in her hat. Nothing about the black-uniformed SS post sentries checking even a Wehrmacht Colonel's papers at the gate escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that eighteen-year-old Marek—who knew Wanda's description at the Hotel Europejski—was being held in the basement 'Tram' corridor of Aleja Szucha right now, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the morning in June 1940 when her mother had stood outside these same barbed-wire barriers begging for news of Count Ignacy. In the choreography of the room, touching the silk band of her fur toque where the two glass cyanide capsules were sewn flowed seamlessly into the bright, chatterbox indifference of a woman stopping at an office building before a sleigh ride, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the crunch of chained tires on packed snow and the bark of SS guard dogs at the barrier while the room held the aroma of frozen exhaust, wet wool, and the invisible stench of fear that hung over the entire police district that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise.

The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Past the iron railings of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. While the night deepened outside, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Krystyna knew what Aleja Szucha was. Her father had been taken from its cellars to the execution pits at Palmiry; her cousin had come out of its basement with broken fingers.

Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Near the sandbagged corners of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. In that narrow pause between beats, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

"You do not need to come inside, Marion," Viktor told her in the car as they drove down the wide, frozen boulevard of Aleje Ujazdowskie toward the modern yellow-stone ministry building at Aleja Szucha 25.

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Beside the stone balustrades of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. As the shadows lengthened, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

"Nonsense, Viktor," she said, adjusting her fur toque in the car mirror—where, pinned inside the silk lining of her hatband, rested two glass potassium-cyanide ampoules Tadeusz had passed her through Wanda that morning. "If I sit in a cold car for an hour while you argue with Kessler, my feet will freeze. I shall have a glass of wine in their casino and wait for you."

* * *

Inside the stark, terrazzo-floored corridors of Aleja Szucha 25, the air was overheated and smelled of floor wax, sauerkraut from the SS canteen, and something faint and metallic rising from the basement stairs. While Viktor was called into Oberführer Hahn’s corner office on the second floor, Kessler escorted Marion and Hauptmann Brandt toward the ground-floor officers’ reading room.

The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Within the mirror-lined rooms of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. As the minute hand crept on, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

In less than three seconds she stood upright again, dusting snow from her hem just as Kessler turned around. She held fast to the recognition that Kessler had paused by the chained boy on purpose to see whether either she or Phillip flinched at a beaten Polish courier, though the thought of her cousin's broken hands when he was released from this same cellar in 1940 returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of carbolic disinfectant, dried blood, sauerkraut, and hot iron hung in the draft alongside the clatter of tin mugs in the guardroom and the muffled scream of a prisoner two doors below that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Against the quiet of the room, the flicker of recognition and infinite gratitude in Marek's one open eye as his bloody fingers closed over the glass bead stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond stooping swiftly behind the screen of Phillip's broad field-gray back as if adjusting the clasp of her fur-trimmed galoshes and the cold, unhesitating mercy of a soldier giving a comrade the only escape left, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when if Marek bit the glass ampoule before they dragged him back downstairs, Wanda and the entire Warsaw network would remain safe.

Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Behind the double-bolted doors of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

As they passed the head of the basement stair—where prisoners sat in rows on wooden benches facing the wall in the basement corridor the Poles called "the Tram"—two bloody-shirted Gestapo technicians came up the steps carrying a tray of empty tin tea mugs. Behind them, chained to the iron radiator at the top of the cellar landing awaiting his second round of questioning, slumped eighteen-year-old Marek. One of his eyes was swollen shut; his fingernails were black with dried blood.

Kessler paused deliberately at the stairhead, watching Marion’s face in the harsh fluorescent light. "A partisan courier from the Gdański rail yard, Fräulein Herschlag," Kessler murmured smoothly. "He claims he does not know who gave him his pass—Wait here a moment while I tell the orderly to bring fresh tea before we resume."

Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Across the cobble-paved expanse of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. Anchored by her silent oath, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

She observed Kessler's furious, livid face as the cellar sergeant whispered in his ear that the prisoner was dead of poison with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the shadow fell across the ground-floor officers' reading room of Aleja Szucha, a crystal glass of dark Spanish Malaga wine poured for her by an SS orderly remained half-hidden, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. With a look of mild distaste at the noisy disorder of a police building governing the motion of taking a slow sip of the dark sweet wine while Kessler hurried to the door at the sound of the commotion to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. "How careless of your guards, Herr Sturmbannführer," Viktor remarked dryly as he rejoined them in the hall. "Come, Marion." No illusion could soften the fact that the shouting on the cellar stairs meant eighteen-year-old Marek had crushed the glass capsule between his teeth before the interrogators touched him again, though every detail of the prayer for the dying she had whispered for Stefan Wilk in Paris remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that Marek had died in thirty seconds without giving up Wanda's name, and Phillip's body had been the shield that made it possible.

Under the starless canopy above Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Kessler stepped four paces into the guardroom to speak to the duty sergeant. Phillip immediately stepped half a pace to his left—positioning his broad field-gray back directly between the guardroom glass and the radiator where Marek sat chained.

In the three seconds Phillip’s back screened her from the guardroom, Krystyna drew the glass cyanide ampoule from her hatband, stooped as if fastening the buckle of her galoshes beside the radiator, and pressed the tiny glass bead into Marek’s bloody, bound fingers—whispering two words in Polish so faint only the boy could hear: *"Bądź wolny"*—*Be free.*

* * *

An hour after leaving the Gestapo headquarters on Aleja Szucha, Viktor dismissed the Mercedes at the Belweder gates and hired an open horse-drawn sleigh to drive Marion through the snow-covered alleys of Łazienki Park before dusk. Wrapped in a heavy wolfskin lap robe as the sleigh runners hissed over the packed white paths past the Palace on the Isle, Viktor reached beneath the furs and took her gloved hand.

Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. From the sentry-guarded gates of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

She answered the silence with leaning her fur-hatted head briefly against Viktor's shoulder as the sleigh rounded the frozen lake, letting the quiet, aristocratic composure of a woman taking the winter air serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She looked across the frozen lake of Łazienki and swore that by summer these trees would see a free sky. Under the dim lamp near the snow-covered sleigh path winding past the Palace on the Isle in Łazienki Park, the heavy wolfskin lap robe tucked around their knees in the open sleigh cast a narrow shade, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. The sight of the classical stone statues of Polish kings standing white and silent along the park avenue brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. With cold clarity she reckoned that Viktor himself had recognized that Kessler was testing her nerves at Aleja Szucha—and her icy composure had passed the test in both men's eyes, while the contrast with eighteen-year-old Marek's swollen eye closing in peace as he bit the glass capsule by the radiator made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the musical chime of the horse's harness bells and the hiss of iron runners on packed snow at the same moment she breathed in horseflesh, crisp January frost, pine needles, and wolf fur an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place.

Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Through the drifting night mist of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

"That business with the poisoned prisoner at Aleja Szucha today was ugly," Viktor said, watching the frozen swans huddle by the ice-hole near the amphitheater. "Kessler wanted to see if you had the stomach for Warsaw. You handled yourself like a general’s daughter, Marion."

Within the lamplit precincts of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Measuring the room one last time, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

"A woman does not live with a soldier without learning not to faint at a little noise, Viktor," she replied evenly, looking out at the white classical colonnade of King Stanisław August’s palace reflected in the black ice.

The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Along the darkened tramlines of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Without a backward glance, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Inside her fur toque, the second of the two glass cyanide ampoules still rested in the silk hatband; the first was in the stomach of an eighteen-year-old boy lying on a concrete slab at Aleja Szucha, who had died with her whispered Polish blessing in his ears so the resistance could live.

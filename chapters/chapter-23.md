# Chapter 23 — The Cellar of the Brühl Palace

*Warsaw — July 31, 1944*

---

By the thirty-first of July, 1944, the thunder of Soviet artillery across the Vistula in Praga was a continuous, low-frequency drumroll that made the crystal pendants of the chandeliers at Foksal 14 chime softly day and night. At five o'clock that afternoon, in a secret apartment on Ulica Filtrowa, General Tadeusz Bór-Komorowski signed the order the city had waited five years to hear: *Godzina "W"*—W-Hour—was set for seventeen hundred hours tomorrow, the first of August.

Beside the stone balustrades of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

She dissolved the three crushed grains of luminal in the bottom of the brandy glass before filling it to the brim. There was no escaping the conclusion that at eight o'clock tomorrow morning Phillip was scheduled to be handed over to Erich Kessler's torture cellar at Aleja Szucha—nine hours before the Warsaw Uprising began, while the contrast with Phillip's bruised, bleeding mouth smiling at her from the back of the Feldgendarmerie van on Ulica Mazowiecka made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the continuous, low-frequency rumble of Red Army artillery across the Vistula in Praga at the same moment she breathed in hot July dust, sweat-soaked field wool, Courvoisier, and impending storm until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. It was in small things like the brass seal of General Rainer Stahel on the steel map cylinder that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with pouring three full fingers of Courvoisier into the crystal tumbler while her left hand crushed three tablets of luminal sodium into colorless powder inside her palm, letting the calm, obedient grace of a mistress preparing for evacuation serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where tonight was the final night of Marion Herschlag's existence: before dawn she had to drug Viktor, empty his safe, and forge the prisoner-transfer order that would free Phillip at six in the morning.

The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

At nine o'clock that evening, Viktor returned to Foksal 14 in a sweat-stained field tunic, his holster unbuckled, carrying a double-locked steel map cylinder. His face was gray with fatigue, yet his eyes burned with a grim, fatalistic fire.

With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the long shadow cast by Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Measuring the room one last time, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

"Pack two suitcases tonight, Marion," Viktor said, dropping the steel cylinder onto the library desk. "Tomorrow morning at ten I am putting you on the special courier train to Breslau and Berlin. The Russians are at Wołomin, and these Polish dogs are getting ready to rise in the streets. General Stahel has declared *Festung Warschau* active at dawn."

Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Beneath the shadowed trees of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Without a backward glance, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

Through the lens of her cover identity, the sweat beading along Viktor's silver hairline as he set the half-empty glass down was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. By the threshold of the tall open window overlooking the stifling July garden, the crystal tumbler of drugged Courvoisier held in Viktor's scarred hand stood out in sharp relief, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. As she completed clinking her own untouched glass against his and watching him drink half the tumbler in one long draught, she drew a look of sad, submissive devotion on the eve of her supposed departure for Berlin around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. "Do not send me to Berlin tomorrow, Viktor," she whispered, unbuttoning her silk dress at the throat. "Give me tonight first." Her training insisted that three grains of luminal sodium combined with half a bottle of brandy and five days of sleeplessness would put Viktor under for ten full hours, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the first ten drops of chloral she had given him in Paris fourteen months ago. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that if Viktor woke before eight o'clock tomorrow morning, neither she nor Phillip would live to hear the five o'clock bells of W-Hour.

Under the hooded blue lamps of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. With practiced serenity, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

"And Hauptmann Brandt?" Marion asked casually, pouring him a large tumbler of Courvoisier at the sideboard.

"Kessler finally produced a file from Berlin proving Brandt’s father died in Prinz-Albrecht-Straße for smuggling Poles," Viktor spat in disgust. "I signed the order transferring Brandt from the Brühl guardhouse to Kessler’s cellar at Aleja Szucha for eight o'clock tomorrow morning. Drinks, Marion—and come here."

* * *

She knew that a man like Viktor von Harenberg would never drink the second half of the drugged brandy if she merely sat beside him; he had to be driven to exhaustion first so thirst would make him drain the glass to the dregs.

The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Where Viktor sought proof of his dominion in the heat of her skin, every bruise on her flesh bought three uninterrupted minutes with his dispatch case. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. In the amber half-light, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Combining the cold, resurrected sovereignty of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna with the quiet motion of sliding off Viktor's unconscious body and standing upright in the red-flashing dark to wipe every trace of him from her skin, she gave the room only what it expected to see, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. She washed from head to foot in cold water, dressed in a dark linen skirt and blouse, and walked into the library with Viktor's keys. In the hush that filled the stifling, artillery-lit bedroom of Foksal 14, the empty crystal tumbler of luminal-laced Courvoisier rolling onto the linen sheet appeared almost motionless, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She let her attention brush past Viktor's slack, unconscious hand hanging over the edge of the mattress, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She forced herself to accept that for fourteen months she had traded the heat of her skin for the Reich's secrets, and tonight the debt was paid in full, though the thought of the vow she had made on the night her father died at Palmiry returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of hot sweat, cognac, summer dust, and the final shedding of her captivity hung in the draft alongside the continuous boom of Russian guns across the river and the slowing rasp of Viktor's breath steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray.

As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Beneath the looming cornice of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Beneath the ruthless weight of the older man's scarred hands, she let him believe he conquered the woman while she harvested the officer. With her eyes clear and level, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

In the stifling July heat of the bedroom, with the distant artillery flashing like summer lightning behind the blackout curtains, Marion stripped off her silk dress and came to him on the Empire bed with a calculated, scorching ferocity that left him breathless. She pushed him back onto the linen sheets, straddling his heavy, scarred hips, her gold hair falling loose over her bare breasts as she took him inside her and rode him with a relentless, undulating rhythm.

In that fierce clash of carnal power and calculated surrender, passion became a blade that drained his vigilance before the clock struck three. Near the iron-grated cellars of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. With unhurried grace, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Viktor’s hands gripped her thighs, his head thrown back against the pillows, his breath coming in hoarse, ragged gasps as she drove him harder and faster—using every ounce of her strength and every dark trick she had learned in fourteen months of captivity to drain the iron from his muscles. Looking down into his flushed, conquered face in the red glare of the eastern artillery, Krystyna felt no pity and no fear: this was her final battle in the bedroom of the enemy, fought for Phillip’s life and for the freedom of her city.

Across the darkened perspective of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. While the Colonel consumed her body with feral urgency, she matched his tempest without yielding a single millimeter of her soul. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Past the iron railings of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Across the tangled linen where power and obsession collided, the raw friction of their nights served as her most impenetrable alibi. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Keeping her pulse steady, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

When Viktor finally arched beneath her with a strangled roar and fell back limp against the sweat-soaked sheets, his eyes already heavy from the first half-glass of luminal, she reached to the nightstand and held the crystal tumbler to his parched lips. "Drink, darling," she murmured softly as he swallowed the remaining drugged cognac to the last drop. Within six minutes, his hand slipped from her hip, and he sank into a coma-deep stupor.

* * *

In the library, Krystyna unlocked both the wall safe and the steel map cylinder. Inside the cylinder lay the master deployment map of *Festung Warschau*: every German bunker, Tiger tank company, Goliath tracked-mine depot, and SS strongpoint in Warsaw marked in red and blue ink. She did not merely photograph it; she took the entire original linen map and replaced it inside the cylinder with an outdated June rail chart.

In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Along the darkened tramlines of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Steeling her resolve once more, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

She left Viktor sleeping like a stone on Ulica Foksal and ran through the dark streets to Tadeusz's command post. Nothing about the red-inked circles marking every German bunker around the Brühl Palace and the Old Town escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the click of the Olympia typewriter keys matching the exact alignment of the military form and the faint drift of oxalic acid, purple stamp pad ink, and the hot midnight wind outside that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Past the edge of the library desk of Foksal 14 under the green desk lamp, the altered prisoner-transfer warrant bearing Viktor's genuine signature and fresh purple eagle stamp waited in the gloom, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Without haste, rolling the master linen map of Festung Warschau around the altered warrant and slipping both into a canvas courier tube reinforced the quiet authority of the swift, triumphant precision of an operative completing her masterwork, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that with Viktor's genuine signature and his own typewriter ribbon on the warrant, the Brühl Palace guardhouse commander would hand Phillip straight to Tadeusz's disguised Feldgendarmerie squad at five-thirty in the morning, though every detail of Phillip forging transit passes at his desk in the Brühl Palace to save Polish lives remained as sharp as cut glass.

In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Within the high-walled gardens of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Letting the silence work for her, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Then she opened Viktor’s command folder. Clipped to the top was the duplicate prisoner-transfer order for *Hauptmann Phillip Brandt, Abt. Ic*, signed at the bottom by *V. Frhr. von Harenberg, Oberst*, authorizing transfer from the Brühl Palace guardhouse at 08:00 hours to the SD at Aleja Szucha.

Across the curfew-bound sweep of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Using Tadeusz’s oxalic-acid ink eradicator from her sewing kit, she bleached out the typed time *08:00* and the destination *Aleja Szucha*, rolled the genuine signed sheet into Viktor’s own Olympia command typewriter in the library, and typed in the exact font: *05:30 Uhr — Überstellung an Feldgericht Warschau-West durch Feldgendarmerie-Streife Kruk*.

The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Within the mirror-lined rooms of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Without haste, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Behind the double-bolted doors of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. As the minute hand crept on, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

She stamped the alteration with Viktor’s personal rubber seal from the desk drawer and slipped down the secret stone stair behind the armoire—where Viktor’s iron brackets had been unscrewed by her own screwdriver that afternoon—into the July night.

* * *

At twenty-five minutes past five on the morning of August first, as a blood-red summer dawn rose through the smoke over the Vistula, a gray Wehrmacht Adler staff car with Feldgendarmerie plates pulled up to the rear iron gates of the Brühl Palace guardhouse on Ulica Fredry. At the wheel sat Tadeusz Kowalski in the field-gray tunic and steel gorget of a Wehrmacht Feldwebel; in the passenger seat sat an eighteen-year-old Kedyw marksman with a captured Schmeisser across his knees. Across the street in the doorway of a bombed ruin waited Krystyna with a Walther P38 in her pocket.

Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Beyond the guarded courtyard of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. While the night deepened outside, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Set against the rear seat of the speeding Adler staff car turning off Ulica Niecała, the unlocked iron handcuffs dropping onto the floorboard between Phillip's boots held a quiet, insistent weight, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Her senses caught the roar of the Adler's engine and the clatter of Tadeusz shifting gears toward the safe house at the same moment she breathed in morning dust, petrol, gun oil, and the fierce joy of deliverance that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. She kissed the raw cuff-marks on his wrists just as he had once kissed Viktor's bruises on hers. Against the quiet of the room, the deep red marks left by the German handcuffs around Phillip's wrists stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted framing Phillip's bruised, unshaven face between her palms as he crushed her against his chest and the familiar armor of pure, unmasked joy flashing in the red dawn light to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when in less than twelve hours thirty thousand soldiers of the Armia Krajowa would rise across every street in Warsaw.

Past the shuttered shopfronts of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. In that narrow pause between beats, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Tadeusz presented the warrant signed by Oberst von Harenberg to the sleepy guardhouse lieutenant. The lieutenant checked the purple eagle seal, glanced at the clock—05:32—cursed the early hour, and unlocked Cell Three.

The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Beyond the shuttered windows of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. As the shadows lengthened, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Two minutes later, Hauptmann Phillip Brandt—unshaven, his captain’s tunic stripped of its shoulder boards, his wrists in iron handcuffs—was marched out the iron gate by Tadeusz and shoved into the rear seat of the Adler. As the car turned the corner into Ulica Niecała out of sight of the Brühl sentries, Krystyna wrenched open the rear door and threw herself inside as Tadeusz unlocked Phillip’s handcuffs.

She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. From the sentry-guarded gates of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. In the hush that followed, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

"Krysiu!" Phillip gasped, catching her in his freed arms as the car sped into the labyrinth of Śródmieście.

Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Through the drifting night mist of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

"You are late for the war, Herr Hauptmann," she whispered against his unshaven cheek, laughing through her tears. "W-Hour is at five o'clock this afternoon."

* * *

At seven o'clock on the morning of August first, in the locked attic above Tadeusz’s command post on Ulica Chmielna—after Phillip and Krystyna had spread the stolen linen master map of *Festung Warschau* across the briefing table for the Home Army’s battalion commanders—Tadeusz led his officers downstairs to issue the ammunition, leaving Krystyna and Phillip alone under the rafters until noon.

Through the narrow archways of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. In the reverent, aching tenderness of Phillip's embrace, their love was the one truth in Warsaw that needed no cipher. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

They held each other in the golden light until the noon church bells called them to their posts. Not three paces from the sunlit attic above the Home Army command post on Ulica Chmielna, the torn field-gray tunic cast forever onto the floorboards beside the cot lay in the amber half-light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She listened to the low murmur of young fighters down in the courtyard cleaning Sten guns and singing softly while the room held the aroma of sun-warmed pine rafters, clean linen, gun oil from below, and the warm skin of her beloved pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She held fast to the recognition that for the first time in their lives they lay together in the full light of morning with no German uniform and no false name between them, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the candlelit garret on Ulica Freta on Christmas Eve when they had prayed to live to see the spring. In the choreography of the room, twining her fingers through his as he moved deep and slow inside her in the shaft of morning sun flowed seamlessly into eyes shining clear and unafraid in the August light, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. Before the night was over, at five o'clock that afternoon the sky over Warsaw would turn to fire, yet nothing that came after could take away this morning, and she intended to be ready.

The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Where the boy who had held her youth now stood beside her as a man, every wall she had built to survive the war dissolved into pure truth. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Anchored by her silent oath, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Ten hours remained until *Godzina "W"*. Outside, the August sun was rising hot and brassy over a city holding its breath before the storm.

In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Along the damp paving stones of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Beneath the quiet worship of Phillip's mouth and dark eyes, five winters of exile burned away in a passion more perilous than any bullet. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

In the quiet sunlit attic, Phillip stripped off the torn, prison-stained field-gray tunic for the last time in his life. Krystyna came into his bare arms, unbuttoning her blouse and pressing her naked breasts against his chest as they sank down together onto the narrow cot beneath the dormer window. There was no darkness now—only the clear golden morning light pouring across their skin as Phillip kissed the raw marks of the past fourteen months from her throat and shoulders, and she kissed the red iron cuff-scars on his wrists.

In those clandestine meetings where no uniform stood between them, there was no mask left between them—only a devotion that had outlived the ashes. Down the long colonnades of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Drawing a slow breath, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

When they joined together on the sunlit cot, it was slow, deep, and intolerably sweet—two soldiers who had walked through the valley of the shadow of death and come out into the morning of battle belonging wholly to one another.

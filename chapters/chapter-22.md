# Chapter 22 — The Trap at Café Ziemiańska

*Warsaw — July 1944*

---

By the twentieth of July, the air in Warsaw felt like the inside of a boiler about to burst. Through the heart of the city streamed an endless, ragged retreat of defeated Wehrmacht divisions from the East—unshaven soldiers without rifles sitting on horse-drawn peasant carts, burned-out Panther tanks towed by half-tracks, and civilian German bureaucrats piling suitcases onto flatcars at the Dworzec Zachodni. On the afternoon of July twentieth, news flashed through the Brühl Palace teleprinters that Colonel von Stauffenberg had detonated a bomb inside Hitler’s Wolf’s Lair headquarters in East Prussia.

Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Across the sunlit street, Phillip unbuckled the flap of his leather holster and shook his head at her once—imperceptibly—before stepping off the curb. Beside the sun-baked pavement of Ulica Mazowiecka twenty yards from Café Ziemiańska, the outline of Tadeusz Kowalski sitting at an outside marble table with a folded newspaper signaling danger marked the stillness, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She was acutely aware of the rumble of retreating Wehrmacht horse-carts on Marszałkowska and the hum of July flies and the faint drift of hot asphalt, horse dung, roasted barley, and the sudden metallic taste of an ambush that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. No illusion could soften the fact that Kessler's radio direction-finders and street watchers had zeroed in on Tadeusz's emergency rendezvous at Café Ziemiańska, though every detail of the Rue de la Huchette ambush in Paris where Stefan Wilk had died in the rain remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, slowing her step by the milliner's shop window as Phillip's eyes locked onto hers from across the street reinforced the quiet authority of the casual pause of a shopper admiring a straw hat in the glass, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, with six SD gunmen closing both ends of the block, neither she nor Tadeusz could reach the corner without being shot or taken alive, whatever price it demanded.

A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Drawing a slow breath, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

Within an hour, the SS and Gestapo in Warsaw went mad. Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler—who had finally received a confirmed repeat wire from Strasbourg that morning proving the real Marion Herschlag had died in 1937, though he did not yet know which woman in Warsaw was using her name because Viktor had registered her at Foksal 14 simply as *Fräulein M. von Harenberg-Haushalt*—learned through a tortured radio directional finder that the high-command mole "Jaskółka" was meeting "Kruk" at four o'clock on the terrace of the old Café Ziemiańska on Ulica Mazowiecka.

Within the high-walled gardens of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. As the minute hand crept on, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

At five minutes to four, Krystyna was walking north up Ulica Mazowiecka in a simple summer print dress and straw hat, twenty yards from the café awning where Tadeusz sat reading the *Nowy Kurjer Warszawski*, when she saw Hauptmann Phillip Brandt step out of a tobacconist’s doorway across the street.

Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. In the long shadow cast by the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Steeling her resolve once more, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Her senses caught the sudden crash of breaking glass as Phillip staggered deliberately into the lead SD plainclothesman at the same moment she breathed in spilled schnapps, hot dust, and the sudden shock of a street disturbance pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. "Out of my way, you civilian swine!" Phillip roared in slurred Prussian German, and drew his Luger. She observed Phillip's captain's tunic ripped open at the collar as he knocked the lead Gestapo man into the gutter with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted backing slowly into the recessed doorway of the adjacent courtyard while every eye on the street turned toward Phillip's shouting and the familiar armor of a startled bystander shrinking back from a drunken German officer to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. With cold clarity she reckoned that Phillip was staging a violent, drunken Wehrmacht officer's brawl right in the faces of Kessler's plainclothes squad to blow the trap before she walked into it, while the contrast with the code of the underground that when the net drops, the decoy draws the spears made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when by attacking an SD agent in plain clothes, Phillip was guaranteeing his own arrest by the Feldgendarmerie to save her life and Tadeusz's.

Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Beyond the shuttered windows of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. In that narrow pause between beats, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Down the long colonnades of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. With unhurried grace, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Behind the double-bolted doors of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Above the slated mansard roofs of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Without haste, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. With practiced serenity, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

Phillip’s face was white beneath his summer cap. Across the street at both corners of Ulica Świętokrzyska and Plac Małachowskiego, six men in gray linen civilian jackets with the unmistakable bulge of Walther pistols under their left armpits were closing in on the café terrace.

* * *

"Geheime Staatspolizei!" the knocked-down plainclothesman yelled from the gutter, reaching for his Walther—only for Phillip to fire two shots from his Luger straight up into the striped canvas awning and the plaster cornice of Café Ziemiańska.

In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Along the darkened tramlines of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Without haste, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

She knew beyond doubt that by shouting that the SS were arresting Wehrmacht officers after the Stauffenberg bomb plot, Phillip had ensured the Army Feldgendarmerie arrested him instead of letting Kessler take him straight to Aleja Szucha, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Phillip's razor-sharp tactical mind turning the chaos of July twentieth into a life-saving shield. From where she stood in the shattered terrace of Café Ziemiańska amid overturned marble tables, Phillip's Luger pistol kicked across the pavement as four Feldgendarmes pinned his arms was just visible, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Even with iron handcuffs locking his wrists behind his back, Phillip's head was high as the van doors slammed shut. The sight of the bright streak of blood running down Phillip's chin as his dark eyes found the empty doorway where she had vanished brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the screaming of sirens, whistles, and furious arguments between Wehrmacht police and SD agents while the room held the aroma of cordite smoke, spilled schnapps, broken plaster dust, and hot July sweat an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Before the night was over, because the Wehrmacht Feldgendarmerie had taken custody of him, Phillip would be held in the Brühl Palace military guardhouse rather than the Gestapo cellars—for a few days at least, and she intended to be ready.

Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. With practiced serenity, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

At the twin roar of the 9mm pistol, pandemonium erupted across Ulica Mazowiecka. Waiters, women, and German clerks dived under the marble tables; iron shop shutters slammed down; and Tadeusz Kowalski overturned his table into the knees of the second SD man and vanished through the café’s rear kitchen passage into the cellars.

A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

"Treason!" Phillip shouted wildly in German, kicking over a row of café chairs to block the three SD men rushing from the north corner. "Stauffenberg has shot the Führer in Prussia and the SS are arresting the Wehrmacht! Help! Wehrmacht officers to me!"

Near the iron-grated cellars of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. While the night deepened outside, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond standing over Tadeusz's map table with her hands clenched white on the wood and the fierce, unyielding determination of a woman who refused to leave her lover to the hangman, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of printer's ink, machine oil, damp brick, and the dry heat of late July hung in the draft alongside the low hum of the underground radio receiver monitoring German police wavelengths as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. "Viktor has blocked Kessler's transfer warrant until the court-martial inquiry on August first," Krystyna told Tadeusz. "Which means on the night of July thirty-first, we take both the Festung Warschau map and Phillip out of the Brühl Palace." Against the muted backdrop of the cellar printing shop on Ulica Wspólna where Tadeusz waited, a crate of Home Army hand grenades—Filipinki—being packed in straw by young couriers drew her eye, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She measured the risk by the certainty that Phillip was locked in the basement guardhouse of the Brühl Palace, and Kessler was already demanding his transfer to Aleja Szucha on suspicion of aiding the escaped courier, though the thought of Phillip's voice in the Łazienki greenhouse: 'When the Home Army gives the order, my pistol and my life are yours' returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when the Red Army's tanks had just reached the eastern suburbs of Praga across the river, and W-Hour was less than ten days away.

Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Along the damp paving stones of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. With her eyes clear and level, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Nothing could have caused more instant, paralyzing chaos on July twentieth, 1944. Two passing Wehrmacht Feldgendarmerie patrol trucks screeched to a halt on Mazowiecka; seeing a decorated Wehrmacht Hauptmann with an Iron Cross being wrestled by civilian-clothed SD men amid shouts of an SS coup, eight army military policemen piled out with submachine guns leveled at Kessler’s agents.

Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Within the mirror-lined rooms of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Anchored by her silent oath, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Through the drifting night mist of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Without a backward glance, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Near the sandbagged corners of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. In the hush that followed, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Outside the tall casements of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Keeping her pulse steady, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

While the Feldgendarmerie and Kessler’s furious plainclothesmen screamed jurisdictional threats at each other across Phillip’s bleeding lip and handcuffed wrists, Krystyna slipped through the courtyard passage into Ulica Jasna—her eyes burning with tears as she saw the army police haul Phillip into the back of the Wehrmacht arrest van.

* * *

Two days after Phillip’s arrest at Café Ziemiańska, Viktor was still livid over his intelligence captain’s apparent drunken brawl with the SD. "Firing his Luger into a café ceiling and shouting about Stauffenberg!" Viktor raged at breakfast. "If I had not locked him in my own Brühl guardhouse for a Wehrmacht court-martial, Kessler would have had him hanging from a meat hook at Aleja Szucha by sunset!"

Beyond the guarded courtyard of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. In those clandestine meetings where no uniform stood between them, a single touch of his mouth undid the hardened spy and woke the woman beneath. In the hush that followed, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Where the shadow fell across the paved side courtyard of the Brühl Palace along Ulica Fredry, the barred basement window grate of Cell Three at the level of her shoes remained half-hidden, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She tucked the handkerchief into her sleeve and walked on into the palace before the sentry turned back. To anyone else, Phillip's dark eyes shining unbroken out of the cellar shadow below the pavement might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She did not allow herself to forget that seeing Phillip's bruised fingers reach up to touch the toe of her shoe through the iron bars filled her with a fierce, lethal determination, though every detail of Prisoners in Pawiak reaching through cellar grates to touch the hands of those who loved them remained as sharp as cut glass. With the unhurried poise of a lady picking up a dropped kerchief in the wind governing the motion of stooping gracefully to retrieve her fallen lace handkerchief from the iron grating to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, letting her fingers tremble for the only man who knew her true name and held it sacred. She was acutely aware of the measured crunch of the Wehrmacht sentry's jackboots turning at the far corner of the wall and the faint drift of hot July stone, damp cellar air rising from the grate, and the leather binocular case in her hand sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

When Phillip whispered her true Polish name against her skin, they held each other as though each stolen hour had been won from death itself. Beneath the looming cornice of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Keeping her pulse steady, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

That afternoon at three, Krystyna walked to the Brühl Palace carrying Viktor’s spare field binoculars in a leather case—taking the paved courtyard path that passed directly beside the barred basement windows of the military guardhouse wing on Ulica Fredry.

Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Within the sacred, unguarded sanctuary of Phillip's arms, the contrast between the Colonel's storm and Phillip's reverence cut her to the bone. In the amber half-light, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

As the courtyard sentry turned at the far end of his beat, Krystyna paused for five seconds beside the third barred cellar grate and let her lace handkerchief flutter down against the iron bars.

A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Where five years of longing deepened into an all-consuming love, every heartbeat shared in the dark bound their fates closer to the same precipice. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

Beneath the shadowed trees of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Against the gentle, trembling honesty of Phillip's touch, their slow, luminous love refused to die beneath the boots of the Reich. Letting the silence work for her, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

In the secret light that shone whenever their eyes met across the dark, she knew what it meant to be cherished as sacred rather than claimed as spoils. From the sentry-guarded gates of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Measuring the room one last time, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Past the iron railings of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Held close against the steady beat of Phillip's heart, the boy from the Konstancin lindens had become the man she would die beside. As the shadows lengthened, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Yet in the stolen, breathless stillness with Phillip, tenderness proved a fiercer fire than brutality could ever ignite. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Across the cobble-paved expanse of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Whenever Phillip's hand found hers in the shadows, his hands asked permission where the world took by force, and that undid her. Drawing a slow breath, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Against the slow, luminous fire that burned between her and Phillip, every unspoken year between thirty-eight and forty-three spoke in a single kiss. Down the wind-scoured length of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. As the minute hand crept on, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

From the gloom inside the basement cell, Phillip’s hand—bruised across the knuckles, his wrist raw from the cuffs—reached up through the iron grating and touched her shoe through the bars. "July thirty-first," she breathed down toward the grate without looking down. "Hold on until dawn on the first, my love."

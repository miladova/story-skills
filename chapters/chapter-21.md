# Chapter 21 — The Wolf's Reckoning

*Warsaw — Late June 1944*

---

On the threshold of the Foksal 14 library, Krystyna kept her right hand inside her leather handbag on the grip of the 6.35mm Browning while her eyes moved from Viktor’s face to the tarnished silver frame in his hand. Then she saw the glass of the frame: the bomb blast that had collapsed the east wing in September 1939 had cracked the miniature portrait in half, and four winters of damp behind the wainscoting had blackened the watercolor paper until only a headless shoulder in white lace remained.

Beneath the looming cornice of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. As the shadows lengthened, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Not three paces from the oak-paneled library of Foksal 14, the smudge of pale Coty face powder on the tip of Viktor's forefinger lay in the amber half-light, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She did not shrink from the finger held before her face; she knocked his hand aside with a sharp slap of her glove. In the presence of the iron carpenter's chisel still lying on the leather desk blotter, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. There was no escaping the conclusion that the ruined miniature frame had not betrayed her Polish identity, but the spilled face powder inside the secret passage had betrayed that she was slipping out of the house at night, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of dropping her gold compact on the dark stone steps on the night she went to meet Wanda in May. In the choreography of the room, leaning back in the leather armchair and looking straight up into Viktor's suffused blue eyes without blinking flowed seamlessly into an expression of icy, outraged composure, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the heavy, deliberate thud of the library door latching shut behind her while the room held the aroma of rose-scented French powder, old oak rot, and the metallic chill of mortal peril as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry.

In the long shadow cast by the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Without haste, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Viktor was not looking at the ruined portrait. He was looking at his own thick forefinger, which he had just drawn across the inner sill of the hidden wall cavity—and which was dusted with pale, rose-scented French Coty face powder from the compact Krystyna had dropped there a week ago.

Along the damp paving stones of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Keeping her pulse steady, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

"Come in, Marion," Viktor said softly, and kicked the oak library door shut behind her. "Close your purse. Sit down."

From the sentry-guarded gates of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Steeling her resolve once more, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

"Your orderly!" she spat in German. "Do you mean the orderly who drinks schnapps in the kitchen with Kessler's chauffeur every Tuesday?" Her training insisted that a proud fifty-three-year-old man with a twenty-six-year-old mistress always fears one thing before espionage: he fears a younger lover, though the thought of her instructor's lesson that when caught in a small mystery, confess to a lesser sin that flatters or inflames the inquisitor's vanity returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of cigar smoke, heated wool, and the static before a lightning strike hung in the draft alongside the low rumble of Viktor's breathing as his temper reached the boiling point sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. To anyone else, the pulse throbbing like a worm at Viktor's temple might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond rising to her feet so her face was inches from his chest and the blazing, indignant fury of a proud woman accused by a jealous keeper, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when she had to turn his suspicion away from the garden stair and straight onto Sturmbannführer Kessler's surveillance.

Behind the double-bolted doors of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. With her eyes clear and level, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

She took her hand off the pistol, snapped her handbag shut, and walked calmly to the leather armchair opposite her father’s desk. "Have you turned carpenter in your spare time, Viktor? What is that filthy hole in the paneling?"

Near the sandbagged corners of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Measuring the room one last time, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Down the wind-scoured length of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

"A priest-hole built into the wall between this library and your dressing room," Viktor said, rising slowly from behind the desk and walking around to stand over her chair. He held his forefinger two inches from her nose. "And inside a Polish cache that has supposedly been sealed since thirty-nine, my orderly found fresh French face powder—Coty *L’Origan*, the exact powder sitting on your dressing table—and heel-marks on the stone steps leading down to the garden postern gate."

* * *

Viktor’s hand closed like an iron shackle around her jaw, forcing her head back against the bookcase. "Do not talk to me about Kessler’s chauffeur! Tell me who came up those stone stairs from the garden while I was at the Brühl Palace, or by God I will tear this room apart brick by brick!"

The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

It was in small things like the white crescent marks his fingers left along her cheekbones that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the draft stirred the tall mahogany bookcase of the Foksal library, Viktor's thick fingers spanning her jawbone while her hands gripped his wrists remained fixed in place, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. With wounded, furious pride mixed with tears of rage governing the motion of holding his murderous stare without a single flicker of fear until his grip loosened by a fraction to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Viktor strode into the dressing room, lifted the loose stone inside the panel, and came back holding the chamois bag of French gold Napoleons. She forced herself to accept that sacrificing her twenty emergency gold Napoleons under the top step was a stroke of genius: it explained both the powder and the footprints as a woman's greedy fear of the SD, though every detail of the twenty gold coins she had tucked beneath the loose stone in October for just such a day remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that when he checked the top step and found the leather pouch of French gold coins, he would believe every word she had just screamed at him.

The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. In the hush that followed, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

"Nobody came up those stairs, you blind Prussian fool!" Marion cried, her eyes flashing with furious tears as she gripped his wrists with both hands. "I opened that panel behind my armoire three weeks ago when I was looking for a place to hide my diamonds and the forty thousand Reichsmarks in gold coin I brought from Paris—because your friend Kessler had two men search my dressing table while we were at Wawer!"

The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. As the minute hand crept on, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Viktor froze, his fingers still clamped around her jaw, his blue eyes narrowing to slits. "Kessler searched your dressing room?"

The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. While the night deepened outside, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

She answered the silence with standing in the bedroom doorway with her arms crossed over her chest and her chin high, letting the unforgiving, sultry anger of a woman who had been wrongly doubted serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. "You little Alsace wildcat," Viktor breathed, kicking the bedroom door shut behind him. "Hiding gold from me in my own walls." Between the doorway and the open doorway between the dressing room and the master bedroom, the chamois leather pouch of twenty French gold coins in Viktor's palm marked the half-lit interval, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Through the lens of her cover identity, Viktor's blue eyes dropping from her bruised jaw to the rise and fall of her breasts was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that finding the gold coins exactly where she said they were had shattered his suspicion and replaced it with a dark, violent surge of relief and desire, while the contrast with the way a storm on the Vistula always broke hardest just before it cleared made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the clink of gold Napoleons as Viktor tossed the pouch onto the bed at the same moment she breathed in chamois leather, old stone dust, and the sudden, dangerous shift in Viktor's blood from suspicion to rut filtering through the heavy draperies without cease.

The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Letting the silence work for her, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

"Who else leaves mud from Aleja Szucha on my Persian rug and goes through my jewel case?" she demanded breathlessly, twisting her chin in his grip. "And yes—two nights when you left me alone until two in the morning while the Russian bombers were over Praga, I went down those stone steps into the garden because I was suffocating inside four walls! If you think I have a lover in this mud-heap of a city, call your firing squad right now—or go into my dressing room and count the gold Napoleons I hid under the bottom step!"

A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. With unhurried grace, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Without a backward glance, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

She had actually hidden twenty gold Napoleons under the loose flagstone at the top of the shaft in October as emergency escape funds. Viktor stared down into her blazing gray-green eyes for five terrible seconds—weighing the gold coins and Kessler’s intrusion against his own terror of losing her.

* * *

The night that followed was the darkest and most ruthless since Paris. Driven half-mad by the double pressure of the collapsing Eastern Front and the terror that Marion might slip through his fingers, Viktor came at her across the bedroom like a man possessed by demons. When she fought him off at first—slapping his face hard enough to leave the print of her palm across his cheek in continuation of her staged fury—he caught both her wrists in one hand, dragged her down onto the Persian carpet beside the Empire bed, and tore the silk dress from her body from collar to hem.

Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Within the gilded cage of the Colonel's possessive desire, she turned his own territorial paranoia into the shield that kept the SD at bay. Without a backward glance, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

She lay still beneath his panting weight on the rug, her eyes dry and cold as winter stars. She let her attention brush past the four red beads of blood welled along the old Verdun scar on Viktor's side, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the ragged, guttural gasps of Viktor's breath and the creak of the floorboards while the room held the aroma of sweat, copper blood from his scratched ribs, bruised skin, and wool pile steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. Across the Persian carpet at the foot of the Empire bed in Foksal 14, the chamois bag of French gold Napoleons spilled gleaming across the crimson bedspread above them rested in the quiet shadow, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. As she completed clenching her jaw and driving her heels into the carpet until Viktor's strength broke inside her, she drew the feral, untamed heat of a woman conquering her conqueror around herself like silk, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that every bruise she took on this carpet tonight had bought the final weeks the Home Army needed before W-Hour, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the green glasshouse in the rain where Phillip had kissed her as if she were made of light.

Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Meeting the bruising force of his mouth with a calculated fire, every rasp of breath in the dark was calculated to leave him sated and unguarded. Drawing a slow breath, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

"Slap me again," he rasped against her mouth, pinning her naked shoulders to the wool pile of the rug with his heavy chest while his knee forced her thighs apart. "Show me how much you hate this city, Marion—"

Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Where intimacy itself was wielded like an instrument of war, she met his relentless storm with an equal and unforgiving fire of her own. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

On the floor of her murdered parents’ bedroom, Krystyna met the tempest with her teeth bared. She raked her nails down his scarred ribs until blood beaded along the white skin, arching up from the carpet as he drove into her with a harsh, punishing force that bruised her spine against the floorboards. It was no longer seduction; it was hand-to-hand combat in the dark—flesh against flesh, iron against velvet—where she took everything his fifty-three-year-old body could hurl at her and burned him dry, holding onto the secret knowledge that in less than five weeks this house, this uniform, and this empire would go up in flames.

At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Under the heavy shadow of the Colonel's territorial hunger, the mark of his fingers on her wrist was the price of the keys in his coat. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. In the dark, bruising arena of the Colonel's quarters, she weaponized his desire until his suspicion drowned in exhaustion. In that narrow pause between beats, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Against the harsh, possessive storm of Viktor's appetite, she answered his roughness with a scorching heat that left him blind to her craft. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

When he finally collapsed spent and groaning against her breasts on the floor, his fingers still tangled in her loosened gold hair, Krystyna stared over his silver-templed head at the locked library door—knowing that she had survived the wolf’s teeth by the breadth of a fingernail, and that the next time he learned the truth, one of them would not leave the room alive.

* * *

At eight o'clock the next morning, Viktor stood in the dressing room watching Corporal Hanke screw four heavy iron angle-brackets across the oak panel behind the shoe shelves of the armoire, sealing the secret stair to the garden.

Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Along the riverward slope of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Within the dressing room of Foksal 14 before the triptych mirror, her gaze returned briefly to the four heavy iron angle-brackets screwed across the secret armoire panel, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. A subtle trace of sewing-machine oil, bruised skin under lavender powder, and raw oak shavings hung in the draft alongside the squeak of Corporal Hanke's screwdriver biting into the seasoned oak that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She tested the top bracket with her little finger; it moved half a millimeter and settled back flush against the wood. Nothing about the tiny ring of dark machine oil hidden inside the counter-sunk head of each screw escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining bored, unbothered indifference to the sealing of her escape route with the quiet motion of drawing the hairbrush smoothly through her gold waves while watching Hanke work in the glass, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that when W-Hour came, those loosened screws would let Phillip storm straight up from the garden into the second floor.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Beyond the shuttered windows of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct marked the slow passage of the rationed night. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. With practiced serenity, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Marion sat at her dressing table in her silk peignoir, brushing her long gold hair with slow, even strokes while her bruised ribs ached from the night on the carpet. "Do tell Hanke to use longer screws, Viktor," she said coolly in the mirror. "I should hate for Herr Kessler’s mice to pry it open again."

Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Within the mirror-lined rooms of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. In the amber half-light, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Past the iron railings of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Above the slated mansard roofs of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Anchored by her silent oath, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Outside the tall casements of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. As the shadows lengthened, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

Yet that afternoon, thirty minutes after Viktor left for the Brühl Palace, Krystyna took a stout steel manicure file and a drop of sewing-machine oil from her vanity drawer, reached behind the shoe shelves, and backed each of Corporal Hanke’s sixteen wood-screws out until they held by a single thread—leaving the iron brackets looking immovable to the eye, yet ready to pull free with one hard wrench when her final hour in this house arrived.

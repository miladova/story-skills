# Chapter 5 — Orders for the East

*Paris — September 1943*

---

By the third week of September, the chestnut trees along the Champs-Élysées had turned the color of rusted iron, their dry leaves skittering beneath the jackboots of the changing guard at the Hôtel Majestic. Inside the Rue de Rivoli apartment, three brass-bound steamer trunks stood open in the foyer, while Viktor’s orderly, Corporal Hanke, packed the Colonel’s dress boots in tissue paper.

Set against the drawing room of the Rue de Rivoli apartment, three brass-bound steamer trunks standing open in the parquet hall held a quiet, insistent weight, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Her senses caught the rustle of tissue paper and the thud of boots as Corporal Hanke packed in the foyer at the same moment she breathed in camphor balls, saddle soap, autumn damp, and Earl Grey tea an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Her voice did not crack when he spoke the name of her street; she only tilted her lips up for his kiss. The sight of the purple OKH teleprinter stamp at the top of Viktor's transfer order brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted resting her palms flat against the lapels of Viktor's tunic to hide the sudden chill in her fingers and the familiar armor of a pout of spoiled, reluctant resignation to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when to sleep with her father's conqueror under her father's roof was a torment she had not dared imagine, yet it placed her at the heart of the Warsaw command.

Viktor stood in the drawing room before the tall windows, a teleprinter sheet from the Oberkommando des Heeres between his fingers. "The order is signed by Keitel himself," he said without turning around. "Chief of Wehrmacht Transport and Counter-Insurgency Fortifications for Oberfeldkommandantur Warschau. We board the special military sleeper at the Gare de l’Est on Thursday at nineteen hundred hours."

Marion, seated on the crimson velvet settee with a cup of tea in her hands, let the porcelain saucer give a single, deliberate clink against the table. "Warsaw," she said, inflecting the word with just the right mixture of dread and reluctant submission. "Comtesse Solange says the partisans shoot at staff cars in broad daylight there, Viktor. And that there is typhus in the eastern quarters."

As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Across the darkened perspective of the Place Vendôme, champagne and flattery remained the sharpest currencies of survival. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. With practiced serenity, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

What Viktor thought was a gilded cage was the one house on earth whose walls belonged to her. Not three paces from the marble mantelpiece stripped of its summer bronzes, the framed map of the Eastern Front being taken down for crating lay in the amber half-light, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She listened to the dry rattle of September chestnut leaves blowing against the tall glass while the room held the aroma of packing straw, pine crates, and Viktor's cigar smoke as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. She did not allow herself to forget that every room on the second floor of the Foksal palace had a servant's passage she had played in as a child, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the secret panel behind the library wainscoting on Ulica Foksal where she used to hide letters from Phillip Brandt. In the choreography of the room, tracing the silver braid of Viktor's collar tab with one manicured nail flowed seamlessly into the clingy, possessive warmth of a woman who feared losing her protector to the war, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Before the night was over, if the hidden passages in the Foksal house were still intact, she could come and go without passing the Wehrmacht sentries at the gate, and she intended to be ready.

He turned from the window, his silver-templed head high, his blue eyes hard and bright with the old soldier’s hunger for a real front. "Let them shoot. For two years that civilian peacock Frank and the SS butchers have mismanaged the Generalgouvernement until every forest between the Bug and the Vistula is crawling with bandits. My job is to turn Warsaw into an iron rail hub that cannot be cut—even if the Russians reach the river."

"And what am I to do while you are turning Warsaw into an iron hub?" she asked softly, setting her teacup aside and rising to walk toward him. "Sit in some drafty Polish hotel listening to artillery?"

A subtle trace of cedarwood, French silk, and the faint acetone from the washbasin where she had dissolved Zygmunt's disk hung in the draft alongside the distant whistle of a barge on the Seine sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. She watched the maid fold the emerald Vionnet gown and felt the ghost of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna stir from her grave. To anyone else, the hollowed tortoiseshell hairbrush resting safely inside her locked leather jewel case might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond turning her head to instruct the French maid which furs to pack in the top tray and the brisk vanity of a woman determined to outdress the wives of German officials in the East, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. There was no escaping the conclusion that she had already memorized every syllable of Zygmunt's Warsaw contact procedure for 'Kruk' on Ulica Chmielna, though the thought of her mother's dressing table on the second floor of Foksal 14, with its silver-backed brushes returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when in seventy-two hours her heels would touch the platform of the Dworzec Główny in Warsaw.

Viktor caught her by the waist, drawing her against his field-gray wool tunic. "I have requisitioned the second floor of a former count’s town palace on a street called the Foksal, three minutes from the Brühl Palace. It has a Bechstein piano, French parquet, and a walled garden. You will lack for nothing, Marion. Except perhaps an excuse to look at other men."

* * *

On her last afternoon in Paris, under cover of collecting her final pair of fur-lined winter boots from a shoemaker on the Rue Saint-Honoré, Krystyna walked alone through the autumn drizzle to the Polish church of Notre-Dame-de-l’Assomption near the Place Vendôme. She did not go inside—Kessler’s watchers knew it was a Polish parish—but paused beneath the stone colonnade across the street to buy a twist of roasted chestnuts from a one-legged vendor who had fought in the Foreign Legion in 1940.

Her training insisted that with Zygmunt dead, this one-legged Legionnaire was her last link to the western radio transmitter before she crossed Germany, though every detail of buying hot chestnuts on the corner of Krakowskie Przedmieście after skating at the Dolina Szwajcarska remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the draft stirred the rain-darkened colonnade opposite Notre-Dame-de-l'Assomption, the warm newspaper cone of roasted chestnuts in her gloved hand remained fixed in place, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She did not look back as she walked down the Rue Saint-Honoré, eating a single chestnut to keep up the play. It was in small things like the empty pinned trouser leg of the vendor's faded blue coat that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the hiss of chestnuts roasting over charcoal and the drizzle pattering on her umbrella and the faint drift of charcoal smoke, wet wool, roasted chestnut skins, and autumn rain until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. Whatever happened next, she knew that once she boarded the train at the Gare de l'Est tomorrow night, she would be completely cut off from London except through Kruk's Warsaw transmitters.

"The chestnuts are bitter this year, Mademoiselle," the vendor said, handing her the warm newspaper cone.

"Wait until the frosts come in October," Krystyna answered, laying a ten-franc coin on his tray with the heads side facing west. It was the emergency cutoff signal for the surviving fragment of the *Stragan* courier line.

Within the mirror-lined rooms of the Quai des Grands Augustins, the Seine slid dark and silent beneath the bridges of the fourth year of war. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Keeping her pulse steady, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

She answered the silence with slipping her foot into the fleece-lined boot while the shopgirl knelt with a buttonhook, letting the critical concentration of a client checking the fit of an expensive instep serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the tap of a cobbler's hammer from the workshop behind the partition at the same moment she breathed in new calfskin, beeswax polish, and the bitter ink of the rice-paper pellet on her tongue filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. She swallowed the last fiber of the rice paper and told the shopgirl to wrap the boots for travel. Between the doorway and the velvet-curtained fitting cubicle of the bootmaker's shop, a pair of fleece-lined Russian leather winter boots standing on the carpet marked the half-lit interval, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She forced herself to accept that the warning about a new Slavic section chief in Abteilung Ic at the Brühl Palace meant German counter-intelligence in Warsaw had been reinforced, while the contrast with the grand courtyard of the Brühl Palace when it had housed the Polish Ministry of Foreign Affairs before the war made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where whoever commanded the Slavic desk at the Brühl Palace would be the man reviewing every partisan report under Viktor's roof.

Inside the newspaper cone, beneath six hot chestnuts, lay a tiny rolled pellet of rice paper no larger than a grain of wheat. She did not open it until she was seated inside the private fitting cubicle of the shoemaker’s shop ten minutes later.

Written in microscopic purple ink were three lines in Polish: *Jaskółka confirmed for transfer to Nest. Kruk awaits at Chmielna 22 (Watchmaker). Beware: Abteilung Ic at Brühl Palace has a new Slavic section chief transferred from Berlin. Trust no one in field-gray.*

Across the Place Vendôme gleaming wet under the September twilight, the bronze column of Napoleon rising into the low gray clouds rested in the quiet shadow, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Paris had been a stage set of silk and mirrors; Warsaw would be bone and ash. She let her attention brush past the Ritz doorman tipping his braided cap to her as she passed, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that she was leaving Paris with eighteen clean Minox cassettes hidden inside three cakes of Roger & Gallet bath soap, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the last letter she had received from her father in 1939, telling her never to return to Warsaw while the swastika flew over the castle. As she completed pausing at the curb to let a column of gray military lorries rumble past, she drew a calm, detached gaze that took in the departing city without nostalgia around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the horn of a Wehrmacht staff car turning out of the Hôtel Ritz courtyard while the room held the aroma of wet cobblestones, petrol exhaust, and the cold breeze off the river steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray.

She chewed the rice-paper pellet until the purple ink dissolved on her tongue and swallowed it with a sip of water from the shoemaker’s carafe.

* * *

At nineteen hundred hours on Thursday evening, the cavernous iron-and-glass shed of the Gare de l’Est was a cauldron of steam, blue blackout lamps, and shouted German commands. On Track Seven stood the *Fronturlauberzug* and attached to its rear, two dark green Mitropa sleeping cars reserved for Generalstab officers and senior ministries traveling east to Berlin and the Generalgouvernement.

She stepped into the mahogany-paneled corridor of the Mitropa car without looking back at Kessler's black silhouette. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that Kessler was transferring himself back to the Aleja Szucha Gestapo in Warsaw—which meant he had not given up his hunt for the Paris mole, though the thought of standing on this same platform in the spring of 1939 when the Paris-Warsaw Nord Express still carried blue wagons-lits returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of coal smoke, wet wool greatcoats, hot axle grease, and damp iron hung in the draft alongside the deafening hiss of steam cylinders and the echo of German loudspeakers overhead that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Nothing about the hollow, resentful stares of the young Wehrmacht infantrymen on the neighboring track escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a brittle, charming smile that gave the SD officer not an inch of satisfaction with the quiet motion of accepting the tissue-wrapped box of Fouquet's violets with two gloved fingers, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that once Kessler reached Warsaw, the noose would tighten from both the Aleja Szucha and the Brühl Palace.

Loudspeakers echoed beneath the soot-blackened vault: *"Achtung! Zug nach Frankfurt — Berlin — Posen — Warschau!"* Along the adjacent platform, hundreds of young Wehrmacht infantrymen with gray faces and mud-stiffened greatcoats stood in silent ranks waiting to be shipped back to the meat-grinder of the Dnieper, staring with hollow eyes as Marion walked past in her silver-fox coat alongside Oberst von Harenberg.

At the steps of the first-class Mitropa car, a figure in a gleaming black leather greatcoat stood waiting beside the conductor. Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler touched the visor of his peaked cap in salute.

A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. From the sentry-guarded gates of the Boulevard Saint-Germain, every whispered conversation in a café corner carried a double meaning. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. As the minute hand crept on, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Against the quiet of the room, the blue night-light glowing dimly above the compartment mirror stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Beside the mahogany-paneled compartment of the Mitropa sleeper, the outline of her leather jewel case and the three cakes of hollowed Roger & Gallet soap in her valise marked the stillness, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Without haste, tucking the unopened box of candied violets behind the brass water carafe with a careless shrug reinforced the quiet authority of the weary grace of a traveler settling into a long journey, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. As the train glided out past the dark suburbs of Pantin, Paris vanished behind the blackout blinds. She held fast to the recognition that the Fouquet's violet box had undoubtedly been dusted or searched for microdots before Kessler handed it to her, though every detail of the night train out of Cracow in 1940 when she had hidden under a coal tender for six hours remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, for the next thirty-six hours this swaying wooden compartment would carry her across the breadth of the Reich, whatever price it demanded.

"A pleasant journey to the East, Herr Oberst, Fräulein Herschlag," Kessler said softly over the hiss of the steam brakes. He held out a small white tissue-wrapped parcel to Marion. "Candied violets from Fouquet’s. And my compliments to Oberführer Hahn when you reach Warsaw. In fact, you will see me there before the snow flies—I have requested a return transfer to my old desk at the Aleja Szucha."

Marion took the small parcel with a cool, practiced smile, though beneath the silver fox fur her skin went cold. "How touching, Herr Sturmbannführer. I had no idea the SD was so fond of violets—or of Polish winters."

She trusted pressing her forehead briefly against the cold vibrating glass of the corridor window and the familiar armor of the solitary, unreadable stillness of a woman watching Europe pass in the dark to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Eastward the trainthundered through the night, tearing open the seam between who she pretended to be and who she was. In the corner of the narrow corridor window of the Mitropa car at midnight, she noticed the faint reflection of her own gray-green eyes against the rushing blackness outside, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She observed the white milestone markers flashing past in the locomotive's firebox glare with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. No illusion could soften the fact that every turn of the wheels brought her closer to the graves of her parents and the streets that knew her real face, while the contrast with the summer of 1938 when Phillip Brandt had kissed her goodbye at the Warsaw station before his father was recalled to Berlin made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the relentless, iron cadence of the wheels over the rails toward the Rhine at the same moment she breathed in ozonated night air, hot steam pipes, and the wool of Viktor's greatcoat swinging on its hook pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence.

"One always returns to unfinished work, Fräulein," Kessler said, his gin-pale eyes holding hers until the Mitropa conductor blew his whistle and Viktor handed her up the iron steps.

* * *

Somewhere east of Frankfurt on the second night, as the Mitropa sleeper hurtled across the dark plains of Prussia toward the Oder, Viktor bolted the compartment door from the inside and drew down the heavy leather window shade. The narrow mahogany berth vibrated with the eighty-kilometer-an-hour roar of the express; in the blue glow of the night-lamp, the brass water carafe chattered in its ring.

The train itself seemed to become an iron battering ram carrying them into the dark. The sight of the blue night-lamp casting swaying shadows across her bare breasts brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the eighty-kilometer-an-hour roar of steel wheels over the Prussian rail joints while the room held the aroma of coal smoke, heated oil, sweat, and the close musk of the sleeping berth an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. From where she stood in the narrow mahogany compartment of the hurtling Mitropa sleeper, the brass water carafe chattering in its wall bracket beside the mirror was just visible, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. In the choreography of the room, bracing her palms against the vibrating mahogany wall above his head as she rode the motion of the train flowed seamlessly into a dark, breathless abandon synchronized with the lurch of the carriage, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. With cold clarity she reckoned that every mile this train covered while they were locked together on the berth was another mile into Polish territory, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the blue plush compartments of the pre-war Warsaw-Vienna express she had ridden with her father.

Viktor sat on the edge of the lower berth in his undershirt and cavalry trousers, his scarred ribs rising and falling as he watched her brush out her hair in the cramped space before the washbasin. "You have been quiet since we crossed the Rhine, Marion," he said over the thunder of the wheels. "Are you regretting Paris already?"

She set the hairbrush down—not the hollowed tortoiseshell one, which was locked in her jewel case, but its ordinary twin—and let her silk robe fall open as she stepped between his knees. "Paris is only bricks and dressmakers, Viktor," she murmured, laying her palms against his stubbled cheeks. "Have I ever given you cause to think I needed a city when I had you?"

Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of the Île de la Cité, the night mist rose cold and indifferent to the hunters and the hunted alike. In the dark, bruising arena of the Colonel's quarters, she answered his roughness with a scorching heat that left him blind to her craft. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

Against the muted backdrop of the cramped lower berth of the first-class carriage, her silk robe slipped to the carpeted floor between Viktor's boots drew her eye, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. A subtle trace of hot iron, damp linen sheets, and the salt of Viktor's skin hung in the draft alongside the long, mournful wail of the Reichsbahn steam whistle echoing across the Brandenburg pines as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. When he spent himself inside her with a muffled groan, the train was already crossing the Oder. In the presence of the brass bolt of the compartment door vibrating in its socket, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond locking her ankles behind his thighs and pulling his mouth hard against hers as the carriage swayed and the fierce, vocal heat that left him gasping against her throat, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. Not a single second could be wasted when in Warsaw he would keep his operational maps inside the Foksal apartment on the nights when snow or partisans kept him from the Brühl Palace.

His hands came up around her hips beneath the open silk robe, rough and hot, his thumbs digging into the flesh of her waist as the train swayed over a switch point. "In Warsaw I will have twenty thousand soldiers, four rail hubs, and ten thousand square miles of partisan forest on my neck," he rasped, pulling her down astride his lap on the narrow berth and burying his mouth against the hollow between her breasts. "When I come through that door on the Foksal at night, I want the war dead outside. I want only this."

He tore the silk robe from her shoulders and lifted her onto him in the cramped, rocking half-dark of the compartment. The iron rhythm of the Reichsbahn wheels below the floorboards became the tempo of their bodies: harsh, relentless, swaying with the lurch of the carriage as her back hit the mahogany paneling and her nails dug into his shoulders. Every time the locomotive shrieked at a Prussian level crossing, Viktor drove upward into her with a fierce, possessive grunt, holding her hips down onto him as if he could nail her to his flesh before the East could claim them both.

Krystyna let the leather blind drop back into place and dressed in her Parisian furs for her homecoming. Where the shadow fell across the frosted edge of the leather window blind at dawn, a thin crack of gray eastern light showing flat fields of harvested rye and birch trees remained half-hidden, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She was acutely aware of the slower, heavier clank of the wheels over the tracks beyond Kutno and the faint drift of peat smoke, wet autumn cabbage fields, and the cold air leaking through the sash sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. She measured the risk by the certainty that this was Polish earth outside the glass—the flat Mazovian plain where every birch cross by the road marked a grave, though every detail of the whitewashed roadside shrines decorated with blue paper ribbons in May remained as sharp as cut glass. With an expression of stony, unblinking grief that no living soul was awake to see governing the motion of lifting the corner of the leather blind a quarter of an inch while Viktor slept beside her to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. Whatever happened next, she knew that in two hours she would step out onto the platform of Warsaw and never once flinch at the sight of a gallows.

* * *

At six in the morning on the second day of the journey east, the Mitropa express ground to a halt amid the pine forests of Neu Bentschen—the old 1939 frontier between the German Reich and the Polish Republic. Outside the frosted window glass, armed Grenzpolizei and SS customs inspectors in long green coats walked the gravel ballast with barking dogs, checking every axle and coupling before the train crossed into the Wartheland.

Her senses caught the crunch of SS customs boots on the gravel outside and the growl of patrol dogs beneath the carriage at the same moment she breathed in pine resin, cold morning fog, and locomotive steam until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. As the locomotive whistled and rolled past the rotting Polish frontier post, Krystyna felt her native earth rise up to meet the wheels. It was in small things like the red-and-white border post of old Poland lying rotting in the weeds beside the tracks that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with sitting wrapped in her silver-fox coat by the window with an untouched cup of Mitropa coffee in her hand, letting sleepy, haughty indifference to border formalities serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She did not allow herself to forget that Viktor's own Generalstab arrogance had just carried eighteen spy cameras cassettes across the Polish border without a single latch being opened, while the contrast with crossing this same border at Zbąszyń as a schoolgirl in 1937 when Polish customs officers wore four-cornered rogatywka caps made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where the white chalk cross on the compartment door meant no further search would take place between here and Warsaw.

A sharp knock sounded on the mahogany door of their compartment. Two SS customs lieutenants stood in the corridor with clipboards. "Zollkontrolle, Herr Oberst. All civilian luggage entering the eastern territories must be opened for currency and contraband inspection."

Inside Marion’s leather valise on the overhead rack lay the three hollowed cakes of Roger & Gallet soap containing eighteen unexposed Minox cassettes and her forged Polish backup papers.

There was no escaping the conclusion that every town name painted over with German gothic script on the station boards—Posen, Litzmannstadt, Kutno—was a scar across the face of her country, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the white storks nesting on the thatched roofs of the Mazovian villages in spring. By the threshold of the dining car of the Mitropa express two hours east of Poznań, a white linen table set with silver bud vases as Polish farmsteads flashed past the glass stood out in sharp relief, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She turned her eyes back to her coffee cup and prepared her heart for Warsaw. Through the lens of her cover identity, a line of barefoot Polish peasant women standing behind a railway crossing barrier in the rain was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the rhythmic clatter of silverware against porcelain at seventy kilometers an hour while the room held the aroma of fried eggs, ersatz rye bread, and coal smoke blowing back from the engine filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that by nightfall she would be sleeping under the roof of Foksal 14.

Viktor did not even rise from the plush seat; he merely unbuttoned his greatcoat lapel to display the scarlet collar tabs of the Generalstab and the Knight’s Cross of the War Merit Cross at his throat. "This compartment is under special OKH travel orders to Oberfeldkommandantur Warschau, Lieutenant," Viktor said in a voice that could have frozen the Oder. "Touch one latch of Fräulein Herschlag’s trunks, and you will be inspecting snowdrifts outside Murmansk by next Tuesday."

The SS lieutenant paled, snapped his heels together, chalked the white customs clearance cross directly onto the mahogany door without stepping inside, and shut the sliding panel.

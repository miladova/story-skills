# Chapter 7 — The Officer on the Stair

*Warsaw — Late October 1943*

---

The Brühl Palace on Plac Piłsudskiego—once the Rococo jewel of the Polish Foreign Ministry, now the seat of the German Governor of the Warsaw District and the senior Wehrmacht staff—blazed behind its blackout curtains on the night of October twenty-fourth. Floodlights hidden beneath the portico illuminated red swastika banners twenty feet long, while SS honor guards in black steel helmets flanked the marble steps.

She felt the marble floor of the Brühl Palace tilt beneath her satin slippers and forced her spine to remain like iron. To anyone else, the heavy cabochon emeralds resting cold against the pulse at the base of her throat might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the string quartet in the gallery playing Mozart's Kleine Nachtmusik above the hum of German voices while the room held the aroma of beeswax, wet wool greatcoats from the cloakroom, expensive cigars, and hothouse chrysanthemums sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. By the threshold of the marble landing of the Brühl Palace grand staircase, the twenty-foot crimson swastika banner hanging beside the Rococo mirrors stood out in sharp relief, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. As she completed resting her white-gloved hand lightly on Viktor's scarlet-striped sleeve as they crossed the parquet, she drew the radiant, untouchable poise of the Colonel's Parisian jewel around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She held fast to the recognition that in ten seconds she would look into the face of the boy who had held her heart five years ago, and neither of them could afford to blink, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of dancing with her father in this very ballroom in January 1938 at the Foreign Ministry diplomatic reception.

When Marion Herschlag ascended the grand staircase on the arm of Oberst von Harenberg, the conversation in the upper gallery hushed by halves. She wore bias-cut white duchesse satin that left her shoulders and back bare to the waist, her throat encircled by the heavy cabochon emeralds Viktor had bought her in the Place Vendôme, her gold hair swept high in a sculptural chignon. Against the drab field-gray of the Wehrmacht and the funereal black of the SS, she looked like a blade of white fire.

"Every eye in the room is on you," Viktor murmured with dark satisfaction as they reached the landing of the Ballroom of Mirrors. "Even General Stahel is craning his neck."

Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Under the hooded blue lamps of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. In the amber half-light, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

In the hush that filled the tall marble columns of the winter salon, the crystal wineglass held in Hauptmann Phillip Brandt's right hand appeared almost motionless, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. A subtle trace of pine needles, French champagne, and the familiar, impossible presence of him three feet away hung in the draft alongside the sudden, deafening roar of her own blood in her ears beneath the Mozart strings until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. For half a heartbeat, the crystal stem of Phillip's wineglass creaked between his whitening fingers. It was in small things like the silver braid of a Wehrmacht Hauptmann's shoulder boards on the man who had once kissed every finger of her hand that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a cool, gracious social smile that revealed nothing but polite curiosity with the quiet motion of extending her gloved right hand at the exact angle a stranger extends a hand to a subordinate officer, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that if Phillip spoke the name 'Krystyna' aloud, neither of them would leave the Brühl Palace except in chains.

"They have been looking at turnip-fed Pomeranian wives for three years, Viktor," Marion replied lightly, touching the emerald at her hollow throat. "Do not leave me stranded with the civilian administrators for too long."

"Come," he said, steering her past the crystal chandeliers toward a group of intelligence officers standing near the tall marble columns of the winter salon. "First you must meet my Section Ic staff. They are the men who keep the partisans off my rail lines. Especially my new chief of Slavic counter-espionage—he grew up in Warsaw before the war, so he knows how these Polish fanatics think."

When Phillip raised his head from her hand, his dark eyes were like banked coals in a field of snow. Past the edge of the circle of Abteilung Ic officers beneath the chandelier, the Iron Cross Second Class ribbon threaded through Phillip's second buttonhole waited in the gloom, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She was acutely aware of the click of Phillip's heels snapping together as he bowed over her extended hand and the faint drift of warm candle wax, starch, and the faint trace of the cedar soap he had always used filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. With cold clarity she reckoned that he had stopped himself on the very brink of the precipice: not a word left his lips except the formal German greeting, though every detail of the night in August 1938 when he had pressed his forehead against hers and sworn in Polish: 'Wait for me until the storm passes' remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, letting her gloved fingers brush his palm for the exact half-second protocol allowed before withdrawing reinforced the quiet authority of the composed, slightly aloof grace of an Alsatian lady acknowledging a captain, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, in that fraction of a second as his lips touched the air above her glove, she felt the violent tremor running through his hand, whatever price it demanded.

The circle of field-gray tunics parted. Standing with his back to the marble column was a tall captain of twenty-eight with broad shoulders, dark gold-brown hair, and a finely cut, aristocratic mouth that had forgotten how to smile. As Viktor spoke his name—"Hauptmann Phillip Brandt, Fräulein Marion Herschlag"—the young officer turned, glass in hand, and looked straight into her eyes.

* * *

"Gnädiges Fräulein," Phillip said. His voice was lower than it had been at twenty-three, roughened by smoke and winter campaigns, yet every cadence of it went through Krystyna’s ribs like a heated wire. "Welcome to Warsaw. I trust the journey from Paris was not too exhausting."

Her senses caught the scrape of a cello bow from the gallery and Viktor's booming laugh at the same moment she breathed in champagne bubbles, hot wax, and the sudden electric stillness between her and Phillip steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. "How tragic," Marion murmured smoothly. "Though at least she left a very good Bechstein behind in the drawing room." She let her attention brush past the whiteness around Phillip's knuckles where he gripped his wineglass stem, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted lowering her champagne glass slowly and tilting her head with polite, detached sympathy and the familiar armor of the mild, transient curiosity of a newcomer hearing an old ghost story about her house to see her through, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. She knew beyond doubt that with a single sentence—'the daughter died during the bombing in September thirty-nine'—Phillip had just built a wall of steel around her life, while the contrast with the afternoon in September 1939 when a bomb had indeed collapsed the east wing of Foksal 14 while she was at the hospital made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when Phillip had not only recognized her instantly; he had lied to his commanding officer's face to protect her before knowing why she was here.

"The trains are tolerable, Herr Hauptmann," Marion answered in her clear Strasbourg German, her chin high, her gray-green eyes meeting his without wavering, "though the scenery east of Berlin is terribly monotonous. Viktor tells me you lived in Warsaw before the war. How strange for you to return to it now."

"A great deal has been torn down since thirty-eight, Fräulein Herschlag," Phillip said evenly, though the muscle along his lean jaw flexed once. "One walks down a street one thought one knew by heart, and finds only strangers living in the old houses."

Past the iron railings of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. Yet in the stolen, breathless stillness with Phillip, every wall she had built to survive the war dissolved into pure truth. Steeling her resolve once more, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

She measured the risk by the certainty that when she said 'at least she left a very good Bechstein behind,' she saw a flash of raw anguish cross Phillip's dark eyes at the cruelty of the mask she had to wear, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of playing Schumann's Träumerei for Phillip on that same Bechstein while the summer rain fell in the Foksal garden. Not three paces from the polished parquet between Viktor and his intelligence captain, Viktor's heavy hand resting on Phillip's field-gray shoulder lay in the amber half-light, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. "Brandt," Viktor said, turning toward the general, "fetch Fräulein Herschlag a glass of Tokaj and show her the winter terrace." Nothing about the reflection of her bare white back and Phillip's dark eyes in the tall Rococo mirror behind them escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the clink of medals as an SS general approached to claim Viktor's attention while the room held the aroma of cigar smoke, French perfume, and the scorching, unspoken gravity pulling her eyes toward Phillip's that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Before the night was over, Viktor was about to leave her alone with Phillip for three minutes while he conferred with the SS, and she intended to be ready.

Viktor clapped a heavy hand on Phillip’s shoulder—the gesture of a commander proud of both his prize thoroughbred and his sharpest staff officer. "Brandt’s father had the Meissen porcelain agency here for fourteen years, Marion. And the estate next to the old Stankiewicz villa out in Konstancin. In fact, Brandt—did you not know the Stankiewicz family whose house I have taken on the Foksal?"

The air between the three of them seemed to turn to solid glass. Krystyna did not breathe; she lifted her champagne flute to her lips and took a slow, measured sip.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond walking beside him without letting her white satin skirt brush his boots and the polite, unhurried stroll of a guest being shown the architecture, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. A subtle trace of night-blooming jasmine, damp autumn stone from the courtyard below, and the heat of Phillip's sleeve an inch from her bare arm hung in the draft alongside the murmur of the ballroom receding behind the potted palms and marble pilasters that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Neither of them spoke until the shadow of the tall Corinthian column fell between them and the ballroom. Framed by the geometry of the arched threshold leading to the enclosed glass winter terrace, two tall flutes of amber Hungarian Tokaj on a silver salver seemed almost innocuous, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She did not allow herself to forget that they were walking side by side under the eyes of fifty German officers, separated by half an inch of air and five years of hell, though the thought of walking side by side down the lime alley at Konstancin on the night he first kissed her mouth returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when they had less than ninety seconds behind the marble pilaster of the winter terrace where the mirrors did not reach.

Phillip did not look away from her eyes. "I knew them, Herr Oberst," he said quietly. "Count Stankiewicz was shot at Palmiry in forty. The Countess died in Pawiak. And the daughter—Krystyna—died during the bombing in September thirty-nine. There is no one left of that house."

* * *

Behind the fluted marble column of the winter terrace, out of sight of the ballroom mirrors and facing only the rain-blackened courtyard of the Brühl Palace below, Phillip stopped. He set the two glasses of Tokaj down on the stone balustrade because his hand was finally shaking so badly the crystal chimed against the marble.

Where the draft stirred the shadowed marble balustrade of the Brühl Palace winter terrace, two untouched flutes of amber Tokaj trembling slightly on the stone ledge remained fixed in place, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. The five years between 1938 and 1943 vanished like smoke, leaving only a wound that had never healed. She observed the dark, bottomless grief in Phillip's eyes as he looked at the emeralds around her throat with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. There was no escaping the conclusion that he had noticed the faint bruise under her pearl bracelet inside thirty seconds—because he had always looked at her as if every inch of her skin mattered, though every detail of Phillip binding her cut wrist with his linen handkerchief in the Konstancin orchard when they were seventeen remained as sharp as cut glass. With a profile carved from white ice while her heart hammered at two hundred beats a minute governing the motion of keeping her hands locked behind her back on the cold marble so she would not touch his cheek to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, letting her fingers tremble for the only man who knew her true name and held it sacred. She was acutely aware of the rain drumming against the glass roof of the conservatory and the distant waltz from the ballroom and the faint drift of wet stone, night jasmine, and the dizzying, aching nearness of the boy who had owned her youth pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence.

He did not touch her—to touch Oberst von Harenberg’s mistress in the Brühl Palace was death—but he stood so close that the heat of his body radiated across her bare shoulders like a stove in winter. His dark eyes moved over her face with a starving, incredulous agony, tracing the line of her cheekbones, her mouth, the small mole just beneath her left ear that no false passport could erase.

"Krysiu," he breathed. It was barely a puff of air against her cheek, spoken in the tender Polish vocative that no living soul had used to her since her mother died.

A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Behind the double-bolted doors of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. Whenever Phillip's hand found hers in the shadows, five winters of exile burned away in a passion more perilous than any bullet. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. While the night deepened outside, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

"Look at the uniform I am wearing, Phillip," she breathed, "and look at the gown I am wearing. Which of us is in deeper hell?" Her training insisted that he had gone to Ulica Foksal in October 1939 looking for her in the ruins while Warsaw was still burning, while the contrast with the charred beams of the east wing that she had climbed over on the night she fled south to Hungary made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the crunch of an SS guard patrol changing watch below the portico at the same moment she breathed in damp autumn leaves from the Saxon Garden next door and the warm cedar scent of his skin an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. The sight of the fine lines of sleeplessness etched around the corners of his twenty-eight-year-old eyes brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with lifting her chin so the light from the ballroom caught the hard, defiant glint in her gray-green eyes, letting the mask of a woman who asked for no forgiveness and offered no apology serve as her shield, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. Time was narrowing toward the point where she could not tell him she was Armia Krajowa yet, though every instinct in her soul cried out that he was still Phillip.

For the first time in three years, Krystyna felt the ice inside her chest crack from top to bottom. Her knees went weak beneath the white satin; her fingers gripped the cold marble balustrade behind her to keep from reaching for his face. "Do not call me that," she whispered in German, her eyes fixed on the ballroom entrance while her throat burned with unshed tears. "Krystyna Stankiewiczówna died in thirty-nine. You just told the Colonel so yourself."

"I went to the Foksal in October thirty-nine, three days after the capitulation," Phillip whispered fiercely, his gaze locked on her profile as if he were memorizing her before a firing squad. "The east wing was rubble. Your old gatekeeper told me you had vanished toward Lwów and the Russians had taken the trains. For four years I thought you were in an unmarked grave—and tonight you walk down that staircase wearing Harenberg’s emeralds and his bruises on your wrist."

In the presence of the small white scar above his right eyebrow catching the chandelier light, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Across the Corinthian column separating the terrace from the ballroom doors, her white satin train pooled beside the toe of his polished boot rested in the quiet shadow, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. As she completed snatching her hand back Half an inch to pick up the glass of Tokaj just as footsteps approached the palms, she drew the cool, composed tilt of her head as she laughed lightly for the benefit of the approaching officers around herself like silk, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. "Thursday at four," Phillip murmured under his breath as he lifted his glass in a formal toast. "The glass conservatory behind the Saxon Garden fountain." She forced herself to accept that that single accidental brush of his bare knuckles against her hand sent a shock of pure, luminous fire straight up her arm to her heart, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the way his hands had felt cupping her face under the linden trees before the world broke. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that they had to find a place in this city where there were no mirrors and no uniforms before the silence killed them.

Her left wrist twitched involuntarily against her satin skirt where a faint shadow from the Mitropa train still showed beneath her pearl bracelet. "And you wear his uniform, Phillip," she whispered back, turning her head at last to look straight into his dark, tortured eyes. "Why did you not denounce me two minutes ago?"

* * *

Viktor stepped through the archway of the winter terrace a second later, accompanied by a heavyset Wehrmacht major general from the Ostbahn directorate. "There you are, Marion," Viktor said, his blue eyes sweeping swiftly from her composed face to Hauptmann Brandt’s formal posture three paces away. "Has Brandt been boring you with pre-war Warsaw history?"

Combining the glittering, mercenary vanity of a mistress paraded before the high command with the quiet motion of leaning lightly into Viktor's side and laughing at the major general's compliment, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She kept her head high as Viktor spun her onto the parquet for the first waltz. Within the archway between the winter terrace and the Ballroom of Mirrors, her gaze returned briefly to Viktor's broad hand splayed low across the bare skin of her back, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. To anyone else, the stillness of Phillip's tall figure standing alone beside the marble column behind them might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that she had just planted her cover excuse for walking in the Saxon Garden right in front of Viktor and the Ostbahn general, though the thought of the stone sundial and fountain in the Saxon Garden where her governess had walked her as a child returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of Crimean caviar, lemon, champagne, and the suffocating heat of the crowded salon hung in the draft alongside the brass orchestra striking up the Blue Danube waltz in the main gallery sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

"On the contrary, Viktor," Marion said, taking a sip of the golden Tokaj and smiling lazily over the rim at the two senior officers. "Hauptmann Brandt was just telling me which streets have the best antique dealers left, since you refuse to let me bring my Sèvres clock from Paris. Though he tells me the Saxon Garden is the only respectable place left to walk inside the city."

"Keep to the German paths if you walk there," the major general advised jovially, bowing over her hand. "Though with Harenberg’s name on your pass and Brandt’s counter-intelligence boys watching the gates, you are safer than the Governor himself."

When her eyes met Phillip's in the mirror for a tenth of a second, she looked away first to save his life. It was in small things like the cold blue stare Viktor directed at every junior officer they danced past that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the swish of white duchesse satin and the rhythmic three-four beat of Strauss and the faint drift of beeswax, hot tungsten chandeliers, and Viktor's vetiver cologne until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. Beside the center of the mirrored ballroom floor, the outline of the cabochon emeralds swinging cold against her collarbone as they turned marked the stillness, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Without haste, resting her cheek near Viktor's silver-braided shoulder as they whirled past the intelligence staff reinforced the quiet authority of the dazzling, half-closed eyes of a woman lost in the music and the luxury, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that every turn of the waltz brought Phillip's reflection into the tall mirrors along the wall—standing by the buffet, watching her without touching his glass, though every detail of dancing with Phillip on the wooden veranda at Konstancin to a wind-up gramophone in August 1938 remained as sharp as cut glass.

"Brandt knows his business," Viktor agreed, resting his heavy hand on the bare curve of Marion’s waist and drawing her back against his side right in front of the younger man. "Come along, Liebchen. Governor Fischer is opening the buffet, and I promised you caviar from the Crimea."

As Viktor steered her back into the blaze of the Ballroom of Mirrors with his palm splayed low and possessive across her naked spine, Krystyna did not look back. Yet she felt Phillip’s eyes burning between her shoulder blades across the entire length of the room—watching another man’s hand claim the skin that had once belonged only to him.

In the corner of the portico of the Brühl Palace at one in the morning, she noticed her silver-fox coat draped over her white satin shoulders by Corporal Hanke, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Her senses caught the roar of staff cars starting in the wet square of Plac Piłsudskiego at the same moment she breathed in cold October mist, petrol exhaust, and the wet gravel of the Saxon square filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. As the car turned toward Nowy Świat, Viktor's hand slid beneath her silver-fox coat onto her thigh. Through the lens of her cover identity, the solitary silhouette of a Wehrmacht captain standing under the Brühl portico watching their tail-lamps pull away was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted stepping into the warm rear cabin of the Mercedes as Viktor followed and the familiar armor of a sleepy, contented yawn behind her white kid glove to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when she had to survive four more nights in Viktor's bed on Ulica Foksal before she could hear Phillip's voice again.

* * *

All day Monday and Tuesday, the autumn rain fell in cold, vertical sheets over Warsaw, turning the Vistula the color of wet lead. Krystyna did not go near the Saxon Garden; a spy who rushes to a rendezvous two days early only leads the watchers to the trap. Instead, on Wednesday morning, she sent a pile of Viktor’s dress shirts and her own silk slips to the laundry of the Hotel Europejski, three blocks from the Brühl Palace, and walked over at noon to complain to the housekeeper about a scorched lace hem.

That single hidden squeeze of their fingers carried five years of shared grief and unbroken loyalty. From where she stood in the steam-filled second-floor linen corridor of the Hotel Europejski, the stack of freshly ironed silk slips folded over stiff gray cardboard was just visible, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She listened to the hiss of industrial steam presses from the basement and the ring of the service elevator while the room held the aroma of hot starch, damp wool uniforms drying on racks, bleach, and wet umbrellas steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. No illusion could soften the fact that seeing little Wanda Zieleńska alive and serving as her courier inside the German officers' hotel brought a fierce lump to her throat, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Wanda at fourteen sitting on the floor at Konstancin with a mouthful of brass pins, hemming Krystyna's summer dresses. In the choreography of the room, inspecting the mended lace hem of the slip with a critical, frowning brow flowed seamlessly into the haughty, demanding manner of an officer's mistress scolding a clumsy maid, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Before the night was over, the fuel-depot tracing now in Wanda's apron pocket would be in Tadeusz's hands on Ulica Chmielna before two o'clock, and she intended to be ready.

In the steam-filled linen corridor on the second floor of the Hotel Europejski, while the German floor supervisor was answering the service telephone in her booth, a slender nineteen-year-old girl with a braid of auburn hair and a spray of freckles across her nose stepped out of the drying room carrying a stack of monogrammed sheets.

It was Wanda Zieleńska—"Sarna."

A subtle trace of Turkish tobacco, wet leather greatcoats, and roasted barley coffee hung in the draft alongside the clatter of coffee cups from the officers' café and the rain lashing the plate glass that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She walked out through the revolving door and turned south toward Nowy Świat without a second glance. Nothing about the wet gray paving stones of the square leading toward the iron railings of the Saxon Garden escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond pausing by the hotel florist stall to buy a bunch of white chrysanthemums before stepping out into the rain and the leisurely, unhurried movements of a woman with nothing on her conscience, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. With cold clarity she reckoned that in the reflection of the lobby mirror she spotted a pale young clerk in a gray Loden coat reading a newspaper without turning the page, though the thought of Tadeusz's warning that the Brühl Palace counter-espionage section watched everyone who lived with senior commanders returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when if the clerk in the Loden coat followed her down Krakowskie Przedmieście, she had to know whether he answered to Kessler or to Phillip.

For half a second Wanda’s gray eyes went huge above the stack of linen; her lips formed the silent Polish words *Panienka Krystyna* before she caught herself and bobbed a deep, servant’s curtsy. "Verzeihung, gnädige Frau," Wanda said in halting hotel German, holding out the freshly pressed silk slip folded over cardboard. "The lace is mended."

Inside the folded cardboard backing of the slip, Krystyna’s thumb felt the stiff ridge of a concealed dispatch slip from Tadeusz. Simultaneously, Krystyna slipped a folded hundred-zloty note into Wanda’s apron pocket—wrapped around the carbon tracing she had made at 3:00 AM of Viktor’s Ostbahn fuel-depot allocations for November.

"See that your iron is cooler next time, girl," Marion said coldly in German as the floor supervisor stepped out of her glass booth, while for the barest instant her fingers squeezed Wanda’s chapped, red-knuckled hand.

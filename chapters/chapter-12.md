# Chapter 12 — Winter Lindens

*Konstancin-Jeziorna — Mid-December 1943*

---

Two weeks before Christmas, deep snow blanketed the pine forests south of Warsaw. On Friday morning, Viktor announced that he had requisitioned a country hunting villa twelve miles south of the city at Konstancin-Jeziorna for a weekend staff conference on winter partisan sweeps—and that Marion would accompany him as hostess.

In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. In the long shadow cast by the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Letting the silence work for her, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Across the snow-drifted gravel drive of Willa pod Lipami in Konstancin, the wrought-iron gate piers topped with stone pinecones that she had touched every summer of her youth rested in the quiet shadow, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Across the hood of the second car, Phillip's dark eyes met hers for a fraction of a second—steady, warning, and protective. Through the lens of her cover identity, the wooden German sign 'ERHOLUNGSHEIM DER WEHRMACHT' nailed over the old stone tablet was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. No illusion could soften the fact that Viktor had unknowingly brought both her and Phillip back to the Stankiewicz summer estate where they had grown up side by side, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of running barefoot down this very drive at age nineteen with Phillip chasing her through the fallen linden blossoms. As she completed drawing a slow breath of the freezing pine air into her lungs before stepping onto the veranda steps, she drew the cool, appraising curiosity of a Parisian woman inspecting a rustic Polish country house around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the crunch of three Wehrmacht staff cars braking in the deep December snow while the room held the aroma of pine resin, woodsmoke from the villa chimneys, and the crisp frost off the Jeziorka river filtering through the heavy draperies without cease.

Beneath the shadowed trees of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Krystyna did not learn which villa it was until the three black Wehrmacht Mercedes staff cars turned off the Wilanów road into the snow-laden avenue of ancient linden trees along the Jeziorka river, and pulled up before the wrought-iron gates of *Willa pod Lipami*.

Her own childhood summer home.

A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Without haste, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

"Quite charming, Viktor," Marion said in German. "Almost like a Bavarian hunting lodge, if one ignores the drafts." With cold clarity she reckoned that every floorboard in this house had a voice, and she had to walk across them as if she had never seen them before, though the thought of her mother standing at the foot of these stairs in a white linen dress calling them in from the river for tea returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of burning pine knots, wet wool, and the beeswax on the stairs she knew blindfolded hung in the draft alongside the roar of pine logs burning in the great stone fireplace and the stamping of snowy jackboots steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She let her attention brush past the carved wooden bear umbrella-stand by the door that still had Phillip's initials scratched under its paw from 1931, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a pleased, slightly condescending smile at the rustic warmth of the hall with the quiet motion of holding her gloved hands out toward the hearth fire while the orderly took her lynx coat, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that Viktor had assigned himself the master suite on the south gallery and quartered his staff captains down the same corridor.

Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Above the slated mansard roofs of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. As the minute hand crept on, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

And stepping out of the second staff car behind Major Kleist, carrying a leather map-case under his arm, was Hauptmann Phillip Brandt.

Standing in the snow beside Viktor’s running board in her white lynx-trimmed coat, Krystyna looked up at the timbered verandas, the octagonal turret room that had been her nursery, and the frozen orchard stretching down to the river—and realized that fate had brought the three of them back to the exact acre of earth where her life with Phillip had begun.

* * *

All Friday evening, Viktor and his six staff officers sat around the long oak dining table—where Krystyna had eaten Christmas Eve wafers with her parents—with red-penciled maps of the Kampinos and Parczew forests spread between the candlesticks. Marion withdrew to the drawing room after coffee, playing solitaire by the fire while through the open archway she listened to every word of the counter-insurgency briefing.

Near the iron-grated cellars of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. In that narrow pause between beats, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Nothing about the shadow of Phillip's shoulders reflected in the glass bookcase across the hall escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Beside the drawing-room hearth of Willa pod Lipami on Friday night, the outline of a pack of French playing cards laid out in seven columns on the green baize table marked the stillness, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Without haste, turning over the Queen of Spades with an unhurried flick of her fingernail while memorizing every sector named at the dining table reinforced the quiet authority of the absorbed, solitary boredom of a woman left to amuse herself with cards, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Not once did Phillip look through the archway while the majors were watching. She knew beyond doubt that Kessler was arriving on December twentieth to coordinate a massive pre-New Year arrest sweep of the Warsaw Home Army, though every detail of playing bridge at this same table with her father, Heinrich Brandt, and Phillip in August 1938 remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, if Phillip was assigned to prepare the liaison files for Kessler, he would see the SD arrest lists twenty-four hours before the trucks rolled, whatever price it demanded.

A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. As the shadows lengthened, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

"The Reichsführer-SS wants a general roundup of suspected Home Army command posts in the Warsaw district before New Year’s Day," Major Kleist was saying over the maps. "Sturmbannführer Kessler arrives from Berlin on the twentieth to coordinate the SD arrest lists with our Abteilung Ic."

"Kessler again," Viktor growled, tapping his cigar ash. "Give him whatever routine partisan sighting reports he asks for, Brandt, and keep him out of my rail-defense files."

Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Past the iron railings of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. In the hush that followed, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

She trusted leaving a single line of footprints down the orchard path as if taking a solitary winter walk to the river view and the familiar armor of the calm, unhurried pace of a guest exploring the grounds to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She pushed open the weathered plank door of the boathouse and stepped inside out of the wind. In the corner of the snow-covered linden orchard sloping down to the frozen Jeziorka river, she noticed the ancient, snow-laden branches of the linden trees forming a white tunnel overhead, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Against the quiet of the room, the stone bench beneath the largest linden tree buried to its armrests in snow stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She measured the risk by the certainty that she was walking down the exact path where Phillip had first told her he loved her in the summer of 1938, while the contrast with the warm July dusk when the linden blossoms had smelled of wild honey and Phillip had pinned one into her braid made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the distant, muffled crack of a hunting rifle two miles away in the Chojnów woods at the same moment she breathed in clean December snow, frozen river reeds, and birch bark that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike.

Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Near the sandbagged corners of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

"Jawohl, Herr Oberst," Phillip’s quiet, steady voice answered from the foot of the table. "I will personally prepare the liaison files for Sturmbannführer Kessler."

At four o'clock on Saturday afternoon, when Viktor and the majors rode out on sleighs to shoot wild boar in the Chojnów forest reserves, Phillip remained behind at the villa on the pretext of decoding a teleprinter pouch from Cracow. Ten minutes after the sleigh bells faded into the pines, Krystyna slipped on her lynx coat and walked down through the snow-buried linden orchard toward the old wooden boathouse on the frozen Jeziorka river.

* * *

Inside the old timber boathouse, the gray winter light filtered through the slatted shutters across the dry slip where her father’s varnished skiff still lay overturned on trestles. Phillip was already there, standing beside the skiff with his greatcoat collar turned up against the frost. He had come down through the pine screen along the boundary wall—the old shortcut between the Brandt villa and the Stankiewicz orchard that they had worn smooth with their bicycles at fifteen.

When his warm palms slid beneath her sweater against her bare ribs, she felt her heart expand until it ached. She observed the frost crystals glittering on the shoulders of Phillip's field-gray wool with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the creak of the frozen river ice shifting beneath the pilings below while the room held the aroma of old river reeds, cedar tar, winter frost, and the scorching warmth inside Phillip's open greatcoat pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. From where she stood in the dim, frost-stilled interior of the Jeziorka boathouse, her father's overturned varnished wooden skiff resting on oak trestles was just visible, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. In the choreography of the room, opening her coat to press her whole body into the shelter of his chest and arms flowed seamlessly into the raw, trembling devotion she gave to the only man she had ever loved, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. She did not allow herself to forget that in this boathouse where they had hidden from summer rain as children, every touch of his hands restored the soul she had thought dead, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the carved initials 'K.S. + P.B. 1937' hidden under the bark of the third linden tree up the slope.

A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. Where five years of longing deepened into an all-consuming love, the boy from the Konstancin lindens had become the man she would die beside. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Anchored by her silent oath, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

When the plank door latched shut behind her, neither of them spoke a word. Phillip crossed the plank floor in two strides and took her into his arms.

Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Within the mirror-lined rooms of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. Against the gentle, trembling honesty of Phillip's touch, tenderness proved a fiercer fire than brutality could ever ignite. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

Against the overturned hull of the skiff, he unbuttoned her lynx coat and slid his hands inside to wrap around her waist, drawing her cold body into the open furnace of his greatcoat. Krystyna buried her face against the bare skin of his throat, breathing him in—cedar soap, wool, winter wind, Phillip—while his hands moved up her spine and cradled the back of her head.

Against the muted backdrop of the slatted wooden window looking out toward the pine screen, the white blanket of untouched snow hiding the boundary wall between their two childhood estates drew her eye, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. A subtle trace of warm skin, wool, and the clean bite of December air hung in the draft alongside the distant jingle of sleigh bells signaling the hunters' return along the Wilanów road an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. She stepped out of the boathouse first, walking up the orchard path while Phillip waited five minutes behind the firs. The sight of the faint flush on her throat and lips that she cooled with a handful of fresh snow at the door brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond buttoning her sweater with trembling fingers while Phillip kissed the inside of her bare wrist one last time and the reluctant, agonizing return to the armor of Marion Herschlag, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. Not a single second could be wasted when she had to be sitting by the drawing-room fire with a book in her lap before Viktor's sleigh pulled into the drive.

In the secret light that shone whenever their eyes met across the dark, his hands asked permission where the world took by force, and that undid her. Behind the double-bolted doors of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Drawing a slow breath, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

"Look where we are, Krysiu," he whispered into her hair, his voice thick with an emotion that made his whole frame shudder against hers. "Fifty yards from the linden tree where I carved our initials in thirty-seven. Last night I had to lie in the guest room across the hall and listen to Harenberg’s boots go into your mother’s bedroom."

Across the cobble-paved expanse of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Held close against the steady beat of Phillip's heart, every unspoken year between thirty-eight and forty-three spoke in a single kiss. In the amber half-light, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

She lifted her mouth to his to stop the pain in his voice. The kiss that followed was slow, deep, and devastatingly tender—a kiss that tasted of everything they had lost and everything they refused to surrender. Phillip’s hands slipped beneath the hem of her wool sweater, his warm, calloused palms spanning her bare ribs and rising to cup the weight of her breasts through the thin silk of her chemise. Unlike Viktor’s rough, claiming grip, Phillip touched her as if every breath she took were sacred to him, his thumbs brushing her peaks until a low, aching sob broke in her throat.

"When Kessler brings the Christmas Eve arrest lists on the twenty-fourth," Phillip whispered against her parted lips, his forehead resting against hers as their breath rose in white clouds together, "I will have a copy out of the Brühl Palace by six o'clock. Meet me at the safe house on Ulica Freta—Number 19, the attic above the bookbinder’s—before the midnight Mass curfew. Will you come?"

"Even if I have to walk through fire," she whispered, kissing the scar above his eyebrow before they parted into the snow.

* * *

When Viktor stamped into the hall ten minutes later, his greatcoat dusted with snow and two dead wild boars lashed to the rear runners of the sleigh outside, Marion was curled on the fireside sofa turning the pages of a French novel, a cup of hot spiced wine steaming at her elbow.

She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Beyond the shuttered windows of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Measuring the room one last time, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

"To a successful hunt, Herr Oberst," Phillip said evenly, and drained the glass without looking at her again. Where the shadow fell across the firelit entrance hall of Willa pod Lipami, the decoded Cracow folder in Phillip's hand and the cup of spiced wine in hers remained half-hidden, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She was acutely aware of the crackle of the hearth logs and the thud of the orderlies unloading the dead boars outside and the faint drift of cloves, hot red wine, pine resin, and cold snow as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. Her training insisted that to sit on her mother's sofa with Viktor's arm around her waist while Phillip stood four feet away drinking Viktor's schnapps required nerves of tempered steel, though every detail of the three of them standing under the same roof while two of them shared the taste of each other's mouths remained as sharp as cut glass. With a lazy, fireside smile directed impartially at the returning hunters governing the motion of taking a slow, unhurried sip of the hot spiced wine over the rim of her porcelain cup to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Whatever happened next, she knew that in eight days Christmas Eve would come, and with it the attic on Ulica Freta.

Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. From the sentry-guarded gates of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Without a backward glance, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

"Two tuskers in the Chojnów pines!" Viktor boomed, stripping off his blood-stiffened shooting gloves and bending to kiss her cheek with lips cold as iron from the sleigh ride. "Where is Brandt? Did the Cracow teleprinter come through?"

Through the drifting night mist of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. With practiced serenity, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

At that moment the side door from the office wing opened, and Hauptmann Brandt stepped into the hall carrying a decoded folder, his face calm, formal, and pale with the winter cold. "Just finished decoding it, Herr Oberst. The Ostbahn snowplow detachments are cleared through Lublin."

A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Steeling her resolve once more, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

"Good man," Viktor said, clapping Phillip on the shoulder while his other arm rested possessively around Marion’s waist. "Come and have a glass of schnapps by the fire, Brandt. You look half-frozen."

Across the width of the hearth rug, as Phillip accepted the crystal schnapps glass from Viktor’s hand and bowed slightly toward Marion on the sofa, Krystyna lifted her cup of spiced wine to her lips—tasting on her mouth the kiss Phillip had given her twenty minutes ago down by the frozen river.

* * *

On Sunday evening at dusk, the three black Mercedes staff cars pulled away from the porch of *Willa pod Lipami* to return to Warsaw through a rising December blizzard. In the rear cabin of the lead car, Viktor slept with his chin sunk into his greatcoat collar, exhausted from two days of boar hunting and schnapps.

As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Along the damp paving stones of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. With unhurried grace, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Her senses caught the hum of snow chains biting into the packed ice of the Wilanów road at the same moment she breathed in wet wool, gun oil on Viktor's sleeping coat, and the memory of river pine sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. She faced forward toward the dark silhouette of Warsaw and waited for Christmas Eve. To anyone else, the snow piling white against the wipers of Phillip's car behind them might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with resting her gloved hand lightly on the window ledge as the villa roofs vanished behind the pines, letting silent, contemplative stillness in the dark car serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She forced herself to accept that leaving Willa pod Lipami in the dark felt less like an exile tonight, because she knew the orchard and the boathouse still held their truth intact, while the contrast with the summer evenings of 1938 when they had driven back to Warsaw along this same road with the top down and stars overhead made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where four days remained until Christmas Eve and the garret on Ulica Freta.

Down the long colonnades of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Krystyna sat awake beside him, turning her head slightly to look back through the oval rear window of the sedan as they turned onto the Wilanów road.

A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Keeping her pulse steady, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Outside the tall casements of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. While the night deepened outside, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

Through the swirling curtain of white snow, the timbered verandas of her childhood home and the snow-laden crowns of the linden orchard receded into the blue winter twilight. Fifty yards behind them, the headlamps of the second staff car—where Phillip rode beside Major Kleist—cut twin tunnels of pale gold through the driving snow, following in their tracks all the way back to the conquered city.

# Chapter 3 — The Porcelain Scar

*Fontainebleau — July 1943*

---

The Château de Courances-les-Bois, six miles south of the Fontainebleau forest, had belonged for three centuries to a line of French peers before the Luftwaffe requisitioned its park and the Wehrmacht claimed its salons for weekend shooting parties. On the second Saturday of July, the afternoon heat hung golden and heavy over the lime-tree alleys while twelve-gauge shotguns cracked across the parterre, shattering clay pigeons above the ornamental canal.

Even in the French countryside, the war between the Wehrmacht and the SS smelled of oiled steel. Where the draft stirred the stone balustrade of the Fontainebleau terrace, a pair of smoking brass shotgun shells lying on the flagstones remained fixed in place, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She was acutely aware of the sharp twin reports of twelve-gauge shotguns echoing off the beech woods and the faint drift of blue cordite smoke, crushed Boxwood hedges, and sun-baked gravel that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. She forced herself to accept that SS-Oberführer Waldeck's unscheduled arrival meant Kessler's inquiry into the Siedlce leak was still alive, though every detail of her father's autumn boar hunts in the Kampinos Forest when the horns sounded through the birch mist remained as sharp as cut glass. With the languid, sun-drenched boredom of a society sportswoman governing the motion of tapping the braided leather tip of her riding crop against her mahogany boot to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Whatever happened next, she knew that if Waldeck had brought Viktor's transport adjutant under questioning, she needed to know before dusk.

Marion stood under the striped silk awning of the terrace in cream-colored Jodhpurs, tall mahogany riding boots, and an open-throated linen shirt that showed the sun-warmed hollow of her collarbone. Beside her, Viktor broke open his Sauer double-barreled shotgun, two brass cartridges ejecting in a curl of blue cordite smoke as he handed the weapon to his loader.

"You are dropping your left shoulder on the cross-shot, Viktor," Marion remarked, shading her eyes with one gloved hand as a clay disc sailed intact into the reeds. "Just as you did at Rambouillet in April."

Her senses caught the splash of shattered clay fragments falling into the ornamental canal at the same moment she breathed in chilled Riesling, gun oil, and the sweat of the retrievers panting in the shade pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She watched Waldeck bow to the host while his cold eyes scanned the terrace until they found hers. She observed the dark grease smudge left by the shotgun breech on Viktor's shooting glove with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with accepting a frosted glass of Moselle from an orderly with a faint nod, letting the effortless superiority of a woman accustomed to generals holding her stirrup serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that Viktor's resentment of the SS apparatus was a rift she could widen with a single well-placed word, while the contrast with the summer afternoon at Konstancin when Phillip had taught her to shoot a Rook rifle at tin cans made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where tonight Viktor's personal adjutant Major Kleist would lock the weekend dispatch pouch in the ground-floor study.

"The glare is off the water," Viktor grunted, though he looked pleased that she had noticed. Then his gaze hardened as a black Horch convertible rolled up the gravel drive and discharged SS-Oberführer Horst Waldeck—Sturmbannführer Kessler’s superior in the Paris SD apparatus—flanked by two aides in black breeches. "Who invited Himmler's pheasant-plucker to a Generalstab shoot?"

"General von Stülpnagel's adjutant," Marion murmured, letting her riding crop tap lightly against the calf of her boot. "Be civil to him, darling. If you shoot him in the reeds, Comtesse Solange will have to re-seat the entire dinner table."

Everything she had witnessed confirmed that Waldeck was walking toward the house alone while the other officers moved down to the lower pond, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her handler's instruction never to flee an inquisitor, but to walk straight into his light. Across the gravel path leading toward the château's orangery, her cream linen cuff turned back twice over her tanned wrist rested in the quiet shadow, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She stepped through the tall glass doors into the cool, checkered shadow of the porcelain gallery. The sight of the seventeenth-century sundial carved into the south wall of the château brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the baying of two pointers kennelled behind the stables while the room held the aroma of geraniums baking in terracotta urns and the dry dust of July an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that if Waldeck wanted five minutes alone to test her cover story, she preferred to take the measure of his blade herself.

Viktor’s scarred fingers closed briefly around the back of her neck beneath her pinned hair, warm and smelling of gunpowder. "Stay on the terrace, Marion. Keep clear of Waldeck. The man collects gossip the way a magpie collects tin."

* * *

The porcelain gallery ran the length of the château’s west wing, lined with glass vitrines displaying Sèvres shepherdesses and Meissen hunting hounds. Marion was standing before a case of cobalt-blue plates when the parquet creaked behind her and SS-Oberführer Waldeck’s reflection appeared in the glass.

Combining a look of haughty, aristocratic irritation at being cornered by a vulgar policeman with the quiet motion of leaning her hip lazily against the brass guard-rail of the display case, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of lemon wax, damp stone, and the sour peppermint lozenges on Waldeck's breath hung in the draft alongside the heavy, measured creak of Waldeck's boots on the checkered marble as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. She held Waldeck's gaze without blinking while Viktor's shadow lengthened down the gallery floor. Within the west-wing porcelain gallery of the château, her gaze returned briefly to a glass vitrine of cobalt-blue Sèvres plates and Meissen figurines, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She held fast to the recognition that Kessler had checked the Lyon raid report against the original oilskin notebook and discovered the one-word discrepancy, though the thought of the three days she had spent in a London safe house memorizing street-by-street maps of interwar Strasbourg returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that with Viktor walking through the door behind Waldeck, she had the chance to turn the SD's suspicion into a blood feud between the two men.

"A remarkable collection, Fräulein Herschlag," Waldeck said, clasping his hands behind his back. He was fifty, portly around the belt, with the florid cheeks of a Munich beer-hall veteran and the small, calculating eyes of a bailiff. "Though porcelain has one defect. Drop it once on stone, and no glue on earth can hide the crack."

Marion turned slowly, resting one hip against the gilded rail of the vitrine. "Which is why sensible men keep it behind glass, Herr Oberführer, instead of tossing it about the barracks. Did you lose your taste for clay pigeons so soon?"

Beside the tall oak doors at the far end of the gallery, the outline of Viktor's broken-open Sauer shotgun cradled in the crook of his elbow marked the stillness, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. "Waldeck," Viktor's voice cut down the gallery like a whip crack. "Did you lose your way to the gun room?" To anyone else, the vein standing out like a cord along Viktor's sunburned temple might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. No illusion could soften the fact that Waldeck had overplayed his hand by questioning her without Viktor's permission under a Wehrmacht roof, though every detail of the way her father'swolfhounds used to circle a trespasser before lunging remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, letting her chin lift with a sharp, wounded intake of breath just as Viktor came within earshot reinforced the quiet authority of the indignant flush of a proud woman insulted behind her protector's back, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the ominous click of Viktor's heel striking the marble border and the faint drift of cordite residue and the cold draft from the north courtyard sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

Waldeck’s smile did not reach his eyes. "Sturmbannführer Kessler mentioned how helpful you were last month at Lapérouse with that Lyon notebook. Odd, though—when our Lyon Kommando raided the Rue de la Charrue at dawn, they found only a deaf cobbler and three cats. Yet three blocks away, on the Rue de la Charité, a safe house had been emptied two hours before our trucks arrived."

Not a muscle flickered around Marion’s mouth. "Then your dead courier had terrible handwriting, Herr Oberführer, or your Lyon policemen cannot read a map. Really, if the Reichssicherheitshauptamt intends to blame my eyesight for its own clumsiness, I shall never translate a French menu for Kessler again."

"The Oberführer was merely admiring the porcelain, Viktor," she purred, knowing the lie would infuriate Viktor twice as much. With cold clarity she reckoned that Waldeck was smart enough to retreat a step when faced with a senior Generalstab colonel on Wehrmacht turf, while the contrast with the rivalries between German ministries that her handlers in Warsaw called the Underground's greatest ally made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the tense silence that fell over the gallery as the two officers faced each other at the same moment she breathed in gun oil, heated wool, and the faint scent of crushed lavender from the garden until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. It was in small things like the white knuckles of Viktor's hand around the checkered walnut stock of his gun that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted stepping smoothly to Viktor's side and laying two fingers on his forearm and the familiar armor of a cool, contemptuous smile directed at the retreating SS officer's back to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when what Waldeck had let slip meant there were files from Berlin in the château tonight.

"You were born in Strasbourg in nineteen-eighteen, I believe," Waldeck murmured, stepping closer until the shadow of his peaked cap fell across her throat. "Educated at the Pensionnat de Valmont in Lausanne. Tell me, Fräulein—when the cathedral bells of Strasbourg ring on Corpus Christi, do they ring the tenor bell first, or the bourdon?"

"The bourdon, of course," she lied smoothly—knowing from five weeks of memorizing Strasbourg parish records in London that the great Totenglocke always sounded first—"and it rattles every teacup in the Rue des Juifs. Why are you asking me catechism questions, Herr Oberführer?" Behind Waldeck’s shoulder, the tall oak doors at the end of the gallery swung open, and Oberst von Harenberg stepped across the threshold with his unloaded shotgun crooked over his forearm.

* * *

Viktor did not wait until after dinner. The moment they went upstairs to dress at six o'clock, he kicked the heavy oak door of their tapestried second-floor bedchamber shut, threw the iron bolt, and caught Marion by the waist before she could pull off her riding boots. His face was dark with blood, his blue eyes blazing with that mixture of territorial paranoia and violent desire that always made him most dangerous.

Through the lens of her cover identity, the red welts left by his fingers around both of her slender wrists was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. From where she stood in the seventeenth-century tapestried bedchamber at Courances, mother-of-pearl shirt buttons scattered across the faded Aubusson carpet was just visible, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the choreography of the room, arching her sweat-sheened throat beneath his teeth while her heels locked behind his thighs flowed seamlessly into a fierce, tempestuous passion that matched his brutality blow for blow, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. Every bruise on her wrists was another bolt shot across the door of her cover identity. She knew beyond doubt that she had just turned Waldeck's Strasbourg probe into an unforgivable personal insult against Viktor von Harenberg, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the cold truth that a man blinded by his own rut and his own pride cannot see the hand in his pocket. Before the night was over, tonight after dinner Viktor would confront Waldeck and force him to reveal what dossiers he had brought from Paris, and she intended to be ready.

"What did that Munich butcher ask you in the gallery?" Viktor demanded, shoving her back against the carved oak post of the seventeenth-century bedstead and pinning her wrists above her head with one massive hand.

"He asked me about Strasbourg bells and that stupid Lyon notebook!" Marion spat back, twisting her wrists in his iron grip so the linen sleeve tore at the shoulder seam, her gray-green eyes flashing fire into his. "He thinks because someone blew up your precious train in Poland, he can creep around your guests and interrogate your mistress like a streetwalker! And you let him walk under the same roof!"

Nothing moved in her expression beyond raking her fingernails down the damp muscle of his back as his rhythm broke into a shudder and the ragged, throated cry of a woman consumed by her lover's strength, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. He collapsed across her breasts like a felled oak, his heartbeat hammering against her sternum. Against the muted backdrop of the high damask canopy overhead, her cream riding breeches tangled beside the bedpost drew her eye, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She let her attention brush past the old Verdun shrapnel scar heaving against her ribs with every breath he took, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She measured the risk by the certainty that even while his body pinned hers to the mattress, her mind was calculating which room on the ground floor held the weekend dispatch pouch, though the thought of the quiet pond at Konstancin where the water lilies closed their white petals at sunset returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of crushed linen, salt sweat, and the resinous cedar of the old paneling hung in the draft alongside the harsh, synchronized rasp of their breathing in the close July heat steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray.

The accusation hit Viktor’s Prussian pride like raw alcohol on an open flame. "I let him do nothing," he growled, his free hand ripping the linen shirt open from throat to belt, scattering mother-of-pearl buttons across the Aubusson rug. His mouth came down on her bare throat, rough and scorching, his teeth scraping the tendon above her collarbone while she writhed against the oak bedpost. "Wait until I am finished with Stülpnagel tonight. Neither Waldeck nor Kessler will set foot inside five miles of my staff again—"

He dragged her riding breeches down over her hips with brutal haste, kicking the mahogany boots aside once she wrenched her heels free, and threw her backward across the high damask counterpane. When she tried to rise on her elbows, half-snarling at his roughness, he caught both her wrists again, pinned them into the pillows above her tousled gold hair, and drove into her with a single, relentless stroke that made the heavy canopy timbers groan.

"Leave the collar unpinned," Viktor said from the bed, watching her in the glass. "Let Waldeck see it at dinner." Nothing about the fresh bruise darkening over her collarbone where she left her neckline deliberately low escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the first tree frogs calling from the ornamental canal as night fell and the faint drift of cooling sweat, lavender soap, and the cigar smoke drifting up from the terrace below that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Where the shadow fell across the leaded casement window overlooking the darkened parterre, the silver ewer of tepid water on the washstand remained half-hidden, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. With a wry, sultry smile as she dressed for dinner in front of the tarnished mirror governing the motion of dipping a linen cloth in the ewer and wiping the sweat and lipstick from Viktor's jaw with practiced intimacy to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. She did not allow herself to forget that Viktor was already calmer, his paranoia slaked by the certainty that her body answered only to him, though every detail of the lesson her handler had taught her: let the target convince himself of your innocence remained as sharp as cut glass.

Krystyna gasped—a raw, involuntary sound torn from deep in her chest—as the sheer, bruising force of him filled her. With her wrists locked in his fist above her head, she arched her bare back off the damask coverlet, wrapping her long legs around his flanks and riding the tempest of his fury. Sweat slicked their skin in the stifling July dusk; every downward slam of his hips was a claim of ownership, and every fierce, rhythmic clench of her body was a tighter knot in the blindfold she tied across his eyes. When he finally buried his face in the curve of her neck with a hoarse, broken groan, she freed one hand and dug her nails deep into his scarred shoulder, whispering into his ear that no man on earth would ever take her from him.

* * *

At half past one in the morning, the generals were still in the billiard room at the far end of the east wing; through the floorboards came the occasional click of ivory balls and the rumble of drunken laughter over vintage Calvados. In the dark corridor of the ground-floor west wing, Krystyna moved barefoot in a dark gray silk dressing gown, carrying a pair of Viktor’s forgotten cigar cutters as her pretext if an orderly should appear.

Under the dim lamp near the darkened adjutant's study behind the château library, the red-bordered OKW portfolio stamped 'FALL WEICHSEL' on the leather blotter cast a narrow shade, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Her senses caught the distant click of billiard balls from the east wing and the tick of a tall case clock at the same moment she breathed in old parchment, red sealing wax, and the night damp off the canal that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Her thumb worked the tiny steel camera body—click, push-pull—sixteen times in the silent room. Against the quiet of the room, the red grease-pencil circle drawn around the Poniatowski and Kierbedź bridges on the map stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with holding the twelve-inch Minox chain taut between thumb and forefinger under the hooded blue beam, letting the stone-still concentration of an intelligence officer photographing her own city's death warrant serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where if she accompanied Viktor to Warsaw, she would be sitting at the very center of the German command on the eve of the uprising.

No orderly appeared. Major Kleist’s temporary office behind the library was locked with a standard French warded mortise lock—a child’s toy compared to the Zeiss-Ikon safes at the Majestic. Using a stiffened steel hairpin from her chignon and a slender torsion bar slipped from the seam of her belt, Krystyna turned the bolt in eleven seconds and slipped inside.

She did not touch the desk lamp. Taking a pencil-thin hooded flashlight from her pocket, she swept the narrow blue beam across the leather dispatch pouch on the blotter. Beside the pouch lay a red-bordered OKW portfolio stamped in heavy black gothic letters: *GEHEIME KOMMANDOSACHE — FALL WEICHSEL (Chefsache — Nur durch Offizier)*.

She did not have time to photograph the junior staff roster on the last page before footsteps sounded in the library. By the threshold of the French mortise lock of the study door, the stiffened steel hairpin and torsion bar in her left palm stood out in sharp relief, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She listened to a sentry's boots crunching across the gravel drive thirty yards outside the window while the room held the aroma of cold iron, beeswax, and the faint trace of Major Kleist's pipe tobacco pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. Her training insisted that in Warsaw every street corner carried the mortal risk of someone recognizing Count Stankiewicz's daughter on a German colonel's arm, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the face of her old housekeeper Hanna and the boys she had danced with at the Hotel Europejski before the war. As she completed closing the red-bordered portfolio in the exact alignment she had found it on the blotter, she drew the noiseless,ghostly grace with which she backed toward the door around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that London and the Home Army headquarters in Warsaw had to receive the Fall Weichsel directive before August.

Her heart gave one hard thud against her ribs as she opened the portfolio cover. Inside lay a folding map of Warsaw and the central Vistula basin, crisscrossed with red grease-pencil cordons, alongside a four-page memorandum signed by the Quartermaster General in Berlin: *Directive for the Reorganization of Oberfeldkommandantur 225 (Warschau) and Fortification of the Vistula Rail Bridges, Effective 1 October 1943.*

And pinned to the top corner of the first page was a personnel transfer draft: *Oberst V. Frhr. von Harenberg — transferred from MBF Paris to Chief of Wehrmacht Transport & Counter-Sabotage Fortifications, Warschau.*

A subtle trace of  spilled Calvados and the stale cigar smoke of twelve staff officers hung in the draft alongside the creak of the billiard-room door opening down the hall an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. "I could not sleep without you," she murmured, slipping her arm through his while the Minox burned cold against her thigh. The sight of the triumphant glint in Viktor's blue eyes after he had humiliated Waldeck over the billiard table brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a sleepy, affectionate smile as she held up the silver cigar cutter with the quiet motion of stepping calmly into the main hall as Viktor emerged with a glass in his hand, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She forced herself to accept that Viktor had not yet told her about the Warsaw transfer order because he himself had only received the draft today, though the thought of her father's voice saying that every road in Europe eventually leads a Pole back to the Vistula returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she had to pretend absolute ignorance of the Warsaw transfer until Viktor chose to announce it.

Krystyna stood frozen over the desk for five seconds, the blue beam of the torch trembling for the first time in two years against the map of her home city—where Ulica Foksal, Nowy Świat, and the Saxon Garden were marked in neat German type. Then her training took hold. She pulled the tortoiseshell comb from her pocket, slid the Riga Minox into her palm, and began to photograph every page.

* * *

At six o'clock the next morning, a pearly July mist hung waist-deep over the bracken of the Fontainebleau forest as Marion rode her bay mare alongside Viktor’s heavy Hanoverian gelding down the sandy bridle path called the Allée des Reines. They rode half a mile ahead of the grooms; the hooves of the two horses made only a soft, rhythmic thudding in the damp sand.

She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that he had just confirmed everything she had photographed at half past one in Major Kleist's study, though every detail of riding through the pine woods between Konstancin and Obory with Phillip Brandt riding bareback beside her remained as sharp as cut glass. Past the edge of the mist-shrouded bridle path of the Allée des Reines, the foam-flecked snaffle bit of her bay mare waited in the gloom, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Deep beneath the staged alarm in her eyes, Krystyna felt the terrible, magnetic pull of her ruined city. In the presence of the silver head of Viktor's riding crop glinting in a shaft of early sun, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the soft thud of eight hooves in the damp forest sand and a cuckoo calling in the oaks and the faint drift of wet bracken, warm horseflesh, saddle soap, and pine resin as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, she had to prepare Zygmunt immediately for the transfer of their highest-placed source from Paris to Warsaw, whatever price it demanded.

Viktor rode with the upright, square-shouldered seat of the old imperial cavalry, his riding crop tucked beneath his arm, his weathered face turned toward the shafts of early sunlight piercing the ancient oaks. "Waldeck left for Paris at five this morning," he said with grim satisfaction. "Stülpnagel told him to his face that if an SD clerk so much as opens a piece of mail addressed to my department, he will have the man arrested by the Feldgendarmerie."

"Good," Marion said, reining her mare closer so her boot brushed his stirrup iron. "I detest men with wet palms and little black notebooks. Though I suppose he will sulk in Berlin now."

She trusted urging her mare into a canter down the sandy track so the wind hid the sudden brightness in her eyes and the familiar armor of a breathless, carefree laugh thrown back over her shoulder as she challenged him to race to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the jingle of curb chains as the two horses tossed their heads at the same moment she breathed in crushed fern fronds, morning dew, and the leather of her tall boots sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. She rode hard into the white mist, feeling the wind tear at her hair like ghosts reaching out from the East. Set against a clearing of ancient sandstone boulders in the Fontainebleau woods, the leather saddlebag behind her cantle where her riding gloves rested held a quiet, insistent weight, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that returning to Warsaw on the arm of Oberst von Harenberg meant walking through the circles of hell in a German evening gown, while the contrast with the unmarked mass graves in the Palmiry forest where her father's bones lay under the pines made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when if she survived the crossing to Warsaw, she would be able to feed the Home Army the exact blueprints of Festung Warschau.

Viktor looked at her sideways—at the wind-flushed curve of her cheek, the loose gold tendrils escaping her riding hat, the dark bruise still visible at the open V of her linen collar. For a moment, something almost like regret passed across his hard features. "Berlin has other plans for the autumn, Marion. The war in the East is no longer a promenade. If they give me an independent command beyond the Oder, Paris will be behind us."

She drew rein in a patch of sunlit mist, turning in the saddle to look at him with wide, startled gray-green eyes—performing the exact shock a pampered Alsatian woman ought to feel. "Beyond the Oder? You mean Poland, Viktor? You would not leave me in Paris!"

Not three paces from the stone mounting block in the château stable yard, the brass cassette of the Minox hidden inside her vanity case upstairs lay in the amber half-light, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. When Viktor kissed her mouth in front of the grooms, she tasted the autumn already coming on the wind. It was in small things like the mud splashed high across the polished mahogany of her riding boots that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She held fast to the recognition that this was her last summer in France; by the time the leaves fell, the game would be played for blood on Polish soil, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the linden alley at Konstancin turning gold in the September before the war. In the choreography of the room, sliding down from the saddle into Viktor's waiting hands and letting him hold her a second too long against his chest flowed seamlessly into the flushed, glowing vitality of a woman who lived only for horses and her lover, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the clatter of iron shoes on the cobblestones as the grooms ran forward while the room held the aroma of fresh hay, woodsmoke from the kitchen chimneys, and hot coffee until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing.

He reached across the gap between their saddles and caught her chin in his gloved hand, his thumb brushing her lower lip. "Where I go, you go, Marion. Even if I have to quarter you in a fortress."

# Chapter 4 — The Watchmaker of Rue de la Huchette

*Paris — August 1943*

---

August came to Paris with thunderheads that refused to break and nights that smelled of hot slate and stagnant river water. On the nineteenth of August, at ten minutes past seven in the evening, a warm, greasy downpour finally burst over the Île de la Cité. Krystyna walked rapidly down the Quai Saint-Michel under a dark blue silk umbrella, wearing a reversible charcoal-and-silver evening cloak over her opera gown. At eight o'clock the curtain of the Palais Garnier would rise on *Tosca*, and Viktor was meeting her in Box Four at five minutes to eight.

There was no time for grief; there was only the math of seconds and steel. No illusion could soften the fact that Kessler's hounds had tracked Zygmunt from the Rue Saint-Séverin bookshop to this very courtyard, though the thought of the oath of the Armia Krajowa that no courier leaves a dying comrade's cipher book in enemy hands returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of wet garbage, cordite, river sewage, and hot blood on the cobblestones hung in the draft alongside the drum of torrential August rain on zinc roofs and the wet cough of a suppressed pistol filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Through the lens of her cover identity, the red froth gathering at the corner of old Stefan Wilk's mouth in the rain was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond backing silently into the vaulted shadow of the porte-cochère while drawing the six-inch steel hatpin from her cloak lapel and the utter, predatory silence of a cornered wolf, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when if the SD plainclothesman found the Warsaw contact cipher in Zygmunt's lining, Tadeusz and the entire Warsaw reception cell would be shot before she ever reached Poland.

She had forty-five minutes to make the final Paris brush pass with Zygmunt in the watchmaker’s passage off the Rue de la Huchette and receive her Warsaw contact ciphers.

Yet as she turned the corner from the Petit Pont into the narrow, rain-blackened throat of the Rue de la Huchette, every hair along her forearms stood upright. Fifty yards ahead, parked half on the curb outside the Algerian brass-seller’s shop, sat a black Citroën Traction Avant with mud-splattered Normandy plates and two rain-coated men smoking under the awning.

Beyond the shuttered windows of the Avenue Foch, the occupied capital wore its elegance like a mask over a clenched jaw. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Without haste, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

She let her attention brush past the blued-steel Walther PP resting loosely in the kneeling agent's right hand, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the draft stirred the narrow passageway beside the overflowing zinc dustbins, the six-inch tempered steel hatpin held like a stiletto along her right index finger remained fixed in place, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. With a face devoid of mercy, fear, or hesitation governing the motion of stepping out of the shadow on the balls of her feet while the rain drowned the rustle of her silk cloak to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. When the SD man turned his head at the brush of her cloak, she drove the six-inch steel needle up to the pearl head beneath his ear. With cold clarity she reckoned that the second agent had run toward the street entrance to whistle for the Citroën, leaving the tall man alone over Zygmunt for twenty seconds, though every detail of the close-combat instructor at the Audley End SOE school demonstrating where the carotid artery runs beneath the angle of the jaw remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that a single shout from this man would bring three guns down the passage before she could reach the back stair.

Gestapo. The Normandy plates were the same false series Kessler’s surveillance teams had used around the Gare de l’Est in July.

She did not break stride or turn back—turning back in an empty street was a confession. Instead, she folded her umbrella as if stepping into the milliner’s doorway, slipped through the vaulted stone porte-cochère of Number 11, and darted up the dark spiral service stair that connected the back courtyard to the watchmaker’s alley. Halfway across the slick cobblestones of the inner court, two shots from a suppressed pistol coughed like acarpet-beater in the rain.

She answered the silence with dropping to one knee in the rain beside the old man and catching his blood-slick hand in hers, letting the fierce, gentle steadiness of a daughter kneeling beside a dying soldier serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. "Kruk," Zygmunt breathed in Polish, pressing the bloody celluloid disk into her palm. "Chmielna... clock shop... go, Jaskółka!" Between the doorway and the wet cobblestones where Zygmunt lay dying, Zygmunt's small 6.35mm Browning pistol lying beneath his left hand marked the half-lit interval, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Nothing about the tiny celluloid disk tucked inside the watch pocket of Zygmunt's waistcoat escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She knew beyond doubt that Zygmunt was dying within sixty seconds and trying desperately to reach the inside pocket of his waistcoat, while the contrast with Major Stefan Wilk sharing his last square of chocolate with her on the winter night she arrived in Paris made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the choked, gurgling wheeze of the SD agent collapsing sideways against the dustbins at the same moment she breathed in iron blood, wet wool, and the ozone of the August thunderstorm overhead that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise.

Behind the overflowing zinc dustbins, Zygmunt lay on his side in the mud, blood bubbling dark and bright through the front of his threadbare tweed waistcoat, while a tall SD plainclothesman in a wet leather trench coat knelt over him, tearing open his coat pockets.

* * *

Boots pounded into the mouth of the outer alley. Krystyna snatched Zygmunt’s tiny 6.35mm Browning from the cobblestones, pressed the muzzle against her dying handler’s temple at his own silent nod—Wait, no: Zygmunt’s eyes were already fixed and glassy, his last breath rattling out into the rain before her finger needed to move. He had died on his own terms.

In the tarnished piece of mirror nailed above the cistern, Marion Herschlag's face looked back at her without a blemish. Against the quiet of the room, the tiny celluloid cipher disk from Zygmunt now tucked beneath her tongue against her back molars stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the distant wail of Feldgendarmerie sirens converging on the Place Saint-Michel while the room held the aroma of lye soap, wet brocade, rainwater, and the phantom copper smell of blood that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Across the stone washhouse behind Saint-Julien-le-Pauvre, the cake of coarse gray lye soap and the rainwater cistern rested in the quiet shadow, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. As she completed scrubbing each fingernail thirty times against the coarse bristles of the cistern brush, she drew the methodical, unshakeable calm that always descended on her after a kill around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She measured the risk by the certainty that not a single microscopic fleck of blood could remain under her cuticles or on the hem of her silver slippers, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Lady Macbeth's madness contrasted with the cold, practical chemistry of washing blood in cold water before warm.

A shadow lunged around the brick corner of the courtyard: the second SD plainclothesman, a stocky man in a felt trilby, raising a Sauer pistol through the curtain of rain. He saw only a woman in a dark cloak kneeling by the dustbins; for half a second he hesitated, shouting, "Halt! Geheime Staatspolizei!"

That half-second killed him. Krystyna did not rise; bracing her right wrist across her left forearm in the crouch they had drilled into her at Audley End, she fired two rounds from the tiny 6.35mm Browning straight up into the dark triangle between his nose and upper lip. The small-caliber cracks were swallowed by a simultaneous clap of August thunder overhead. The man in the trilby pitched backward onto the slick flagstones without a cry.

A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Along the damp paving stones of the Rue de Rivoli, every passing staff car carried the weight of orders drafted in Berlin. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. In the hush that followed, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Within the hooded wicker seat of the vélo-taxi crossing the Pont au Change, her gaze returned briefly to her reversible silver brocade evening cloak draped dry and gleaming over her knees, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. A subtle trace of wet Parisian chestnut trees, ozone, and the Guerlain Shalimar she dabbed behind her ears hung in the draft alongside the rhythmic squeak of the bicycle chain and the splash of tires through puddles along the Châtelet pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She transferred the celluloid disk from her mouth into the hollow velvet lining of her opera glasses. She observed the illuminated marquee of the Palais Garnier glowing blue-veiled at the end of the Avenue de l'Opéra with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the serene, expectant beauty of a woman riding to the opera with the quiet motion of opening her gold compact in the swaying cab to powder her nose and retouch her carmine lips, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that if she arrived at the Place de l'Opéra even three minutes breathless, Viktor would ask where she had been.

She had twenty-eight minutes before the curtain rose at the Palais Garnier.

Moving with icy speed, she dropped the empty Browning into the dead agent’s pocket so ballistics would waste two days matching two different calibers, wiped the pearl-headed hatpin on the dead man’s lapel, and slipped out through the baker’s cellar into the Rue Galande just as shouts erupted at the front of the Rue de la Huchette. In the stone washhouse behind the courtyard of Saint-Julien-le-Pauvre, she plunged her blood-streaked hands and forearms into the cold rainwater cistern, scrubbing beneath her fingernails with a cake of coarse lye soap until the skin burned raw.

At the door of Box Four, the liveried usher bowed low and drew back the crimson velvet curtain. Beside the grand marble staircase of the Palais Garnier, the outline of the crimson carpet and the rows of gilded candelabra marked the stillness, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She was acutely aware of the tuning of the orchestra strings filtering through the closed doors of the auditorium and the faint drift of expensive French perfume, wet wool dress uniforms, and beeswax an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. There was no escaping the conclusion that half the senior staff of the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich was climbing these stairs beside her, though every detail of going to the Teatr Wielki in Warsaw with her parents in the winter of 1938 to hear Kiepura sing remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, resting her freshly washed, white-gloved hand on the marble balustrade as she ascended reinforced the quiet authority of a radiant, unhurried smile directed at Major Lüttwitz on the landing, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, nobody looking at her silver gown could imagine that twenty-two minutes ago she had shot a Gestapo agent between the eyes, whatever price it demanded.

Then she stripped off the charcoal-outer cloak, turned its rain-wet side inward so only the dry silver brocade lining showed, pinned the pearl hatpin back into her chignon, and stepped out onto the Quai de Montebello to hail a passing vélo-taxi.

* * *

Viktor rose from his gilt chair in the front of Box Four as she entered, resplendent in his full-dress Generalstab tunic with the scarlet collar tabs and the Pour le Mérite of his uncle’s regiment gleaming beside his Iron Cross. As she slipped off her silver brocade cloak, he took her bare right hand—she had peeled off her glove at the door—and raised it to his lips.

Her senses caught the roar of applause from the parterre below and the brassy swell of the orchestra pit at the same moment she breathed in velvet dust, Shalimar perfume, and the cold rainwater still clinging to Kessler's black cuffs as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. "In the Latin Quarter?" Marion exclaimed, turning to Viktor with a shiver. "How perfectly ghastly! Tell me they have cordoned the bridges!" In the presence of the mud splatter drying on the toe of Kessler's left jackboot, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted raising a hand to her bare throat with a delicate, theatrical gasp of horror and the familiar armor of the shocked, wide-eyed dismay of a sheltered woman hearing of street murder to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her training insisted that Kessler had come straight from the corpses in the Rue de la Huchette to the Opéra to see who was in their seats on time, while the contrast with the dead SD agent's felt trilby rolling into the puddle as the thunder cracked made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when if a single drop of blood had remained on her slippers or cloak, Kessler's eyes would have found it in three seconds.

"Your fingers are like river ice, Liebchen," he murmured, frowning slightly as he rubbed her small hand between both of his warm palms. "Where the devil was Hanke?"

"Stuck behind a coal lorry on the Boulevard Haussmann," Marion said smoothly, sinking into the velvet chair beside him and laying her mother-of-pearl opera glasses on the plush parapet—their hollow barrel carrying Zygmunt’s last cipher less than six inches from Viktor’s elbow. "I walked the last hundred yards under the arcade and got splashed for my trouble. Have I missed the overture?"

Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of the Hôtel Majestic, the clerks of the Wehrmacht measured Europe in rail timetables and coal quotas. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Drawing a slow breath, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

She forced herself to accept that Viktor was furious that Kessler had ignored General von Stülpnagel's warning and entered his private box, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the line from Tosca she had just heard on stage: 'And before him, all Rome trembled'. From where she stood in the velvet parapet overlooking the chandelier-lit auditorium, Viktor's heavy hand tightening around her fingers on his knee was just visible, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Not eighteen inches from Kessler's eyes, the weapon that had killed his man held her hair in place. To anyone else, the pearl head of the six-inch steel hatpin gleaming openly in the gold coils of her chignon might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the intermission bell chiming in the marble foyer behind them while the room held the aroma of cigar smoke drifting from the neighboring boxes and the warm tungsten of the sconces sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. Before the night was over, Kessler was watching her hands—and her hands were white, manicured, and smelled faintly of Guerlain, and she intended to be ready.

"Just in time," Viktor said, as the house lights dimmed to a bruised amber glow and the conductor’s baton came down on the first three crashing chords of Puccini’s *Tosca*—Scarpia’s theme, the music of the secret police. In the dark of the box, Viktor kept her cold hand captive on his scarlet-striped knee.

Throughout the first act—while Cavaradossi sang in the church of Sant’Andrea della Valle and the Baron Scarpia entered with his spies—Krystyna sat motionless in the gilt chair, watching the stage while her blood slowly warmed beneath Viktor’s palm. Then, just as the first-act curtain fell to thunderous applause, the brass latch at the rear of Box Four clicked open, and Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler stepped into the box.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond picking up her ivory fan and fanning her collarbone slowly while the two officers confronted each other and aloof, slightly shaken elegance as she looked away toward the stage curtain, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of starch, wool, and the static electricity of two men on the verge of drawing pistols hung in the draft alongside Viktor's low, dangerous growl as he rose to his full height in front of Kessler until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. "Take your police reports back to the morgue, Sturmbannführer," Viktor snapped, and shut the box door in Kessler's face. Against the muted backdrop of the rear alcove of Box Four where the silver cloak hung, the dry silver brocade lining facing outward on the brass hook drew her eye, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that by playing the frightened dove, she provoked Viktor into driving the hawk from the box, though the thought of the old Polish proverb that when two dogs fight over a bone, the fox carries off the hen returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when with Zygmunt dead, her Paris pipeline was severed—which meant the Warsaw transfer was now her only road forward.

"Forgive the intrusion, Herr Oberst, Fräulein Herschlag," Kessler said softly over the applause, his gin-pale eyes glittering in the light of the box sconce. "I have just come from the Préfecture on the Île de la Cité. Forty minutes ago, two of Oberführer Waldeck’s plainclothes operatives were killed in an alley off the Rue de la Huchette—one with a blade through the carotid, the other shot through the face at close range."

* * *

On Stage in the second act, Floria Tosca drove the fruit knife into Baron Scarpia’s heart and laid two candles beside his corpse. Watching from Box Four, Krystyna felt a strange, dizzying vertigo: the opera and the alley behind the Rue de la Huchette had bled into one another until the red velvet of the Palais Garnier looked the color of arterial blood.

Where the shadow fell across the narrow velvet chaise longue in the Rue de Rivoli dressing room, the six-inch pearl-headed hatpin lying fallen on the carpet beside the silver hairbrush remained half-hidden, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. He groaned against her ear, driving deeper as her body shuddered beneath him with grief transmuted into heat. Through the lens of her cover identity, the tears wetting the collar of Viktor's white linen shirt was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that she was using the enemy's own body as a shield to hide the tears she wept for the man his regime had murdered, though every detail of Stefan Wilk's ink-stained fingers handing her a volume of Mickiewicz in the back of his shop remained as sharp as cut glass. With a wild, trembling abandon that made Viktor believe she was undone by passion for him governing the motion of clinging fiercely to Viktor's broad shoulders and biting into the muscle of his neck to stifle a sob to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. She was acutely aware of the crash of August thunder over the Tuileries and the harsh creak of the chaise frame and the faint drift of lye soap, Shalimar, warm sweat, and the ozone of lightning filtering through the heavy draperies without cease.

When they returned to the Rue de Rivoli at midnight, the thunderstorm broke a second time over the Louvre. Inside the dressing room, as Marion unpinned her hair before the triptych mirror—drawing the six-inch pearl-headed hatpin slowly from the heavy gold coils and laying it on the glass tray—her hands finally began to tremble. It was the delayed aftershock of old Stefan Wilk’s dying breath in the mud and the two shots in the dark.

Viktor came up behind her in his shirtsleeves and braces, catching sight of the fine tremor in her fingers in the mirror. "Still thinking of Kessler’s dead policemen?" he asked, his hands descending onto her bare shoulders.

Never had the boundary between hatred, survival, and carnal fever felt so razor-thin. She held fast to the recognition that every thrust of his hips grounded her back in the brutal physical present and kept her from screaming, while the contrast with the rain falling on the cobbles of the Rue de la Huchette where Stefan's body now lay on a police slab made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the torrential sheet of rain lashing the dressing-room casement at the same moment she breathed in bruised skin, damp hair, and the heavy musk of their joined bodies steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She let her attention brush past Viktor's large hand spanning the back of her skull to press her face into his throat, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with wrapping her thighs tighter around his hips and arching up to meet his weight, letting the hoarse, breathless cries that fed his certainty of his own power serve as her shield, turning his possessive hunger into the very instrument of his blindness. Time was narrowing toward the point where tomorrow she would burn the celluloid cipher disk into her memory and dissolve the plastic in acetone.

She could not let him see the grief tearing at her ribs; if she wept in silence, he would wonder why two dead SD men moved her so deeply. Instead, she spun around on the velvet dressing stool, buried her hands in the open front of his white linen shirt, and pulled him down to her mouth with a fierce, almost desperate hunger.

"Take me to bed, Viktor," she whispered against his lips, her voice thick and shaking with real tears that she let him mistake for terror and arousal. "I hate that man's eyes—I want to feel you—only you—"

Nothing about the tiny celluloid disk resting safely inside the brass barrel of the opera glasses escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. By the threshold of the quiet bedroom at three in the morning after the storm passed, the hollow mother-of-pearl opera glasses sitting on the nightstand stood out in sharp relief, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. As she completed lying motionless on her back with dry, burning eyes fixed on the ceiling, she drew the cold, resurrected armor of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna around herself like silk, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. She whispered a silent Polish prayer for Stefan Wilk's soul and waited for the autumn. No illusion could soften the fact that her Paris network was gone, her handler was dead, and only Warsaw remained ahead of her, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the stone mermaid of Warsaw holding her sword and shield above the Vistula river. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that when Viktor went east to build Festung Warschau, she would go with him as the blade at his ribs.

A dark, triumphant heat flared in Viktor’s face. He did not even carry her into the bedroom; he swept the silver brushes and the pearl hatpin from the marble counter with one forearm, lifted her out of the silk slip onto the velvet chaise longue in the dressing room, and came down over her with all his heavy, scarred weight. Krystyna locked her arms around his neck, burying her face against the rough wool and sweat of his shoulder as he drove into her, letting the harsh, rhythmic battering of his body mask the silent, convulsive sobs for Stefan Wilk that shook her chest.

* * *

At nine o'clock the next morning, the August sun was already baking the wet slate roofs of the Rue de Rivoli into a blinding glare. Viktor sat at the breakfast table with the preliminary police report from the Préfecture spread beside his coffee cup, while Marion sliced a ripe white peach onto her porcelain plate with a silver fruit knife.

Combining amused, slightly bored skepticism at police theories with the quiet motion of licking a drop of peach juice from the corner of her thumb with lazy composure, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Viktor snorted into his coffee cup and tossed the SD report aside. In the hush that filled the sunlit breakfast table on the Rue de Rivoli, the silver fruit knife slicing cleanly through the white peach on her plate appeared almost motionless, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Against the quiet of the room, the forensic diagram attached to the second page of Viktor's report stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. With cold clarity she reckoned that Stefan Wilk's cover name had died with him in the courtyard, leaving her Warsaw recognition cipher completely unknown to the SD, though the thought of Stefan's gentle voice in the confessional of Saint-Germain-des-Prés returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of ripe peaches, hot coffee, and the freshly polished steel hatpin resting in her dressing-room tray hung in the draft alongside the morning traffic along the Tuileries and the rustle of the police report in Viktor's hand that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike.

"They identified the third body in the Rue de la Huchette courtyard at dawn," Viktor said, tapping the typed sheet with his knuckle. "An emigré Polish bookseller named Stefan Wilk—living on false Vichy papers since forty-one. Kessler believes he was the Paris relay for the Siedlce train intelligence."

Marion did not let the silver fruit knife pause in the flesh of the peach. "A Polish bookseller?" she repeated with a light shrug of her silk-clad shoulders. "Then perhaps Herr Kessler will finally stop pestering your staff at the Majestic and go back to Berlin where he belongs."

She walked into the dressing room and pinned her hair up with the very needle Kessler's doctors were hunting. She observed the sunlight glinting off the pearl head of the freshly washed hatpin in the next room with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of a street organ grinding an old Java tune two blocks away near the Palais-Royal and the faint drift of warm stone, beeswax, and summer dust pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. Past the edge of the tall balcony doors open to the August heat, her tortoiseshell hairbrush and hatpin tray visible through the bedroom doorway waited in the gloom, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Without haste, rising from the table to kiss the top of Viktor's silver-templed head before going to dress reinforced the quiet authority of affectionate domestic ease, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She knew beyond doubt that the Paris chapter of her war was closing; every surviving thread now pointed eastward toward the Vistula, though every detail of the celluloid cipher numbers she had memorized before dissolving the disk in acetone at dawn remained as sharp as cut glass.

"Not yet," Viktor said grimly. "Because the two SD plainclothesmen who cornered Wilk were killed by a second operative who escaped with Wilk’s cipher book—and Kessler’s forensics doctor says the taller agent was killed with a six-inch steel hatpin driven through the carotid artery. A woman’s weapon, Marion."

Marion lifted a slice of white peach to her mouth, chewed slowly, and looked across the sunny table into Viktor’s cold blue eyes. "How wonderfully melodramatic. Like something out of a penny feuilleton. Remind me never to walk in the Latin Quarter after dark, Viktor—I should hate to have Kessler measuring my hatpins."

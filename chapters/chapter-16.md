# Chapter 16 — The Nocturne in D-Minor

*Warsaw — March 1944*

---

In the third week of March, while the ice on the Vistula cracked with reports like cannon fire and floated northward in jagged gray floes, Viktor gave a formal dinner for twelve at Foksal 14. Around the long mahogany table—set with the Stankiewicz family’s own crested Meissen porcelain and heavy silver candelabra—sat General Rainer Stahel, two colonels from the Ostbahn directorate, Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler, and, at the foot of the table as junior adjutant, Hauptmann Phillip Brandt.

Above the slated mansard roofs of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Without a backward glance, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

She laid her bare fingers on the cool ivory keys her mother had loved and struck the opening agitated chords in D minor. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that byGerman decree in the Generalgouvernement, playing a single note of Frédéric Chopin was a crime punishable by imprisonment, even in this room where her mother had played Chopin every Sunday, while the contrast with Helena Stankiewiczowa's hands flying over these very keys in the Revolutionary Étude in August 1939 made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the rustle of her bronze silk taffeta skirt and the murmur of twelve German officers over brandy at the same moment she breathed in Havana cigars, roasted coffee, beeswax candles, and the damp March thaw outside the windows pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She observed the volume of Schumann's Kreisleriana resting open on the carved music rack with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with walking gracefully to the piano bench and seating herself without looking up at Phillip as he stepped to her left shoulder, letting the gracious, slightly theatrical poise of an accomplished salon pianist serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where Phillip was standing six inches behind her bare left shoulder with Viktor and Kessler watching every breath they took from ten feet away.

A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Down the wind-scoured length of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. With practiced serenity, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

Marion presided at the foot opposite Viktor in a gown of deep bronze silk taffeta that rustled like dry autumn leaves whenever she moved. All through the five courses of roast venison from the Kampinos reserves and Rhine wine, she was dazzling—laughing at General Stahel’s stories of the Italian front, trading barbed witticisms with Kessler, and never once glancing down the table toward Phillip.

She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Steeling her resolve once more, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

When the gentlemen rose to take their coffee and cigars in the white-and-gold drawing room, Viktor walked over to the black Bechstein grand piano by the garden casements and lifted the mahogany fallboard.

Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Along the riverward slope of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Letting the silence work for her, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

The sight of Sturmbannführer Kessler standing beside the marble mantelpiece watching the reflection of her hands in the piano lid brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Across the carved mahogany music rack of the Bechstein, Phillip's right hand resting lightly on the upper corner of the printed score rested in the quiet shadow, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. As she completed leaning forward into the crescendo as Phillip's arm reached across her vision to turn the first page, she drew total artistic absorption in the German Romantic score around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. That half-second brush of his warm wrist against her bare skin flowed straight into the singing legato of her left hand. She held fast to the recognition that Schumann had written Kreisleriana for Clara Wieck when her father forbade them to meet—a secret coded letter in music played right in front of the Gestapo, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of playing four-hand duets with Phillip on the upright piano at Konstancin while the summer rain fell outside. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that as Phillip's fingers turned the third page at the transition to the slow movement, the inside of his wrist brushed her bare left shoulder blade.

Along the damp paving stones of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

"Come and play for the General, Marion," Viktor commanded, his chest swelling with pride as the officers gathered with their brandy snifters. "Show them that Paris has not forgotten how to play Schumann. Brandt—you know music. Stand by the rack and turn the pages for Fräulein Herschlag."

* * *

In the slow, aching middle section of the Schumann—marked *Sehr innig und nicht zu rasch*—the music seemed to speak aloud everything the two of them were forbidden to say in the light. Standing at her left shoulder, Phillip looked down exclusively at the score, yet Krystyna could see the rise and fall of his chest in the polished black lacquer of the piano lid. When he leaned down to turn the fourth page, his sleeve grazed her bare arm, and under cover of a fortissimo bass chord in D minor, his lips moved without a sound:

A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Near the sandbagged corners of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Combining the radiant, flattered vanity of a woman basking in the generals' praise with the quiet motion of rising from the piano bench and leaning back slightly into Viktor's hands with a practiced, breathless smile, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She looked straight at Sturmbannführer Kessler as she accepted General Stahel's toast, and Kessler clapped softly with the rest. Within the polished black lacquer surface of the Bechstein grand, her gaze returned briefly to the reflection of Phillip's quiet face and Viktor's possessive hands in the dark wood, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the presence of Phillip stepping back into the shadow of the bookcase with his hands clasped behind his back, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. No illusion could soften the fact that under the cover of a fortissimo Schumann chord, Phillip had just passed her the exact train number and hour for Tuesday's Easter munitions transport at Wawer, though the thought of her mother's rule that a true pianist never lets her face show how hard the passage is returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of hot wax, brandy, and the electric contrast between Phillip's vanished wrist and Viktor's heavy palms hung in the draft alongside the polite applause of the senior officers and the ring of the final D-minor chord as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry.

In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Keeping her pulse steady, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

*Wawer siding. Tuesday midnight. Train 804.*

Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Across the hushed thoroughfares of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. While the night deepened outside, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Her left hand did not miss a thirty-second note. She flowed straight into the final stretto, bringing her hands down on the closing D-minor octave with a clean, resonant ring that brought applause from General Stahel and the Ostbahn colonels.

In the long shadow cast by Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. In that narrow pause between beats, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

"Brava, Fräulein!" General Stahel cried, raising his brandy glass. "You play with a man’s fire!"

The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. As the shadows lengthened, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

"She does everything with fire, Herr General," Viktor said, stepping behind the piano bench to lay both his heavy hands over Marion’s bare shoulders—forcing Phillip to step back two paces and click his heels as Viktor’s fingers claimed her skin in front of the room.

* * *

At half past eleven, the guests descended the grand marble staircase toward their waiting staff cars in the courtyard. Krystyna stood at the top of the landing beside Viktor as General Stahel, Kessler, and last of all Hauptmann Phillip Brandt bowed and went down the steps in their greatcoats.

With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. In that fierce clash of carnal power and calculated surrender, she matched his tempest without yielding a single millimeter of her soul. Without haste, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

When Viktor shuddered against her throat, the last staff car's tail-lamps vanished down Ulica Foksal. To anyone else, the brass keyhole plate of the drawing-room door pressing cold into the small of her bare back might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the roar of the departing staff cars in the courtyard below and the thud of the doors behind her back and the faint drift of cigar smoke, spilled Rhine wine, warm sweat, and crushed taffeta sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. Beside the bolted white-and-gold double doors of the Foksal drawing room, the outline of her bronze silk taffeta skirt bunched loud and rustling between her waist and the oak panels marked the stillness, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Without haste, wrapping her arms tight around Viktor's neck so he could not see the fierce, dry anguish in her open eyes reinforced the quiet authority of the raw, gasping heat that convinced Viktor her fire burned only for him, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. With cold clarity she reckoned that thirty feet below in the courtyard Phillip was walking out into the March night carrying the memory of her shoulder under his wrist, while she paid the price of their cover against these doors, though every detail of the D-minor chord of Schumann's Kreisleriana still vibrating in the strings of the Bechstein across the room remained as sharp as cut glass.

While the Colonel consumed her body with feral urgency, the raw friction of their nights served as her most impenetrable alibi. Under the starless canopy above Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. As the minute hand crept on, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

The moment the heavy double doors of the second-floor drawing room closed behind the orderly, Viktor did not even wait for the cars in the courtyard to pull away. His eyes were dark and bloodshot from three glasses of Rhine wine and the sight of twelve officers staring at Marion’s bare shoulders at the piano. Before Phillip’s boots had reached the bottom of the marble stairs below, Viktor caught Marion by the waist, spun her around against the closed white-and-gold double doors of the drawing room, and dragged the bronze taffeta bodice down off her breasts.

Beneath the looming cornice of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Across the tangled linen where power and obsession collided, she traded the fever of her skin for the combinations that armed the Resistance. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

"Every man at that table was undressing you with his eyes while you played," Viktor growled against her throat, his rough hands kneading her bare flesh while the loud crackle of the bronze taffeta echoed in the empty salon. "Stahel, Reinecke, even that cold fish Kessler—"

The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. In the raw, unsparing heat of their nights behind bolted doors, no gentleness softened the bargain, for power was the only language he trusted. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Anchored by her silent oath, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Below the tall stair window, Krystyna heard the courtyard gate open and the distinct, measured crunch of Phillip’s boots walking out into Ulica Foksal. The torment of it—being pinned against the drawing-room doors by Viktor’s heavy body while the man she loved walked away through the slush thirty feet below—was almost more than flesh could bear.

Experience inside the high command had proved to her that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. When Viktor claimed her with the violent hunger of a man at war, he saw only the wanton consort, never the hand that photographed his maps. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

She closed her eyes, dug her fingernails into Viktor’s silver-templed scalp as he yanked her heavy bronze skirts up around her waist, and took the harsh, driving fury of his body against the bolted oak doors—using every rasp of her breath and every clench of her muscles to seal the blindness of the man whose train she and Phillip had just condemned for Tuesday night.

* * *

At noon on the day after the dinner party and her Schumann recital, a uniformed SD orderly delivered a small parcel wrapped in marbled paper to Foksal 14 while Viktor was home for luncheon. Inside lay an antique 1880 Leipzig edition of Frédéric Chopin’s *Nocturnes*, with Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler’s engraved visiting card tucked into the flyleaf: *"To Fräulein Herschlag—in case Schumann ever grows too German for so fine a touch."*

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Within the lamplit precincts of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. In the hush that followed, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

In the corner of the white marble fireplace of the Foksal drawing room, she noticed the 1880 Leipzig edition of Chopin's Nocturnes curling into yellow flame on the birch logs, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Her senses caught the roar of the chimney draft catching the dry nineteenth-century paper at the same moment she breathed in burning paper, birch smoke, and the bitter ash of a sacrifice made to blind the enemy until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. Not a single note of Chopin had died in that fire; every bar was waiting in her blood for the day Warsaw was free. It was in small things like Kessler's engraved visiting card blackening first in the center of the coals that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted dusting her hands together lightly as the black pages dissolved into sparks up the flue and the familiar armor of the careless, arrogant disdain of a woman who valued bath salts over poetry to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when Viktor laughed aloud and promised to tell Kessler at the afternoon briefing that his gift had warmed the drawing room.

Along the darkened tramlines of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

It was a viper’s strike disguised as a compliment: possessing or playing Chopin in Warsaw was forbidden to Poles as nationalist subversion, and Kessler wanted to see whether she hid the book in her room.

The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Marion did not hesitate for half a second. With a cold, contemptuous laugh right in front of Viktor, she carried the open volume across the drawing room and tossed it straight onto the blazing birch logs in the white marble fireplace.

She held fast to the hard-won certainty that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Measuring the room one last time, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Within the mirror-lined rooms of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Without a backward glance, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

"Really, Viktor, your policeman has the taste of a provincial schoolmaster," she said, watching the pages of Chopin—the music her mother had died loving—curl into black flame while her face remained as hard as diamond. "Tell him next time to send French bath salts instead of Polish garret music."

* * *

At four that afternoon, when Marion drove to the Brühl Palace with Viktor to sign her renewed spring residency pass in the ground-floor adjutant’s office, Phillip met her at the registry counter to stamp the purple seal.

Outside the tall casements of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. When Phillip whispered her true Polish name against her skin, every heartbeat shared in the dark bound their fates closer to the same precipice. Drawing a slow breath, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

She nodded once to the Captain and walked out into the March sunshine beside Viktor. From where she stood in the high mahogany registry counter on the ground floor of the Brühl Palace, the purple ink stamp of Abteilung Ic pressing onto her spring residency pass was just visible, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She listened to the clatter of the telegraph keys across the hall and the thud of the brass seal while the room held the aroma of violet stamp ink, floor wax, and the sudden electric heat where Phillip's fingers covered hers filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. She measured the risk by the certainty that after the torment of last night—hearing Phillip walk down the stairs while Viktor pinned her to the drawing-room doors—this single touch restored their bond, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Phillip turning the pages of the Schumann score inches behind her bare shoulder. In the choreography of the room, drawing her stamped pass back into her handbag the instant Viktor's shadow fell across the doorway flowed seamlessly into cool, bureaucratic formality across the counter, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. Before the night was over, in ten days Train 804 would burn in the Wawer pines, and she intended to be ready.

The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beyond the guarded courtyard of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Within the sacred, unguarded sanctuary of Phillip's arms, their slow, luminous love refused to die beneath the boots of the Reich. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. In the amber half-light, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

While Viktor stepped into the telegraph room across the hall for sixty seconds, Phillip held her leather pass-folder open between them on the high mahogany counter. His fingers covered hers over the edge of the card.

Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Where five years of longing deepened into an all-consuming love, she knew what it meant to be cherished as sacred rather than claimed as spoils. With her eyes clear and level, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

"Last night at the piano," Phillip murmured in German so low it did not carry three feet, though his dark eyes burned into hers, "when I watched Harenberg put his hands on your shoulders after the Schumann, I understood how a man commits murder in a crowded room."

Against the gentle, trembling honesty of Phillip's touch, the boy from the Konstancin lindens had become the man she would die beside. Beyond the shuttered windows of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter marked the slow passage of the rationed night. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. With unhurried grace, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

From the sentry-guarded gates of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. In the secret light that shone whenever their eyes met across the dark, tenderness proved a fiercer fire than brutality could ever ignite. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

"Wait for Easter week at Wawer, my love," Krystyna whispered back without moving her lips as the purple stamp came down on her card. "Every time he touches me, we take another regiment from him."

# Chapter 24 — W-Hour (17:00)

*Warsaw — August 1, 1944*

---

By four o'clock in the afternoon of August first, a strange, electric silence had fallen over the scorching streets of Warsaw. Young men in oversized trench coats—concealing Sten guns and home-made *Filipinki* grenades—and girls in bulky skirts walked swiftly toward assembly points in every courtyard; German patrols gripped their rifles nervously at the street corners.

Along the quiet curbstones of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. In the amber half-light, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

A subtle trace of sharp ammonia salts, gun oil, and the suffocating heat of an August afternoon hung in the draft alongside the heavy, ticking silence of the room before the storm an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Viktor's eyes were bloodshot from the luminal and madness, fixed on her face with a terrible, betrayed grief. The sight of the blued-steel muzzle of Viktor's Walther PPK pointed steadily at the center of her breastbone brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond stopping in the center of the parquet without taking a step backward and no Alsatian accent and no mask left—only Count Ignacy Stankiewicz's daughter looking down the barrel of her enemy's gun, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. No illusion could soften the fact that Kessler had uncovered the Stankiewicz archive after Phillip escaped at dawn and had brought the photograph straight to Viktor to prove who 'Marion Herschlag' really was, though the thought of sitting for that studio portrait on Krakowskie Przedmieście in May 1938 with a white rose at her collar returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when in eighteen minutes the clocks of Warsaw would strike five and the Uprising would erupt outside the window.

Within the lamplit precincts of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

At twenty minutes past four, Krystyna discovered that the secondary quartz crystal for Tadeusz’s Battalion *Gustaw* shortwave transmitter—needed to communicate with London once the street fighting began—was still hidden inside the hollow base of her mother’s metronome on the Bechstein piano at Foksal 14. Viktor was scheduled to be at General Stahel’s command bunker at the Brühl Palace all afternoon.

Under the starless canopy above Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Anchored by her silent oath, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

"Wait for me at the garden postern gate with the pistols," Krystyna told Phillip as they slipped down the alley behind Ulica Kopernika at four-forty. "I can go up the armoire stair, take the metronome from the piano, and be back in three minutes."

Across the hushed thoroughfares of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. As the shadows lengthened, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

She climbed the hidden stone stair inside the wall, stepped through her mother’s armoire, and walked into the white-and-gold drawing room of Foksal 14.

Along the riverward slope of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Without haste, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Beyond the shuttered windows of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room marked the slow passage of the rationed night. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Keeping her pulse steady, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Within the mirror-lined rooms of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Steeling her resolve once more, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Past the iron railings of Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. With her eyes clear and level, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Sitting on the bench of the Bechstein piano in his sweat-stained shirt, with a half-empty bottle of ammonia smelling salts beside him and his service Walther PPK cocked in his right hand, was Oberst Viktor von Harenberg. And open across the music rack of the piano lay the leather-bound Stankiewicz family photograph album that Sturmbannführer Kessler had brought from the confiscated municipal archives an hour earlier—opened to a full-page 1938 studio portrait labeled *Hrabianka Krystyna Stankiewiczówna*.

* * *

"Krystyna," Viktor said. He pronounced the Polish name slowly, as if tasting broken glass on his tongue. "Count Ignacy Stankiewicz’s daughter. Sitting in my bed in Paris. Sitting in her own father’s house on the Foksal while she fed my trains and my maps to the bandits. And Brandt—Brandt grew up next door to you at Konstancin, didn't he?"

A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Above the slated mansard roofs of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Steeling her resolve once more, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

With cold clarity she reckoned that the Warsaw Uprising had begun—below in the courtyard Tadeusz's fighters were already engaging the Wehrmacht guard, though every detail of five years of silent underground war exploding into open battle at the stroke of five remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the draft stirred the center of the white drawing room as the five o'clock bells struck, the mahogany metronome sitting on the piano lid six feet from her hand remained fixed in place, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. "Listen to the street, Viktor," Krystyna said over the roar of the grenades. "Your thousand-year Reich is over." In the presence of the red-and-white Polish flag unfurling from the balcony of the tenement across Ulica Foksal, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the sudden, earth-shaking eruption of machine guns and grenades in the courtyard below as W-Hour began and the faint drift of cordite smoke drifting through the shattered windowpane and hot summer dust as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. Whatever happened next, she knew that Phillip had heard the gunfire at the postern gate and was already racing up the hidden stone stair inside the wall.

A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. Outside the tall casements of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. With her eyes clear and level, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

"Yes, Viktor," Krystyna said. For the first time in fourteen months, she spoke to him in her own voice—clear, cold, and stripped of every coquettish Strasbourg cadence. "My father was murdered by your people at Palmiry. My mother died on rotten straw in Pawiak. Every night you touched me, you paid for their graves with a piece of your army."

A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Along the darkened tramlines of Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Measuring the room one last time, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Viktor rose slowly from the piano bench, the Walther PPK trembling slightly in his thick, scarred fist. "I would have given you half of Europe, Marion. Even when Kessler showed me this book an hour ago, I told him he was lying—until I opened my map cylinder and found a June rail chart where *Festung Warschau* had been. Did you laugh with Brandt when you came from my bed?"

A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Beneath the looming cornice of Nowy Świat, five years of silent endurance had burst into open flame at the stroke of five. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

"I never spoke your name when I was with him," she said, edging one step closer to the piano where the mahogany metronome sat.

A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the long shadow cast by Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. In the hush that followed, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Outside, across the rooftops of Warsaw, the church bells of Holy Cross and the siren on the Prudential tower suddenly struck the fifth hour: **17:00—Godzina "W"**.

A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Along the damp paving stones of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. As the minute hand crept on, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. From the sentry-guarded gates of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. While the night deepened outside, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Behind the double-bolted doors of Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. Letting the silence work for her, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

In the same heartbeat, the entire street of Nowy Świat and Ulica Foksal erupted in a deafening hurricane of gunfire, exploding *Filipinki* grenades, and shattering window glass as Tadeusz’s assault platoons stormed the German guardhouse in the courtyard below.

* * *

As a grenade explosion blew in the tall garden casements of the drawing room in a shower of glass, the dressing-room door burst open and Phillip lunged across the threshold with his Luger in his hand, shouting, "Krysiu—down!"

She held fast to the hard-won certainty that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Near the sandbagged corners of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. While the night deepened outside, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

She answered the silence with lowering the smoking pistol and dropping to her knees beside Phillip to bind his grazed forearm with a torn strip of curtain linen, letting the fierce, trembling relief of two survivors as their hands met over the blood serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the dying discord of the piano strings and the roar of insurgent gunfire from Nowy Świat at the same moment she breathed in hot cordite, spilled blood, shattered window glass, and the smoke of burning German trucks outside sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. Phillip pulled her against his chest, took the mahogany metronome from the piano, and led her down into the blazing street. Between the doorway and the blood-streaked parquet beside her mother's Bechstein grand piano, Viktor von Harenberg's Walther PPK smoking in her two steady hands marked the half-lit interval, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She knew beyond doubt that with Viktor's own pistol in her father's drawing room, she had ended the fourteen-month nightmare and saved Phillip's life, while the contrast with her mother's Bechstein playing its last chord as the conqueror fell across the keys made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where down in the courtyard cheers in Polish were ringing out as Tadeusz's company raised the white-and-red flag over the gate of Foksal 14.

Experience inside the high command had proved to her that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Down the wind-scoured length of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Letting the silence work for her, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Viktor spun like a cornered boar and fired twice at the doorway. The first 7.65mm bullet took a chunk of mahogany out of the doorjamb; the second grazed Phillip’s left forearm, knocking the Luger spinning across the waxed parquet as Phillip dived straight into Viktor’s knees, driving the heavier fifty-three-year-old colonel backward against the Bechstein grand piano.

She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beyond the guarded courtyard of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. With unhurried grace, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

The two men crashed against the keyboard in a dissonant roar of eighty-eight strings. Despite the luminal still in his blood, Viktor fought with the berserk, bull-necked strength of an old trench soldier—his left hand clamping around Phillip’s wounded arm while his right hand clubbed the barrel of the Walther PPK toward Phillip’s temple, snarling, "You traitor dog—"

Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Without a backward glance, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

The pistol skittered loose from their locked fists across the blood-streaked parquet to Krystyna’s feet.

Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Drawing a slow breath, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Krystyna scooped up Viktor’s Walther PPK in both hands, stepped to within four feet of the piano, and as Viktor wrenched his arm free to drive his trench knife into Phillip’s throat, she pulled the trigger twice.

Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. In that narrow pause between beats, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Both bullets struck Oberst Viktor von Harenberg beneath the left collarbone—through the Iron Cross ribbon and straight through the heart. He stiffened against the open keys of her mother’s Bechstein, looked once into Krystyna’s gray-green eyes with an expression of dark, stunned recognition, and slid lifeless to the floor across the shattered glass.

* * *

Five minutes after Viktor von Harenberg fell dead beside the Bechstein piano, the heavy oak front doors of Foksal 14 burst open below, and Tadeusz Kowalski’s assault platoon swarmed up the grand marble staircase wearing white-and-red armbands over captured German helmets.

In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Across the wrought-iron second-floor balcony of Foksal 14 overlooking the street, the great white-and-red flag of Poland unfurling in the smoke of the first hour of the Uprising rested in the quiet shadow, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Down on the street, old Hanna Nowakowa looked up from the crowd, crossed herself, and wept with joy. It was in small things like Phillip's bandaged left arm resting beside hers on the iron balustrade under the red-and-white cloth that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She measured the risk by the certainty that standing on her murdered father's balcony with Phillip's arm around her shoulders as the Polish flag flew once more over Foksal 14 was the resurrection of everything they had bled for, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Count Ignacy Stankiewicz standing on this very balcony on November eleventh, 1938, saluting the lancers below. As she completed knotting the flag halyard fast to the iron scroll of the Stankiewicz crest with both hands, she drew the proud, tear-streaked face of Krystyna Stankiewiczówna in the open sun around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to three hundred voices on Nowy Świat singing the national anthem above the crackle of rifle fire while the room held the aroma of cordite smoke, summer wind, and the hot tears running freely down Krystyna's cheeks until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing.

Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Beside the stone balustrades of Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. In that narrow pause between beats, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

"The courtyard guardhouse is taken!" Tadeusz shouted up the stairwell in Polish, raising his captured Schmeisser as he saw Krystyna and Phillip standing at the second-floor landing with Viktor’s Walther PPK in Krystyna’s hand. "Krystyna—here!"

Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Through the narrow archways of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Tadeusz threw a rolled bundle of white-and-red wool bunting up the marble banister. Phillip caught it with his unwounded right hand, and together he and Krystyna walked out through the shattered glass of the drawing room onto the wrought-iron balcony of Foksal 14 overlooking the street.

Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Past the shuttered shopfronts of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Across the curfew-bound sweep of Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. With practiced serenity, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Across the darkened perspective of Nowy Świat, five years of silent endurance had burst into open flame at the stroke of five. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. In the amber half-light, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Under the hooded blue lamps of Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

As Krystyna tied the staff of the white-and-red Polish flag to the iron railing where the Wehrmacht sign had hung for four years, and the crimson-and-white banner unfurled in the smoke-filled August wind, three hundred people down on Nowy Świat and Ulica Foksal dropped to their knees on the cobblestones, weeping and singing *Jeszcze Polska nie zginęła*—Poland has not perished yet.

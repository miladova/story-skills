# Chapter 13 — The Attic on Ulica Freta

*Warsaw — Christmas Eve 1943*

---

On Christmas Eve—*Wigilia*, the sacred night when every Polish family broke the white unleavened *opłatek* wafer when the first star appeared in the eastern sky—Warsaw lay under a bitter, diamond-clear frost. At the Brühl Palace, Governor Fischer and Oberst von Harenberg were hosting a compulsory *Weihnachtsfeier* for the senior officers of the garrison from five until nine in the evening, followed by a midnight staff supper.

She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that for thenext three and a half hours Marion Herschlag was supposedly asleep with a migraine behind locked doors on Ulica Foksal, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of hiding in this same stone passage with her brother during hide-and-seek on Christmas Eve 1932. By the threshold of the hidden stone service stair inside the walls of Foksal 14, the plain charcoal wool shawl and flat-heeled Polish winter boots she wore over her dress stood out in sharp relief, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. When she stepped out into the back alley, her boots crunched in the snow like any Polish clerk's wife hurrying home for Wigilia. It was in small things like the rusted iron bolt of the garden postern gate oiled the night before that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the soft click of the oiled oak panel closing behind the armoire shelves while the room held the aroma of lime mortar, century-old brick dust, and cold winter draft until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that if Viktor returned before nine-thirty or sent the maid in with tea, the bolted bedroom door was her only buffer.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Down the wind-scoured length of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

At half past five, Marion pleaded a violent migraine brought on by the glare off the snow, kissing Viktor’s cheek at the door of Foksal 14 as he departed in full dress uniform. "Rest until nine, Liebchen," Viktor told her, adjusting his Knight’s Cross war-merit ribbon in the hall glass. "I will send Hanke back with the car at nine-thirty to bring you to the supper."

Through the narrow archways of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Anchored by her silent oath, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Three minutes after the tail-lamps of Viktor’s Mercedes turned onto Nowy Świat, Krystyna locked the master bedroom doors from the inside, stuffed two bolsters beneath the down duvet, and stepped into her mother’s dressing-room armoire. She slid back the oiled oak panel behind the shoe shelves and descended the narrow, pitch-black stone service stair into the walled back garden.

The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Combining the bowed, hurried walk of an Old Town resident avoiding the patrols with the quiet motion of pulling the charcoal shawl high across her nose and cheekbones as she turned into the archway of Number 19, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of woodsmoke, fried carp from poor kitchens, spruce boughs, and twenty-degree frost hung in the draft alongside the muffled singing of a Polish Christmas carol—'Bóg się rodzi'—behind shuttered tenement windows filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. She climbed the four flights of creaking wooden stairs to the attic and tapped twice, then once, on the timber door. In the hush that filled the narrow, snow-choked medieval street of Ulica Freta in the Old Town, the gothic brick tower of Saint Hyacinth's Dominican church rising against the first star appeared almost motionless, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that all across this conquered, starving city, families were breaking the white wafer and leaving an empty chair at the table for the dead, though the thought of her father breaking the opłatek with her and her mother in the Foksal dining room while the tree blazed with wax candles returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that in the attic above the bookbinder's shop, Wanda was waiting to carry the Gestapo arrest list to Tadeusz's runners before eight o'clock.

Wrapped in a plain charcoal wool shawl and wearing Wanda’s borrowed flat-heeled winter boots instead of Parisian furs, she slipped through the postern gate into the alley behind Ulica Kopernika and walked northward through the snow toward the Old Town—*Stare Miasto*.

Above the crooked seventeenth-century gables of Ulica Freta, the first winter star was just piercing the indigo sky over the frozen Vistula.

* * *

The attic door opened a crack, and Wanda Zieleńska drew her inside. The slanted garret under the Old Town roof tiles was lit by a single tallow candle set inside a chipped saucer; on the rough pine table lay a sprig of green fir and a single square of white *opłatek* wafer. A tiny iron goat-stove burned peat briquettes in the corner.

Past the edge of the low-beamed garret under the seventeenth-century roof tiles of Ulica Freta, the four carbon-flimsy sheets of Sturmbannführer Kessler's Christmas Eve raid order waited in the gloom, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. When Wanda's footsteps vanished on the ground floor, the silence in the candlelit attic became deafening. She let her attention brush past the Seventh Entry on Kessler's list where 'Kowalski, Uhrmacher' had been typed in black ribbon, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She held fast to the recognition that Kessler had put Tadeusz's clock shop on the midnight raid list—which meant Phillip's theft of this list had just saved Tadeusz's life and the entire Warsaw network, though every detail of the Christmas Eves of their youth when Heinrich Brandt used to walk across the snow at Konstancin to toast Count Ignacy remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, holding the thin white fragment of the unleavened opłatek wafer in her trembling fingers reinforced the quiet authority of the stripped-bare truth of two people standing at the center of the cyclone, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the crackle of peat in the tiny iron stove and Wanda's footsteps fading down the wooden stair and the faint drift of tallow candle wax, fresh fir needles, sheepskin, and melting snow steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray.

Beside the stone balustrades of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Two minutes later, footsteps came swiftly up the dark stair—light, sure, taking two treads at a time. The door opened, and Phillip stepped into the garret. Over his uniform he wore a long civilian railwayman’s sheepskin coat dusted with snow; his face was raw from the frost, his dark eyes blazing in the candlelight.

Without a word, he reached inside the sheepskin coat and laid four carbon-flimsy sheets on the pine table: *Sicherheitspolizei und SD Warschau — Sonderaktion Heiligabend (24.XII.1943, 23:00 Uhr)*.

A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Measuring the room one last time, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

"To the spring, Krysiu," Phillip whispered in Polish, and placed the white wafer on her tongue before his mouth took hers. No illusion could soften the fact that for five years they had not shared a Christmas Eve, and now they stood with the white wafer between them while forty Gestapo trucks warmed their engines across the city, while the contrast with the Polish custom that those who break the opłatek together on Wigilia will find their way back to each other before the year turns made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the soft murmur of church bells ringing across the snow from the Old Town Cathedral at the same moment she breathed in unleavened wheat wafer, spruce resin, and the warm breath rising between them that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Nothing about Phillip's bare hand shedding the railwayman's sheepskin coat onto the floorboards escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted holding out her piece of the white wafer so Phillip could break a corner from hers as she broke a corner from his and the familiar armor of eyes shining wet and luminous in the yellow candlelight to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when whatever tomorrow brought in the cellars of Warsaw, tonight belonged to Krystyna and Phillip.

"Forty-two addresses across Żoliborz, Mokotów, and Stare Miasto," Phillip said in Polish, his finger dropping onto the third line. "Kessler signed the raid order at four this afternoon. Look at number seven, Wanda: *Ulica Chmielna 22 — A. Kowalski, Uhrmacher*."

Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the long shadow cast by the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Without a backward glance, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

Krystyna’s blood ran cold. "Tadeusz."

"The trucks do not roll from Aleja Szucha until eleven tonight," Phillip said, handing the four sheets to Wanda. "If your runners move now, every single address—including Chmielna 22—can be emptied and clean three hours before the Gestapo knocks."

Wanda tucked the carbon sheets inside her wool corset, broke a corner from the white *opłatek* wafer on the table to press into Krystyna’s and Phillip’s hands—*"May God keep you both until a free Poland,"* the girl whispered with tears in her eyes—and slipped out the door down the stairs. The wooden latch clicked shut, leaving Krystyna and Phillip alone under the snow-laden roof of the Old Town.

* * *

When Phillip kissed her in the candlelit garret, there was no hurry left in the world—only an agonizingly slow, luminous fire that had waited five winters in the dark to burn. He unfastened the rough wool shawl from her shoulders, then knelt on the pine floorboards to unlace Wanda’s borrowed boots from her feet, warming each of her silk-stockinged insteps between his palms before he rose.

Against the quiet of the room, the way Phillip's lips had kissed the fading bruise on her hip before claiming her stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Not three paces from the narrow pallet mattress beside the glowing iron stove in the Freta garret, Phillip's discarded Wehrmacht tunic thrown into the dark corner out of the candlelight lay in the amber half-light, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the choreography of the room, tracing every rib and muscle of his bare back while his mouth worshipped her breasts flowed seamlessly into no mask on earth—only a woman weeping with joy and passion in her lover's arms, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. In the golden circle of the single candle, the uniform in the corner ceased to exist. With cold clarity she reckoned that for the first time since she had become a spy, her body and her soul were in the same bed at the same moment, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the boy who had held her hand by the Jeziorka river now grown into a man whose tenderness was stronger than any army. Before the night was over, this tender, reverent fire was ten times more dangerous to her cover than Viktor's brutality, because it made her want to live, and she intended to be ready.

A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. Yet in the stolen, breathless stillness with Phillip, their love was the one truth in Warsaw that needed no cipher. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. With her eyes clear and level, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

"Let me look at you," he whispered, his hands trembling as he unbuttoned the high collar of her dark wool dress and slid it down over her shoulders. Unlike Viktor, who tore at silk in the dark as if plundering a conquered town, Phillip undressed her with a slow, worshipful reverence that made every nerve beneath her skin ache with unbearable sweetness. When she stood before the little iron stove in only her pale skin and the single candle’s gold light, his dark eyes moved over the faint, fading shadow of Viktor’s fingers on her hip—and he dropped to his knees on the floorboards and pressed his mouth softly, fervently, to the bruise, as if his lips could draw the poison from her flesh.

A broken sob tore from Krystyna’s throat. Her fingers tangled in his dark-gold hair, pulling him up until he unbuttoned his field-gray tunic and threw the hated uniform coat into the far dark corner of the eaves, coming to her on the narrow pallet mattress in only his bare skin.

At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Beneath the looming cornice of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. Whenever Phillip's hand found hers in the shadows, every wall she had built to survive the war dissolved into pure truth. With unhurried grace, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond cradling his face between her palms so she could watch the dark, luminous wonder in his eyes as he moved inside her and a breathless, whispered litany of Polish endearments against his lips, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. Nothing the Reich possessed could ever touch what they had just reclaimed under the Old Town roof. Framed by the geometry of the slanted wooden eaves above the pallet bed, the coarse wool blanket tangled around their bare, sweat-sheened legs seemed almost innocuous, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She observed the rapid, synchronous thud of their two hearts pressed breast to breast with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She knew beyond doubt that with every slow, deep thrust of his body while their eyes stayed locked together, five years of loneliness burned away into pure light, though the thought of the night in August 1938 when they had lain in the hayloft at Konstancin and promised never to belong to anyone else returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of peat smoke, warm linen, and the salt of tears dried on their cheeks hung in the draft alongside the distant chime of the Old Town bells striking eight through the frosty night pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence.

Against the slow, luminous fire that burned between her and Phillip, five winters of exile burned away in a passion more perilous than any bullet. Near the iron-grated cellars of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Skin to skin under the coarse wool blanket, the heat between them was a revelation that undid every defense the hardened spy had built. Phillip kissed her mouth, her eyelids, the hollow of her throat, the soft undersides of her breasts, taking his time until she was arching beneath his hands and whispering his name in Polish over and over—*Filipie, mój Filipie*. When he finally parted her thighs and slid inside her, slow and deep and shaking with the effort of his own restraint, Krystyna gasped aloud at the difference: there was no war here, no armor, no calculation—only an all-consuming, reverent fullness that joined them heart to heart and bone to bone.

She wrapped her legs high around his waist, her hands framing his lean, tear-wet cheeks so their eyes stayed wide open and locked together in the candlelight as they moved. Every slow, rolling stroke of his body inside hers felt like a vow reclaimed from death; when the crest broke over them both, she buried her mouth against his shoulder to muffle her cry, while Phillip shuddered deep inside her, holding her crushed against his heart as if he would never let the world tear her from his arms again.

"Be careful tonight at the supper, Krysiu," he whispered. "When Kessler finds forty empty nests at midnight, he will smell smoke." The sight of the sprig of green fir Phillip tucked inside the pocket of her wool shawl brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the low whistle of the wind off the Vistula embankment below and the faint drift of cooling skin, spruce resin, and the lingering warmth of his mouth on her collarbone an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Where the draft stirred the small frosted skylight of the attic at half past eight, her dark wool dress being buttoned up her spine by Phillip's gentle fingers remained fixed in place, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. With the gathering of her iron resolve before stepping back into the frost governing the motion of turning in his arms once her dress was buttoned to press one last, desperate kiss to the corner of his jaw to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, letting her fingers tremble for the only man who knew her true name and held it sacred. She measured the risk by the certainty that by now Wanda and Tadeusz's runners had evacuated Chmielna 22 and all forty-two addresses on Kessler's Christmas Eve list, though every detail of the empty chair left at Polish Christmas Eve tables for the traveler returning from afar remained as sharp as cut glass.

* * *

At nine-fifteen, Krystyna climbed the hidden stone stair inside the wall of Foksal 14, stepped out through the back of her mother’s armoire, and unlocked the bedroom door just five minutes before Corporal Hanke rang the courtyard bell with the Mercedes. In the Venetian mirror, she powdered over the glow in her cheeks, pinned her hair high, and fastened Viktor’s cabochon emeralds around the throat Phillip had kissed an hour before.

Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Along the darkened tramlines of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Letting the silence work for her, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Between the doorway and the candlelit dining hall of the Brühl Palace at ten on Christmas Eve, the silver-frosted fir branches and crystal decanters lining the high command table marked the half-lit interval, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Her senses caught the clink of Meissen porcelain and German officers singing 'Stille Nacht' around the piano at the same moment she breathed in roast goose, red cabbage, beeswax candles, and the cold starch of Kessler's black tunic as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. "I do hope your Christmas present is more amusing than that dreadful little notebook in Paris, Herr Sturmbannführer," she purred. In the presence of the antique pocket watch Kessler checked beside his wineglass as the clock neared eleven, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with extending her white-gloved hand to Kessler with a cool, languid smile, letting the glittering, untouchable poise of Marion Herschlag recovered from her headache serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where at eleven o'clock Kessler's trucks would roll out from Aleja Szucha and find forty-two empty rooms.

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Within the high-walled gardens of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

When she entered the Brühl Palace dining hall at ten, the long table was banked with silver-frosted fir branches and blazing candelabra. Sitting three seats down from Viktor, immaculate in his black SD uniform with the silver runes, was Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler—newly arrived from Berlin.

Across the curfew-bound sweep of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Without haste, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

And at the junior staff table near the doors sat Hauptmann Phillip Brandt, his face calm and pale above his high collar.

"Fräulein Herschlag," Kessler said smoothly, rising to bow as Viktor seated her. "You see, I kept my promise at the Gare de l’Est. Warsaw in winter has a charm of its own. And by midnight tonight, I expect to give the Governor a very substantial Christmas present."

* * *

At ten minutes past midnight on Christmas Eve, just as Governor Fischer was raising his crystal flute of champagne to propose a toast to the Führer in the Brühl Palace dining hall, an SS Untersturmführer hurried into the room with pale cheeks and whispered urgently into Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler’s ear.

Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Beyond the guarded courtyard of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. In that narrow pause between beats, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

She drank a sip of the German champagne to the silent health of the forty-two men who had escaped the night. Across the candlelit banquet table of the Brühl Palace at ten minutes past midnight, the cracked crystal base of Sturmbannführer Kessler's champagne flute on the damask cloth rested in the quiet shadow, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She listened to the murmur of the SS courier's whispered report and the clink of eighty glasses raised in toast while the room held the aroma of spruce resin, beeswax, champagne, and the sudden icy wind of Kessler's humiliation sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. There was no escaping the conclusion that all forty-two Home Army commanders—including Tadeusz Kowalski—were alive and safe under different roofs tonight because of the two hours in the Freta garret, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the single tallow candle in the saucer on Ulica Freta where she and Phillip had lain in each other's arms three hours ago. As she completed raising her champagne glass smoothly toward Governor Fischer with a radiant social smile, she drew flawless, festive poise under the inquisitor's stare around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that from this midnight hour onward, Erich Kessler would hunt the mole inside the Brühl Palace with the ferocity of a wounded viper.

Past the shuttered shopfronts of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. As the shadows lengthened, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Krystyna, seated three places away with her fingers resting lightly on the stem of her wineglass, watched the blood drain from Kessler’s thin face until his skin looked like tallow under the candles. All forty-two Gestapo squads had reported in from Żoliborz, Mokotów, Ulica Chmielna, and the Old Town: forty-two doors battered down in the frost, and forty-two empty rooms.

The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Beyond the shuttered windows of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. In the hush that followed, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

Kessler set his untouched champagne flute down so hard the crystal base cracked against the tablecloth. His gin-pale eyes swept slowly up the length of the candlelit table—past Viktor, past Marion’s glittering emeralds, and down to the junior staff table where Hauptmann Phillip Brandt sat calmly peeling a winter apple with a fruit knife.

Across the sea of candles and swastika banners, Krystyna did not look at Phillip; she lifted her glass to Governor Fischer’s toast and smiled with the cold, untouchable serenity of an angel standing on a minefield.

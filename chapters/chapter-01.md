# Chapter 1 — The Chandelier and the Collar

*Paris — May 1943*

---

In the mirrored salon of the Hôtel de Varennes on the Avenue Foch, the crystal chandeliers had been fitted with amber bulbs to appease the blackout wardens, yet they still burned bright enough to turn every glass of stolen Krug champagne into liquid brass. Marion Herschlag stood beneath the tallest of the gilded pier glasses, the bias-cut emerald silk of her Vionnet gown clinging to the hollow of her waist and the long, pale curve of her spine. She was twenty-five years old, and to every German general officer in the room she was the spoils of Europe made flesh—Alsatian by paper, Swiss by finishing school, and wholly the property of Oberst Viktor Freiherr von Harenberg.

Every man in the Hôtel de Varennes believed a beautiful woman's vanity was a mirror in which only he was reflected. Across the tall gilded console beneath the Gobelin tapestry, a silver tray of half-empty champagne flutes and crushed Turkish cigarettes rested in the quiet shadow, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She listened to the brittle cadence of Parisian French colliding with clipped Prussian consonants while the room held the aroma of stolen Chanel Cuir de Russie and the heavy beeswax polish of the parquet filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. She held fast to the recognition that Major Lüttwitz's drunken boast about seventeen stalled munitions trains at the Oder bridgehead matched the exact gap in London's intelligence, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her father's white-gloved butler pouring Tokaj in the dining room on Ulica Foksal before the bombs fell. As she completed a slow, languid turn of her wrist as she set her crystal flute upon the marble, she drew the bored, sensuous half-smile of a woman who cared only for jewels and winter furs around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that the cipher clerk on Rue Saint-Séverin would be waiting for the rail numbers before the six o'clock curfew.

"You are not drinking, Liebchen," Viktor murmured behind her. He did not announce his approach; for a man of fifty-three with a barrel chest and the heavy, rolling gait of an old cavalryman, he moved across parquet like a wolf over crusted snow. His broad palm settled low on the naked skin of her back, just where the emerald silk plunged toward the dimple of her spine. His thumb pressed inward—not a caress, but a brand.

Marion tilted her chin, letting her dark gold hair brush the silver-braided shoulder board of his field-gray tunic. "When Comtesse Solange serves the thirty-seven vintage to Major Lüttwitz, Viktor, someone must remain sober enough to watch him spill it on her Aubusson rug." She spoke the crisp, lightly musical High German of Strasbourg, rounding her vowels just enough to make the Prussian officers smile.

A subtle trace of roasted chestnuts, expensive cigar smoke, and the cold iron smell of wet wool hung in the draft alongside a distant Wehrmacht motorcycle patrol backfiring down the Avenue Foch steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She had learned in her first month at the Hôtel Majestic that German staff officers guarded their maps from men, never from the women they undressed. She let her attention brush past the dueling scar that bisected Major Lüttwitz's flushed left cheek, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining an expression of pampered, feline contentment with the quiet motion of the subtle arch of her collarbone as she leaned backward into the heat of Viktor's palm, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. No illusion could soften the fact that Viktor had drunk three glasses of cognac yet had not missed a single glance exchanged across the room, though the thought of the first night in Zurich when her Polish handler had handed her the dead Strasbourg woman's birth certificate returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she needed three uninterrupted minutes with the dispatch case locked in the boot of his Horch.

Viktor’s laugh was a low grate in his throat, scarred twenty-seven years ago by phosgene gas on the Meuse. His cold blue eyes swept the salon—past the swastika pennants draped beside seventeenth-century Gobelin tapestries, past the French industrialists laughing too loudly at German jokes—before returning to the pulse at the base of her throat. "Lüttwitz is a quartermaster clerk in a major’s collar tabs. He has spent the last twenty minutes staring at your breasts and babbling about locomotive shortages east of Breslau."

"Has he?" Marion lifted her flute to her lips, her gray-green eyes lazy and amused over the rim. "How tedious of him. Though if he is short of locomotives in Silesia, I suppose I shall have to forgive you for being two hours late to dinner on Tuesday."

With cold clarity she reckoned that every name murmured near the buffet was another thread in the supply net strangling her homeland, though every detail of her mother's emerald ring, pried from Helena Stankiewiczowa's finger by a Gestapo clerk at Pawiak remained as sharp as cut glass. Beside the marble mantelpiece crowned by a Sèvres clock, the outline of Comtesse Solange's diamond lorgnette swinging from a black velvet ribbon marked the stillness, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Beneath the bias-cut emerald silk, her heart beat with the slow, icy regularity of a metronome. Nothing about the gold-embossed eagle on the cover of the adjutant's leather folder escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the solo violinist in the alcove drawing a mournful phrase from Debussy and the faint drift of hothouse white lilacs wilting in the overheated salon air that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, the eastern transport timetables would change at midnight on the fifteenth, whatever price it demanded.

"On Tuesday," Viktor said softly, his fingers tightening against the bone of her hip until she felt the familiar, bruising heat of his grip through the thin silk, "you will not care what hour the clock strikes. Smile at the Comtesse, Marion. She is watching to see whether I keep you on a short leash or a long one."

* * *

Before calling for his car, Viktor drew her into the small octagonal card room behind the library and shut the double doors with his boot heel. The key turned once in the brass lock. He did not switch on the sconce; only the amber bar of light beneath the door and the glow of a dying coal grate illuminated the red damask walls and the green baize gaming tables.

She trusted the deliberate tilt of her throat into the broad span of his fingers and the familiar armor of a defiant, half-aroused insolence that she knew drove him past reason to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the muffled laughter of the salon guests filtering through two inches of oak at the same moment she breathed in dying anthracite embers, old leather bindings, and Viktor's vetiver cologne that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. He did not want fear from her; fear bored him, because every clerk and conquered citizen in Europe gave him fear. In the corner of the octagonal card room's green baize table, she noticed an ivory-inlaid cribbage board and a pair of untouched crystal tumblers, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She knew beyond doubt that his jealousy was always sharpest on the nights when his Berlin dispatches weighed heaviest on him, while the contrast with the cold cellar in Lwów where she had learned to control her breathing under a spotlight made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when if he suspected her morning rides in the Bois were brush passes with Zygmunt, she would never leave the Rue de Rivoli alive.

"Hauptmann Meissner of the Luftwaffe," Viktor said, his voice dropping an octave as he crowded her back against the edge of the mahogany whist table, "asked the Comtesse whether you were still fond of riding in the Bois de Boulogne at nine in the morning. How does a twenty-six-year-old fighter pilot know what hour you ride, Marion?"

She did not flinch when his hands bracketed her hips, lifting her slightly so the backs of her thighs pressed against the carved rim of the table. "Because half of Paris has eyes, Viktor, and because your own orderly requisitions my mare from the Tattersall stables every Monday and Thursday. If you wish me to ride veiled like a Turkish wife, you have only to say so."

From where she stood in the red damask wall beside the hearth, the brass key protruding from the interior keyhole of the double doors was just visible, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Under her palm, the heavy thud of his heart was faster than her own. She observed the silver death's-head cufflinks he wore only on nights when he had met with the SD with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She measured the risk by the certainty that by challenging him back, she turned an interrogation into foreplay inside ten seconds, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her father's warning that a bully always looks at the eyes before he looks at the hands. In the choreography of the room, her fingers sliding slowly up the front of his field-gray wool tunic to rest over his ribs flowed seamlessly into the dark, heavy-lidded challenge of a woman who matched his ruthlessness, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the pop of a cooling coal settling in the iron grate while the room held the aroma of scorched wool and the crushed gardenia at her waist pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence.

His jaw tightened. One large, calloused hand rose to her throat—not choking, but spanning the delicate column of her windpipe, his thumb resting directly over the carotid pulse to feel whether it jumped. It was an old interrogator's trick. Krystyna had practiced slowing her heart rate in dark closets until she could lie to the devil himself without a flutter.

"I do not share what is mine," Viktor said softly, his mouth an inch from hers, his breath warm with Courvoisier. "Not with fighter pilots who have milk on their chins, and not with ghosts. When I look at you in a room full of men, I want to see only my mark on you."

When his mouth came down hard on hers, tasting of brandy and salt, she opened to him without reserve. She did not allow herself to forget that he was carrying the eastern rail schedules in his personal briefcase tonight rather than leaving them at the Majestic, though the thought of the charred ruins of the Royal Castle in Warsaw as she had last seen them through a train window in 1940 returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of wet Parisian slate and the warm musk rising from her emerald silk hung in the draft alongside rain beginning to spatter against the leaded transom window overhead an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. The sight of the stiff silver cord of his General Staff aiguillette brushing her bare collarbone brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond a slow intake of breath that pressed her breasts against the medals on his chest and the fierce, unbowed hunger he mistook for passion, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Not a single second could be wasted when every minute spent in this card room brought him closer to the careless sleep she needed after midnight.

She lifted her chin into the pressure of his palm, letting her lips part until they brushed the rough corner of his mouth. "Then look closer, Herr Oberst," she whispered against his skin. "Have you ever found another man's shadow in your bed?"

* * *

Outside, a thin May rain was falling across the Avenue Foch, turning the cobblestones into black mirrors under the slitted blue headlamps of the Wehrmacht staff cars. Viktor’s chauffeur, Corporal Hanke, held the rear door of the long black Horch open and saluted as Viktor handed Marion inside. On the floorboard beside the jump seat rested the flat, steel-reinforced calfskin dispatch case that never left the Colonel’s sight.

In the presence of the small brass Eagle-and-Swastika medallion bolted to the Horch's walnut dashboard, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the shadow fell across the plush gray broadcloth interior of the Horch staff car, the steel-reinforced calfskin dispatch case wedged between Viktor's jackboots remained half-hidden, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. With the fastidious disdain of a Western European woman who found the East barbaric governing the motion of a languid, indifferent shrug of her fur-draped shoulders to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She let her bare knee brush against his wool-clad thigh as the car turned onto the Rue de Rivoli. There was no escaping the conclusion that the central Polish corridor meant the rail lines running straight through Siedlce, Rembertów, and Warsaw East, though every detail of riding the summer train from Warsaw to Konstancin with wind in her hair and a basket of wild strawberries remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that if the Armia Krajowa knew which forty-eight-hour window carried the heavy artillery shells, a single charge could cripple an entire panzer corps.

As the Horch glided down toward the Place de l’Étoile and the Champs-Élysées, Paris lay entombed in the blackout. The Arc de Triomphe was a hulking monolith against the bruised clouds; along the Rue de Rivoli, the arcades stood empty save for the two-man Feldgendarmerie patrols in their steel gorgets, their boots striking the wet stone in rhythmic pairs.

In the dark rear cabin, Viktor sat with his heavy thigh pressed against hers, his gloved hand resting atop the handle of the calfskin case between his boots. "Berlin is tearing up the summer replacement schedules," he said, staring out at the rain-streaked silhouette of the Place de la Concorde. "They want thirty trainloads of heavy artillery ammunition moved through the central Polish corridor every forty-eight hours before the Kursk offensive opens. And the damned partisans between Brest and Warsaw are blowing a culvert every third night."

She answered the silence with the graceful extension of her gloved hand as Viktor helped her from the running board, letting the serene entitlement of a high officer's favored companion serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Every fork, every sheet, and every painting in the apartment upstairs had belonged to people whose names were now ash. Under the dim lamp near the rain-slick colonnade of the Rue de Rivoli, the steel gorgets of two Feldgendarmerie sentries gleaming under a hooded blue lamp cast a narrow shade, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. To anyone else, the iron grillwork of the courtyard gate bearing the monogram of a Jewish banking family deported to Drancy might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Her training insisted that every sentry at this gate knew her face and waved her through without checking her handbag, while the contrast with the night in October 1939 when German trucks had first rolled down Nowy Świat with loudspeakers blaring made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the crunch of tires pulling into the guarded courtyard opposite the Tuileries at the same moment she breathed in wet stone, coal smoke, and the river damp rising off the Seine sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

Marion rested her cheek against the fur collar of her sable wrap, watching the blue dashboard light play across his granite profile. "Poland," she murmured with a delicate shudder of distaste, as any pampered Strasbourg woman would. "A dreadful country. Mud, fanatics, and no decent wine. Thank heaven they keep you at the Majestic, Viktor."

He turned his head slowly in the dark of the car. "Do not thank heaven too quickly, Marion. General von Stülpnagel cannot hold onto every logistics officer in France forever if the Ostbahn keeps bleeding."

When the elevator doors slid open on the fourth-floor landing, the silence of the apartment greeted them like a drawn blade. It was in small things like the fresh mud from the Fontainebleau artillery range still caked on the welt of his boot that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the clank of the elevator cables ascending to the fourth floor while the room held the aroma of rainwater steaming off wool greatcoats and the crushed gardenia at her breast until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. By the threshold of the mirrored cage of the wrought-iron elevator, the brass key ring Viktor unclipped from his belt beneath his greatcoat stood out in sharp relief, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. As she completed leaning her head briefly against the wet wool of his shoulder as the cage rose, she drew a heavy, wine-warmed drowsiness that promised him an easy conquest upstairs around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She forced herself to accept that the key to the calfskin dispatch case was the small flat Zeiss-Ikon key on the secondary ring, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of learning to read mirror-reversed numbers in the cellar training school in Hampstead.

Something cold and electric passed through Krystyna’s veins at his words, though her hand beneath the sable wrap remained utterly still. Warsaw. Even the name spoken in his harsh Prussian cadence made the scar tissue inside her chest pull tight.

* * *

The bedroom overlooked the blacked-out expanse of the Tuileries Gardens, its tall casements shrouded in heavy crimson velour. Viktor set the calfskin dispatch case beside the empire armoire, unbuckled his belt and holstered Walther PPK, and laid them on the marble-topped commode. When he turned, he had not yet unbuttoned his collar; his blue eyes were fixed on her with that dark, predatory stillness that always preceded the storm.

In the hush that filled the tall gilded pier glass beside the four-poster bed, the pooled emerald silk of her torn gown lying on the Persian carpet appeared almost motionless, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. A subtle trace of sweat, spilled Armagnac, crushed gardenias, and the salt of bruised skin hung in the draft alongside the harsh, ragged cadence of their breathing and the creak of the bedstead filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. In the bedroom of the enemy, intimacy was not surrender; it was the most ruthless ambush of all. Through the lens of her cover identity, the jagged white shrapnel scar curving beneath Viktor's left ribcage from Verdun was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a feral, unyielding passion that convinced him no other woman in Europe could match his fire with the quiet motion of the deliberate arch of her spine as she met the bruising weight of his thrusts, she gave the room only what it expected to see, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that the chloral draft in the Armagnac needed twenty minutes to pull him down into a dead, dreamless stupor.

"Come here," he said.

Marion did not lower her eyes. She walked to him across the Persian carpet, the emerald silk whispering against her thighs, and stopped close enough that the silver buttons of his tunic grazed the tips of her breasts. Before he could reach for the hooks of her gown, he caught a handful of her pinned gold hair at the nape and wrenched her head back, taking her mouth with a harsh, bruising hunger that tasted of brandy and iron. She did not go limp in his grip. She met the violence of his mouth with a feral heat of her own, her nails raking the heavy wool of his shoulders as her tongue answered his.

She watched his throat work as he drained the glass to the last amber drop and set it empty on the nightstand. Past the edge of the marble-topped commode across the shadowed bedchamber, Viktor's holstered Walther PPK resting beside his silver key ring waited in the gloom, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She was acutely aware of the steady patter of May rain against the crimson velour blackout curtains and the faint drift of warm linen, brandy, and the musky heat of spent flesh steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that he always drank the entire glass of Armagnac in three swallows when his blood was still hot from bed, though every detail of the chemistry lectures of Professor Kowalski in Warsaw on the exact dosage of sedatives by body weight remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, the slow, languorous trace of her fingertips down the center of his damp chest as she handed him the crystal tumbler reinforced the quiet authority of the drowsy, sated tenderness of a lover who had been thoroughly conquered, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, if she gave him too much chloral he would suspect drugging at dawn; too little and the click of the lock would wake him, whatever price it demanded.

A low groan tore from Viktor’s chest. With one rough jerk, he split the delicate silk hooks at her spine, stripping the emerald Vionnet down to her waist so the cool air of the room hit her bare breasts. His hands—broad, scarred, rough-palmed—closed over her flesh with possessive force, his thumbs brushing hard across her nipples until she arched against him with a sharp, ragged gasp. He spun her around and drove her forward against the tall gilded pier glass beside the bed, pressing the length of his heavy body against her naked back.

In the dim amber reflection of the mirror, Krystyna watched the two of them: the fifty-three-year-old Prussian colonel with his silver temples and scarred ribs, and the pale, slender woman pinned beneath his weight, her breasts crushed against the cold glass, her eyes glittering like wet slate. When he yanked the silk skirt up around her hips and parted her thighs with his knee, she braced her palms flat against her own reflection and pushed back against him, meeting the first brutal, searing thrust of his body with a low, throated cry that made his breath hiss through his teeth.

Her senses caught the gradual slowing of Viktor's breath into a deep, guttural snore at the same moment she breathed in almond-scented soap, damp hair, and the heavy wool of his discarded tunic that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. When she touched the lashes of his left eye with the tip of her little finger, he did not stir. Nothing about the slack, unguarded line of Viktor's mouth against the linen pillow escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted sliding her naked thigh out from beneath the heavy weight of his knee an inch at a time and the familiar armor of utter physical stillness as she tested the depth of his sleep to see her through, turning his possessive hunger into the very instrument of his blindness. She held fast to the recognition that she had exactly thirty-five minutes of guaranteed unconsciousness before the drug began to thin in his bloodstream, while the contrast with counting seconds in the dark while German boots searched the cellar of her aunt's house in 1940 made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when the Riga Minox camera in the dressing table held only eighteen exposures left on its brass cassette.

It was never gentle between them; gentleness would have been a lie neither of them could stomach. He took her with a ruthless, battering rhythm that rattled the gilded frame of the pier glass, one hand clamped over her hipbone hard enough to leave violet fingerprints by morning, the other tangled in her hair to keep her face turned toward the mirror so she had to watch him possess her. And Marion—eyes locked on his in the glass, lips parted, skin slick with sweat—matched him stroke for stroke, clenching around him, raking her nails back across his thigh, feeding his vanity and his carnal obsession until he dragged her down onto the tangled linen of the four-poster bed and spent himself inside her with a hoarse, shuddering shout against her bruised throat.

Afterward, while he lay breathing heavily on his back with one arm still flung possessively across her ribs, she rose on one elbow, her hair falling in damp gold ropes over her bare breasts, and poured two fingers of dark Armagnac from the bedside decanter. With her back turned for the space of a single heartbeat, her thumbnail flicked the tiny glass ampoule hidden in the hollow of her ring, letting ten clear, tasteless drops fall into the amber liquor before she carried the glass to his lips.

* * *

At a quarter past three in the morning, the dressing room off the bedchamber was lit only by a single fifteen-watt bulb shaded with green parchment. Krystyna stood naked before the marble washstand, a silk kimono thrown loosely over her shoulders, and twisted the ivory handle of her tortoiseshell hairbrush three turns to the left. The bristle plate came away in her palm, revealing a velvet-lined cavity and the gleaming, three-inch stainless-steel cylinder of a Riga Minox camera.

No illusion could soften the fact that Train 4418 carrying eighty tons of heavy howitzer fuses would halt for forty minutes at the Wawer siding outside Warsaw, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the railway bridge over the Vistula where she and Phillip had watched the ice break in March of 1938. Not three paces from the marble washstand in the narrow dressing room, the three-inch stainless-steel Riga Minox camera and its brass measuring chain lay in the amber half-light, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Each push-pull of the tiny steel camera body felt like cocking the hammer of a rifle. Against the quiet of the room, Viktor's jagged black signature at the foot of the Ostbahn schedule stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the tiny, surgical click of the shutter between ticks of her wristwatch while the room held the aroma of developed celluloid, lavender soap, and the cold porcelain of the basin that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Before the night was over, if a single frame blurred in the dim fifteen-watt light, the demolition teams in Poland would strike an empty decoy train, and she intended to be ready.

Spread flat upon the dark towel over the washbasin lay the seven onion-skin pages from Viktor’s calfskin case: *OKW / Chef des Transportwesens — Ostbahn Priority Schedule 14-B (Sector Brest – Siedlce – Warschau-Ost)*. Every train number, axle count, flak-car attachment, and marshaling-yard halt between the twentieth of May and the tenth of June was typed in violet ribbon, signed at the bottom in Viktor’s own jagged black fountain-pen script.

Working by touch and the small steel measuring chain clipped to the Minox’s eyelet, she held the miniature lens exactly twelve inches above the first page. *Click. Push-pull.* The tiny brass film-advance slid shut and cocked again, barely louder than the snap of an eyelash. Eighteen exposures: two for each of the seven pages, and four for the attached map overlay showing the Wehrmacht garrison fuel depots outside Praga and Rembertów.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond threading the ivory handle clockwise until the seam vanished completely beneath the silver ferrule and the meticulous discipline that left no trace, no fingerprint, and no lint behind, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of tortoiseshell resin, French face powder, and the faint trace of chloral hydrate hung in the draft alongside the distant rumble of an early market cart rattling toward Les Halles pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She checked the marble counter twice under the green lamp before switching off the bulb. Framed by the geometry of the velvet-lined cavity inside her tortoiseshell hairbrush, the exposed brass film cassette containing eighteen frames of high-command secrets seemed almost innocuous, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. With cold clarity she reckoned that once the brush was screwed shut, not even a cursory customs search would notice the extra two ounces of weight, though the thought of the hollow heel of her riding boot where she kept the potassium cyanide capsule returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when at eleven o'clock that morning she would carry the cassette into the Church of Saint-Germain-des-Prés.

When the last exposure was wound into the take-up chamber, she returned the onion-skin sheets to the folder in the exact alignment she had found them—aligning the bottom right corner one millimeter askew, just as Viktor had left it, and replacing the single gray eyebrow hair she had laid across the clasp as a telltale. She locked the calfskin case, wiped the brass hasp with the hem of her silk kimono, and set the key ring back beside his Walther PPK.

At twenty minutes to four, she slid back beneath the down duvet beside him. Viktor muttered something indistinct in his sleep and rolled toward her heat, his heavy arm hooking around her waist to drag her bare spine against his chest. Krystyna lay wide awake in the dark with his hand cupped over her breast, staring at the black slit between the crimson curtains where, a thousand miles to the east, the trains she had just condemned were already gathering steam.

Where the draft stirred the shadowed bedside where Viktor's boots stood at attention, the calfskin dispatch case restored to its exact spot within a millimeter of the wainscoting remained fixed in place, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She closed her eyes as his rough chin grazed her shoulder, counting the hours until the church bells of Saint-Germain. The sight of the dark imprint of his fingers beginning to purple along her hip brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She knew beyond doubt that by seven o'clock he would wake with a slight headache and blame the Comtesse's champagne, though every detail of her father saying that the Russian tsars and Prussian kings had both broken their teeth on Warsaw's stones remained as sharp as cut glass. With the soft, yielding warmth of a sleeping lover when his arm dragged her close governing the motion of slipping naked beneath the linen sheet without disturbing the fold of the blanket to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the deep, regular rhythm of Viktor's breathing from the four-poster bed and the faint drift of stale brandy, warm linen, and the cold pre-dawn damp against the glass an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place.

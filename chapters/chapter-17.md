# Chapter 17 — The Ostbahn Ambush

*Warsaw & Wawer — April 1944*

---

At twelve minutes past midnight on Tuesday of Easter week, a dull, subterranean boom rolled across the Vistula from the eastern pine woods of Wawer, rattling the windowpanes of Ulica Foksal six miles away. Three seconds later, a second and third explosion turned the low clouds over the Praga horizon the color of molten copper.

The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. Without haste, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

A subtle trace of warm bedsheets, ozone, and the sudden electricity of a decisive strike hung in the draft alongside the distant rumble of secondary ammunition detonations rolling across the Vistula and the shrill ring of the field phone steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. While Viktor cursed the partisans and pulled on his boots, Krystyna felt the red glow on the eastern clouds warm her very bones. She let her attention brush past Viktor's jaw muscles working like iron pistons as he strapped on his pistol belt, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond clutching the crimson sheet to her bare chest with a look of sleepy, feminine alarm and the worried concern of a mistress afraid for her colonel's safety in the night woods, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She did not allow herself to forget that Tadeusz's Kedyw demolition teams had hit Train 804 at the exact kilometer marker Phillip had whispered over the Bechstein piano, though the thought of the first Siedlce train derailment she had engineered from Paris eleven months ago returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when at dawn Kessler would walk the Wawer embankment looking for any trace of how the Home Army knew Train 804's schedule.

The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. Keeping her pulse steady, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

In the master bedroom of Foksal 14, the field telephone beside the bed began to shrill before the window glass had stopped vibrating. Viktor rolled out of the sheets naked, snatched up the black Bakelite receiver, and listened for twenty seconds while the copper glare from the eastern sky pulsed through the cracks in the shutters.

The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Steeling her resolve once more, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

"Train 804," Viktor spat, slamming the receiver down on its cradle, his scarred chest heaving as he reached for his breeches and jackboots. "Blown off the embankment two kilometers south of the Wawer siding with plastic mines! Twelve flatcars of Panther tank transmissions and four cars of artillery propellant burning in the pines!"

The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of Ulica Mazowiecka, young men and girls walked with the taut stillness of soldiers waiting for the fifth hour. Between the ticking clocks of the watchmaker's bench and the vaults of Section Ic, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. With her eyes clear and level, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

There was no escaping the conclusion that twelve flatcars of Panther tank parts and four cars of propellant destroyed in a single stroke would cripple an entire armored division on the Bug, though every detail of Tadeusz's missing finger and his quiet promise that Warsaw was not dead yet remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the shadow fell across the tall garden window after Viktor's armored car roared out of the courtyard, the faint orange halo still hanging low in the April night sky beyond Praga remained half-hidden, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She whispered a prayer that Tadeusz and his boys were already safe inside the city cellars. Nothing about the distant staccato of a machine gun firing blindly into the Wawer woods escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the rumble of German fire lorries and Wehrmacht half-tracks racing eastward across the Poniatowski Bridge and the faint drift of wet April earth from the thawing garden and coal smoke that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Whatever happened next, she knew that she had to meet Wanda at ten in the morning to learn whether all of Tadeusz's demolition boys had pulled back cleanly through Grochów.

A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of the central railway line, every troop train moving toward the front rode over rails watched by unseen eyes. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Measuring the room one last time, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Marion sat up against the pillows, pulling the crimson silk sheet to her collarbone with wide, sleep-startled eyes. "Must you go out there at midnight, Viktor? What if the partisans are still in the woods?"

A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of Ulica Wspólna, the underground presses ran day and night behind bricked-up cellar doors. In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of the Poniatowski Viaduct, an endless westward tide of mud-caked Wehrmacht wagons rumbled back from the East. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. In the hush that followed, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

"I am taking an armored car and two platoons of Feldgendarmerie," Viktor snarled, buckling his Walther PPK over his tunic. "And I am ordering Brandt and Kessler to meet me at the embankment at dawn. Keep the doors bolted until I return."

* * *

At nine o'clock the next morning, under a raw April sky that smelled of pine smoke and wet cinders, Krystyna drove out toward the eastern suburb of Wawer in Viktor’s second car—using the excuse of bringing the Colonel a thermos of hot cognac-laced coffee after his sleepless night at the derailment site.

She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. In the hush that followed, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

She answered the silence with handing the leather-cased coffee thermos to Viktor's orderly without letting her eyes linger for more than a second on the tweezers, letting bored, fastidious distaste for the soot and wreckage of the railway line serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the hiss of the overturned locomotive's boiler and the clank of cranes lifting twisted steel rails at the same moment she breathed in burned cordite, pine resin, scorched paint, and wet April sand that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Across the hood of the command car, Phillip's eyes met hers once—and both of them knew what had to be done before ten o'clock. Under the dim lamp near the soot-blackened sandy embankment above the wrecked Wawer munitions train, the charred fragment of blue French paper and the Hotel Europejski laundry tag held in Kessler's tweezers cast a narrow shade, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Her training insisted that one of Tadeusz's demolition boys had accidentally dropped the laundry wrapper Wanda used to pass the fuse primers out of the Hotel Europejski, while the contrast with Wanda's freckled face and auburn braid in the steam-filled linen corridor on the second floor of the Europejski made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where Kessler would raid the second-floor linen room of the Hotel Europejski within the hour—and unless Wanda was warned in the next thirty minutes, she was dead.

Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Along the quiet curbstones of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. As the minute hand crept on, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Where the Ostbahn double track ran between sandy pine dunes two kilometers south of the Wawer station, the scene looked like a circle of Dante’s Inferno. The heavy Reichsbahn locomotive lay on its side in the ditch like a gutted iron whale, still hissing white steam from its ruptured boiler; twelve twisted flatcars of blackened Panther tank hulls and shattered gearboxes were piled three high across the torn rails, while Polish forced-labor crews worked under the submachine guns of the Waffen-SS to clear the ties.

Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. While the night deepened outside, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

Up on the sandy embankment stood Viktor, his greatcoat smeared with soot, beside Hauptmann Phillip Brandt and Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler.

Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above the Ostbahn marshaling yards, scorched sleepers and twisted steel testified to the night strikes of the sabotage platoons. Under the cold, unblinking scrutiny of the counter-intelligence hounds, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Letting the silence work for her, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

By the threshold of the pine track beside the command vehicles at Wawer, Phillip's small black Adler staff sedan parked behind Viktor's Mercedes stood out in sharp relief, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. "Go on back to the Foksal, Marion," Viktor told her, waving a soot-stained glove. "Brandt will handle the hotel sweep with Kessler's men." She observed the mud spurting from the tires of Phillip's Adler sedan as he pulled onto the Warsaw road two hundred yards ahead of her car with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She forced herself to accept that Kessler's radio car was ordering the Aleja Szucha squad to converge on the Hotel Europejski, which gave them twenty-five minutes while the cars crossed town, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the back coal chute and laundry courtyard of the Hotel Europejski facing Ulica Ossolińskich. As she completed pulling her fur collar tight and telling Viktor that the smoke was giving her a headache so she would drive straight back to Nowy Świat, she drew the petulant impatience of a woman who regretted coming out to a dirty wreck around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to Kessler barking orders into his field radio to dispatch two Gestapo cars to the Hotel Europejski while the room held the aroma of diesel fumes, crushed pine needles, and the hot coffee Viktor poured into a tin cup pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence.

Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of Ulica Senatorska, the basement hospitals packed sterile gauze by candlelight for the battle to come. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. With unhurried grace, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

As Marion walked up the embankment path carrying the leather-cased thermos, Kessler was holding up a pair of nickel tweezers between his black-gloved fingers. Clipped in the jaws of the tweezers was a tiny charred corner of sky-blue French silk paper.

Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of the Vistula riverbanks, the warm July wind carried the low, continuous drumroll from the eastern front. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. Without a backward glance, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Beyond the shuttered windows of the Brühl Palace war rooms, staff officers worked through the humid nights drawing the red rings of Festung Warschau. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Drawing a slow breath, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

"Found beside the command-detonator battery in the pine thicket, Herr Oberst," Kessler said softly, his gin-pale eyes shifting from Viktor to Phillip and then to Marion as she approached. "Blue *Papier Job* cigarette paper from Paris—and wrapped around it was a laundry tag bearing the purple ink stamp of the Hotel Europejski’s second-floor linen room."

* * *

The race across the Poniatowski Bridge into the center of Warsaw took fourteen minutes. Having told Corporal Hanke to drop her at the corner of Krakowskie Przedmieście so she could visit the apothecary, Krystyna slipped through the side arcade of the Hotel Europejski at nine-forty-two—three minutes before two black Gestapo Citroëns from Aleja Szucha screeched to a halt at the front revolving doors.

Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Past the iron railings of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. Drawing a slow breath, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

"Hold onto my neck, Sarna!" Krystyna hissed in Polish, hooking her right arm beneath Wanda's good shoulder. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that Wanda had been hit through the left shoulder, and the SD gunman was twenty feet down the passage reloading his slide, though the thought of little Wanda holding the hem of Krystyna's wedding-white ball gown in 1938 returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of cordite smoke, hot laundry steam, bleach, and fresh blood hung in the draft alongside the deafening double crack of the Walther P38 in the enclosed stairwell and the shouts of Gestapo men below an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. The sight of Wanda's pale, freckled face biting back a scream as she clutched her bloody shoulder brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the cold, lethal reflex of a combat operative protecting her own with the quiet motion of drawing the suppressed 6.35mm automatic from her handbag and firing twice down the steam-filled corridor to drive the SD man back behind the mangle, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she had ninety seconds to get the bleeding girl down the outer coal hoist into the Ossolińskich alley where Phillip's car was waiting.

Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Above the slated mansard roofs of Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Up on the second-floor linen corridor, Krystyna heard the freight elevator gate slam open and the shouts of SS plainclothesmen in the main hall: *"Wäscherei besetzen! Keiner verlässt das Stockwerk!"*

Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Outside the tall casements of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Wanda was already running down the narrow back service passage toward the attic laundry stairs, her white apron torn off, a bundle of cipher slips clutched to her chest. As Krystyna reached the turn of the back landing to wave her toward the roof coal-hoist, an SD plainclothesman rounded the corner of the drying room and fired twice with a Walther P38.

As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. Along the darkened tramlines of Plac Bankowy, even the sentries behind the wire glanced eastward whenever the wind carried the rumble of guns. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. In that narrow pause between beats, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. Beneath the looming cornice of the Wawer pine woods, the smell of burned cordite lingered among the sand dunes beside the tracks. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. In the long shadow cast by Plac Zamkowy, the royal column looked down upon a city counting the hours to its rising. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Along the damp paving stones of Łazienki Park, the summer rain washed the classical marble while bunkers went up at every crossroad. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. With practiced serenity, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

The first bullet shattered the plaster an inch from Krystyna’s shoulder; the second struck Wanda high through the left collarbone, spinning the nineteen-year-old girl against the iron balustrade in a spray of bright arterial red.

* * *

While Wanda was scrambling across the first-floor ash balcony at the rear of the Hotel Europejski with a bullet in her shoulder, Sturmbannführer Kessler himself strode through the front revolving doors of the hotel into the marble lobby with four SS troopers at his heels, shouting for the manager to seal the staircases.

Behind the double-bolted doors of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. With practiced serenity, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

In the presence of Kessler's furious, impatient bow as he left her with the manager and raced up the stairs too late, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Past the edge of the foot of the grand marble staircase in the Hotel Europejski lobby, gold coins and French cosmetics rolling across the terrazzo between the jackboots of Kessler's squad waited in the gloom, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Without haste, letting her eyelashes flutter open as Kessler's corporal handed back her gold compact reinforced the quiet authority of the pale, shaken fragility of a delicate lady startled by armed men running into a hotel, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Thirty seconds after Kessler bounded up the steps, she walked calmly out the side arcade door into Phillip's waiting car. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that by fainting directly across the bottom steps in front of the hotel manager, she physically blocked the SS squad for forty seconds without raising suspicion, though every detail of the theatrical swoon she had practiced in drama club at her Lausanne school remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, those forty seconds allowed Wanda to reach the iron ladder in the Ossolińskich alley before the front squad reached the second floor, whatever price it demanded.

Near the sandbagged corners of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. In the amber half-light, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Down the main grand staircase walked Marion Herschlag—having descended from the second floor ten seconds after firing her suppressed Browning into the steam corridor—holding a lace handkerchief to her lips.

Down the wind-scoured length of the Praga horizon, the eastern sky flickered after dark with the pale summer lightning of approaching artillery. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. In the invisible web stretching from the drawing room to the underground cellars, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Right at the foot of the stairs, directly in the path of Kessler and his four troopers, Marion gave a low cry, rolled her eyes back, and collapsed in a graceful heap of silver-fox fur across the bottom three marble steps, scattering the contents of her handbag—lipstick, compact, keys, and French gold coins—between the jackboots of the SS.

Beyond the guarded courtyard of Żoliborz and Mokotów, hidden armories beneath the convent floors counted their rounds for the coming storm. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. Anchored by her silent oath, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Within the high-walled gardens of the Botanical Gardens, rain-drenched lilacs grew wild through the shattered glass of the orangery. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. As the shadows lengthened, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Near the iron-grated cellars of Aleje Ujazdowskie, the scent of wild Warsaw lilacs mingled with the diesel fumes of retreating columns. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. Without haste, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

Protocol and reflexes trapped them: Kessler and two troopers had to halt for forty crucial seconds to lift Oberst von Harenberg’s fainted mistress into an armchair and gather her rolling gold coins—the exact forty seconds Krystyna had calculated she needed before excusing herself through the side pharmacy arcade to join Phillip and Wanda in the back alley.

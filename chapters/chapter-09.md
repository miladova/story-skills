# Chapter 9 — The Conservatory at Dusk

*Warsaw — Mid-November 1943*

---

At a quarter to four on Thursday afternoon, a freezing fog rolled off the Vistula and settled among the bare chestnut alleys of the Saxon Garden. Along the wrought-iron railings on Plac Piłsudskiego, enameled white signs proclaimed *NUR FÜR DEUTSCHE — POLEN RAUSS*, while elderly German civilian clerks walked their dachshunds past the baroque marble statues wrapped in winter straw.

Combining the aloof, slightly bored demeanor of a German officer's companion with the quiet motion of strolling past the Marconi fountain with the unhurried pace of a woman taking the afternoon air, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of freezing river fog, wet straw, and the camphor of her sable muff hung in the draft alongside the crunch of frozen gravel under her boots and the distant bell of a Marszałkowska tram an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. Not a single footstep followed her down the yew-lined path toward the ruined glasshouse. Within the frost-rimed central alley of the Saxon Garden, her gaze returned briefly to the baroque marble statues wrapped in winter straw against the November freeze, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She knew beyond doubt that she was walking into a closed glass house behind the Brühl Palace where a single betrayal by Phillip would deliver her straight to the Gestapo, though the thought of feeding the swans by the Saxon Garden pond with her mother when she was nine years old returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that if Phillip had brought a counter-intelligence stenographer to hide behind the oleander tubs, her finger was already resting on the 6.35mm pistol inside her muff.

Along the darkened tramlines of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Krystyna entered through the Marszałkowska gate wearing a dark green loden cape over her wool suit, her hands tucked into a sable muff. She walked slowly past the dry basin of the Marconi fountain, checking the reflections in the frosted glass of the mineral-water pavilion to make sure no Loden-coated watcher had followed her from Nowy Świat.

At the northern edge of the park, half-hidden behind a screen of ragged yew hedges and backing onto the rear courtyard wall of the Brühl Palace, stood the nineteenth-century iron-and-glass palm conservatory. Half its roof panes had been blown out by artillery in September 1939 and boarded over with tarred planks; the German gardeners used it now only to store winter evergreen tubs and stacks of terra-cotta pots.

Beside the rusted iron doorway of the bombed-out palm conservatory, the outline of stacks of cracked terra-cotta pots and tarred roof planks marked the stillness, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. He did not move toward her until she drew her right hand empty from the sable muff. In the presence of the field-gray greatcoat hanging open over his captain's tunic, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She measured the risk by the certainty that seeing Phillip standing bareheaded among the dead ferns made the five-year gulf between their lives collapse in a single breath, though every detail of the greenhouse at Konstancin where they had sheltered from a summer hailstorm in July 1938 remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, letting her right hand release the safety catch of the tiny pistol inside her sable muff before she took a step down the aisle reinforced the quiet authority of a face of pale, guarded stillness as she shut the iron door behind her, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the drip of condensed fog from the iron roof ribs onto dead palm leaves and the faint drift of wet peat moss, winter mould, cold iron, and damp earth as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry.

A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Drawing a slow breath, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

She pushed open the rusted iron door. Inside, the air was dim, green-shadowed, and smelled of wet peat moss, rotting fern fronds, and cold iron.

At the far end of the central aisle, standing motionless between two rows of dormant oleander trees in wooden tubs, stood Hauptmann Phillip Brandt. He had taken off his peaked cap; his field-gray greatcoat was unbuttoned at the throat, and his dark eyes were fixed on the doorway.

"Take your hand off the gun in your muff, Krysiu," Phillip said softly in Polish. "I came alone." She did not allow herself to forget that there was no third person in the conservatory—he had come completely alone, putting his own neck into her hands, while the contrast with Phillip's voice at twenty-three reading Mickiewicz's Pan Tadeusz in Polish better than half the boys in Warsaw made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the low whistle of the November wind through a cracked pane overhead at the same moment she breathed in oleander leaves, damp wool, and the cedar scent she had remembered for sixty months sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. To anyone else, the dark hollows beneath Phillip's eyes showing he had not slept since Sunday night might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted stopping four paces away from him with the width of a potting table between them and the familiar armor of the taut, defensive poise of a wild creature ready to strike or flee to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when one word of truth spoken here would bind their lives together on the edge of the scaffold.

* * *

"How did you know there was a gun in my muff?" Krystyna asked in Polish, her voice low and shaking slightly as the sound of her own name on his tongue undid the lock on her chest.

It was in small things like the tremor in Phillip's jaw as he spoke of his father's death that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. From where she stood in the shadowed brick aisle of the Saxon Garden conservatory, the wooden potting table scarred with old trowel marks between them was just visible, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. In the choreography of the room, her gloved hand coming out of the sable muff to press against her own breastbone flowed seamlessly into the shattering of four years of bitterness as the truth broke over her, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. "Oh, Phillip," she breathed, the tears she had held back since Paris suddenly scalding her eyelids. There was no escaping the conclusion that Heinrich Brandt—the gentle German gentleman who had given her a porcelain figurine on her sixteenth birthday—had died in a Gestapo cellar saving Polish lives, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of sitting in Heinrich Brandt's study in Konstancin drinking hot lemon tea after skating. Before the night was over, Phillip had been fighting the same war in the dark, completely alone inside the belly of the Wehrmacht, and she intended to be ready.

A faint, heartbreaking ghost of the smile she remembered touched the corner of Phillip’s mouth. "Because when you were eighteen and your father’s stallion bolted at Konstancin, you tilted your right shoulder forward in that exact way before you jumped the fence. You have not changed how you brace yourself for a fall, Krysiu."

A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beyond the guarded courtyard of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. Against the slow, luminous fire that burned between her and Phillip, there was no mask left between them—only a devotion that had outlived the ashes. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Without a backward glance, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

He took two steps around the potting table and stopped an arm’s length from her. Up close in the gray conservatory light, she could see the silver thread or two at his temples—he was only twenty-eight, yet the war had aged him like iron left in winter rain—and the raw, unguarded hunger in his eyes as he looked down at her.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond reaching up with a trembling, ungloved hand to touch the white scar above his right eyebrow and no mask at all—only twenty-five-year-old Krystyna looking at the boy who held her heart, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. When her fingertips touched his skin, Phillip closed his eyes and pressed his mouth into the center of her palm. Against the muted backdrop of the narrow space between two tall tubs of dormant palms, the silver collar tabs of Phillip's tunic gleaming inches from her cheek drew her eye, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Through the lens of her cover identity, the warmth of Phillip's cheek leaning into her cold bare fingers was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Her training insisted that he had watched Viktor's hands on her bare back at the Brühl Palace while carrying that grief in his chest, though the thought of the night in August 1938 when Phillip had kissed the hollow of her throat under the Konstancin lindens returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of damp earth, cedarwood, and the salt of the single tear that spilled down her cheek hung in the draft alongside the distant rumble of a Wehrmacht lorry passing outside the park railings filtering through the heavy draperies without cease.

"Why are you in that uniform, Phillip?" she whispered fiercely. "When my father was shot in the Palmiry forest—when my mother died on rotten straw in Pawiak—where were you?"

In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. Where the boy who had held her youth now stood beside her as a man, they held each other as though each stolen hour had been won from death itself. With practiced serenity, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

"In Moabit military prison in Berlin for the first nine months of forty," Phillip said quietly, not flinching from her accusation. "My father was arrested by the Gestapo in November thirty-nine for using our Meissen shipping crates to smuggle twelve Polish professors from Cracow into Sweden. He died of heart failure during interrogation at Prinz-Albrecht-Straße. Because I spoke fluent Polish and Russian, Admiral Canaris’s Abwehr pulled me out of a penal battalion in forty-one and put me into Abteilung Ic."

"Every night for four years," Phillip whispered against her wrist, "I prayed that if you were alive, no man was hurting you." She let her attention brush past the rapid, hammering pulse under Phillip's jaw where her thumb rested, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the ragged intake of Phillip's breath against the skin of her wrist and the faint drift of warm skin, wet wool, and the unbearable sweetness of her childhood name whispered against her palm steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. Where the shadow fell across the boarded-over alcove at the rear of the glasshouse, the sable muff dropped forgotten onto the edge of the potting bench remained half-hidden, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. With the aching, luminous vulnerability of a woman coming home from the dead governing the motion of her fingers curling into the short dark-gold hair at the nape of his neck to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, letting her fingers tremble for the only man who knew her true name and held it sacred. She forced herself to accept that nothing Viktor von Harenberg had ever done to her body in the dark had touched the place inside her that Phillip woke with a single kiss on her palm, though every detail of the vow they had made at nineteen that they belonged to each other across any border men could draw remained as sharp as cut glass.

Krystyna’s breath caught in her throat. "Heinrich Brandt died at Prinz-Albrecht-Straße?"

"And every day since they sent me to the Brühl Palace," Phillip said, stepping close enough that his open greatcoat brushed her cape, "I have sat at that desk signing false clearance passes and misdirecting Gestapo raids by twenty minutes. Who do you think tipped off the Żoliborz printing press in October? And when you walked down that staircase on Sunday night on Viktor von Harenberg’s arm—with his emeralds around your throat—I thought I had lost my mind."

* * *

"He does not hurt me in any way that matters," Krystyna whispered, though her voice broke on the lie as Phillip’s hands came up to frame her face—not grabbing her hair or bruising her jaw as Viktor did, but cradling her cheekbones with a reverent, trembling gentleness that made her knees buckle. "Marion Herschlag is a ghost made of paper and French silk, Phillip. She lets him take a body that isn't mine so I can empty his safe at three in the morning."

Under the dim lamp near the shadowed corner behind the oleander tubs, the folded tissue-paper tracing of the Nowogrodzka cable conduit in her palm cast a narrow shade, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Her senses caught the crunch of two Feldgendarmerie sentries' boots passing twenty feet outside the frosted glass at the same moment she breathed in wet wool, winter mist, and the intoxicating heat of Phillip's mouth a fraction of an inch from hers that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. Pressed chest to chest behind the oleanders, she felt Phillip's heart beating directly against hers. Nothing about the blue shadow of the passing soldiers' helmets sliding across the frosted panes escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with pressing her palm flat over his mouth as they froze together in the shadow of the palms, letting the silent, synchronized stillness of two conspirators sharing a single breath serve as her shield, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. Time was narrowing toward the point where not only had Phillip saved her cover, he had just handed her half of the W-Netz intelligence Tadeusz needed.

"Do you think I don't know what Harenberg is?" Phillip’s forehead came down against hers, his breath hot and ragged against her parted lips, his thumbs brushing the dampness from beneath her lashes. "I sit across his desk every morning. I smell your perfume on his tunic cuff. I watch him look at the clock at nine in the evening when he wants to go back to the Foksal—and I have to sit at attention with my hands behind my back so I don't draw my Luger and shoot him through the throat."

In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. In the reverent, aching tenderness of Phillip's embrace, a single touch of his mouth undid the hardened spy and woke the woman beneath. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

"You must not," she breathed, her hands gripping his lapels as the heat between their bodies turned the freezing conservatory into a furnace. "If he dies now, they replace him with an SS general and we lose the Ostbahn schedules—and the *W-Netz* cable maps."

"Bring me the wax impression of Harenberg's key," Phillip whispered against her hair, "and I will open the Brühl vault myself." By the threshold of the iron-framed glass wall facing the Brühl Palace courtyard, her dark green loden cape tangled with the lapels of his field-gray greatcoat stood out in sharp relief, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She listened to the receding footsteps of the patrol fading toward the Saxon colonnade while the room held the aroma of peat moss, cedar soap, and the faint trace of her French perfume now on his collar that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that to get the inner cipher schedule from the Brühl Palace safe, she would have to take a wax impression of Viktor's master key on Ulica Foksal, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the duplicate key Tadeusz had forged for the Fontainebleau dispatch box. As she completed slipping the tissue-paper tracing deep into the hidden lining of her sable muff, she drew the urgent, breathless focus of an operative sealing an alliance around herself like silk, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that if they combined her access to Viktor's bedroom with Phillip's access to the Abteilung Ic vault, no secret in the Warsaw command was safe from them.

At the word *W-Netz*, Phillip’s dark eyes stilled inches from hers. "So it is Oddział II you are reporting to. Kruk’s network."

Beneath the quiet worship of Phillip's mouth and dark eyes, the contrast between the Colonel's storm and Phillip's reverence cut her to the bone. Along the riverward slope of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Keeping her pulse steady, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

"Yes," she whispered. "And they asked me yesterday whether Hauptmann Brandt of Abteilung Ic should be compromised—or liquidated."

A subtle trace of freezing fog and the lingering warmth where his hands had framed her cheeks hung in the draft alongside the four-thirty park bell ringing from the Marszałkowska gatekeeper's lodge pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She walked out into the freezing Warsaw fog with her blood singing like a violin wire. She observed the single green oleander leaf caught in the cuff of her loden cape with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the composed, indifferent stride she resumed the instant her boots touched the public path with the quiet motion of stepping backward through the iron door while her eyes held his until the last possible second, she gave the room only what it expected to see, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. She held fast to the recognition that walking out of this conservatory back into Viktor von Harenberg's house was going to tear her in two, yet for the first time in four years she was no longer alone in the dark, though the thought of Phillip watching her train pull out of the station in 1938 until the smoke hid him returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she had to leave the park by the south gate while Phillip walked back into the rear courtyard of the Brühl Palace.

Phillip gave a low, fierce laugh against her mouth and reached inside his tunic pocket, drawing out a folded tracing on tissue paper. "Tell Kruk that if his boys shoot me on the street, he won't get the second half of the Nowogrodzka repeater vault blueprint. Here is the outer conduit diagram. The inner cipher schedule is locked in Harenberg’s personal wall safe at the Brühl Palace—which requires both my Ic counter-key and Harenberg’s master key."

As she took the tissue paper, his mouth brushed hers—barely a breath of contact, soft and scorching as a live coal, tasting of winter rain and five years of starving longing—before the crunch of heavy jackboots sounded on the gravel path just outside the conservatory glass.

* * *

By five o'clock, when the streetlamps along Krakowskie Przedmieście came on behind their blue blackout hoods, Krystyna stopped at the confectioner’s shop opposite the Hotel Europejski to buy a box of marzipan and slipped into the ladies’ cloakroom off the hotel mezzanine. Two minutes later, Wanda Zieleńska entered with an armful of fresh hand towels.

No illusion could soften the fact that with a single message she had just saved Phillip's life from the Home Army's liquidation squads and bound him into the network, though every detail of Wanda's mother sewing the blue silk lining into Krystyna's confirmation dress in 1933 remained as sharp as cut glass. Past the edge of the tiled mezzanine cloakroom of the Hotel Europejski, the brass water tap running full into the porcelain basin to mask their voices waited in the gloom, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She dropped a fifty-groszy coin into the saucer on the counter and swept out into the mezzanine. The sight of the hollow cardboard cylinder inside the stack of clean hotel towels brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the splash of tap water and the distant clink of silverware from the officers' dining room and the faint drift of carbolic soap, wet wool coats, and the sweet almond scent of the marzipan box an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, if she could deliver the wax impression of Viktor's key by Saturday, Tadeusz could cut the duplicate in an hour at the Chmielna shop, whatever price it demanded.

Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Across the hushed thoroughfares of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Without haste, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

"Sarna," Krystyna whispered beneath the running water of the brass tap, pressing the tissue-paper conduit diagram from Phillip into the girl’s chapped hand. "Take this to Kruk tonight. Tell him the outer conduit of the Nowogrodzka repeater is here—and tell him to call off the Kedyw dogs from Hauptmann Brandt. Brandt is ours."

Wanda’s freckled face lit with astonishment as she tucked the tissue paper into the hollow cardboard core of a towel roll. "The German captain from the Brühl Palace? Tadeusz thought he was an SD plant!"

In the long shadow cast by Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. As the minute hand crept on, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

She trusted turning into the iron gates of Foksal 14 and nodding coolly to the sentry at the guardhouse and the familiar armor of the composed, domestic return of a woman bringing sweets home for coffee to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the clang of a streetcar and the rhythmic tramp of a four-man Wehrmacht patrol at the same moment she breathed in first snow flurries mixing with the coal smoke of the evening stoves as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. Viktor was already home; the second-floor windows of the white drawing room glowed yellow behind the cracks in the shutters. Set against the wet pavement of Nowy Świat under the blue blackout lamps, the white cardboard confectioner's box tied with gold string in her hand held a quiet, insistent weight, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. With cold clarity she reckoned that every step back toward Ulica Foksal required banking the luminous fire Phillip had lit inside her so Viktor would not see its glow, while the contrast with walking home from the Saxon Garden in winter when her father waited by the library fire made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when tonight she would need soft beeswax in her soap dish and a steady hand after midnight.

"He is Heinrich Brandt’s son from Konstancin," Krystyna whispered, her fingers tightening briefly on Wanda’s wrist. "He is risking his neck for us every hour he sits at that desk. And tell Tadeusz I will have the wax impression of Harenberg’s master safe key by Saturday noon."

She dried her hands on a linen towel, checked her lipstick in the cloakroom mirror—where her lips still looked fuller, flushed from the single fleeting touch of Phillip’s mouth in the conservatory—and walked back out through the marble lobby.

* * *

Late that Thursday night, while Viktor remained at the Brühl Palace until two in the morning overseeing an emergency troop movement toward Kowel, Krystyna sat at her mother’s marquetry writing desk in the locked dressing room of Foksal 14. Before her lay a sheet of thick cream stationery addressed in German to a milliner in Zurich—a routine vanity letter asking for velvet hat ribbons that Viktor’s own military censor clerk stamped every week without reading twice.

Not three paces from her mother's marquetry writing desk in the locked dressing room, the bone toothpick and the vial of colorless pyramidon sympathetic ink lay in the amber half-light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She sealed the envelope, rinsed the bone toothpick in alcohol, and went to bed with her heart lighter than it had been in four years. To anyone else, the formal German salutation 'Sehr geehrte Madame Blanc' written in black ink across the top might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She knew beyond doubt that every word written invisibly between the lines of this milliner's order would reach London via Sweden inside ten days, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of writing letters to Phillip in Berlin in the winter of 1938 at this very desk. In the choreography of the room, tilting the cream stationery sheet at a forty-five-degree angle to the green lamp to verify no watermark showed flowed seamlessly into the meticulous craft of an intelligence officer encoding a dispatch, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the distant whistle of a military train crossing the iron Kierbedź Bridge over the Vistula while the room held the aroma of distilled water, French stationery paper, and the dried oleander leaf hidden in her jewel box sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply.

Beneath the looming cornice of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. In the hush that followed, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Between the ink lines of the German letter, using a clean bone toothpick dipped in a colorless solution of pyramidon dissolved in distilled water, Krystyna wrote in microscopic Polish script her full report to London on Hauptmann Phillip Brandt and the *W-Netz* cable conduit.

As the colorless liquid dried on the cream paper without leaving so much as a gloss under the angled lamp, she paused with the bone toothpick between her fingers, feeling again the warm, reverent pressure of Phillip’s palms framing her face among the dead ferns of the Saxon Garden conservatory.

He was alive. He was hers. And together inside the Brühl Palace, they were going to tear the German command apart from within.

# Chapter 25 — Through the Sewers of the Old Town

*Warsaw — August to September 1944*

---

For the first three days of August, Warsaw tasted a wild, intoxicating freedom that seemed worth five years of tombs. Out of cellars and attics came the white-and-red flags of the Polish Republic; overturned tramcars and tearing cobblestones became barricades across Nowy Świat, Marszałkowska, and the Old Town; and in the courtyard of Foksal 14, Wanda Zieleńska—her left shoulder healed and a Red Cross brassard on her sleeve—pinned two white-and-red *Armia Krajowa* armbands stamped with the black anchor of *Polska Walcząca* onto Krystyna’s and Phillip’s sleeves.

Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Along the quiet curbstones of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. Anchored by her silent oath, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

When Phillip shot Sturmbannführer Kessler dead by the Saxon colonnade, the ghosts of Stefan Wilk and young Marek were finally laid to rest. She did not allow herself to forget that for the first time since September 1939 she wore her own name, spoke her own language aloud in the sun, and fought beside the man she loved without a lie between them, though the thought of the fourteen months in Paris and on Ulica Foksal when every breath had been a mask returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of cordite, pulverized brick dust, hot radio tubes, and sweat hung in the draft alongside the crackle of the German tactical wavelength in their headphones and the crash of mortar shells above filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Through the lens of her cover identity, the red-and-white Polish flag flying from the top of the sixteen-story Prudential skyscraper above them was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining no mask at all—only Lieutenant 'Jaskółka' of the Home Army beside the man who had chosen her people as his own with the quiet motion of handing Phillip the next decoded German frequency crystal while their shoulders pressed together at the map table, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that every German supply canister Phillip diverted from the sky fed two hundred young fighters on the barricades.

At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

Phillip had burned his Wehrmacht tunic in the courtyard bonfire on the first night; wearing a captured German camo smock belted with Polish eagle webbing and carrying a Mauser rifle alongside Tadeusz’s radio team, he became Battalion *Gustaw*’s most lethal weapon.

The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Down the wind-scoured length of Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Deep behind the lacquered mask of Marion Herschlag, she knew that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Without a backward glance, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

Using the *Festung Warschau* map Krystyna had stolen from Viktor’s cylinder and his own knowledge of the German *Abteilung Ic* tactical wavelengths, Phillip sat beside Krystyna at the captured Telefunken transceiver in the cellar of the Main Post Office on Plac Napoleona, speaking into the microphone in his faultless Prussian Generalstab cadence—redirecting three Luftwaffe ammunition drops straight onto Home Army drop-zones and sending SS-Gruppenführer Reinefarth’s relief columns into mined cul-de-sacs in Wola.

Along the darkened tramlines of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. In the hush that followed, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

She let her attention brush past Phillip's soot-blackened face as he held the barricade corner with Tadeusz until the wounded were below ground, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Beside the burning barricade across Ulica Świętojańska in the Old Town in late August, the outline of a crate of captured German Panzerfaust anti-tank rockets stacked behind the paving stones marked the stillness, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Without haste, helping Wanda drag a wounded sixteen-year-old Boy Scout courier into the vaulted cellar of the Dominican church reinforced the quiet authority of the soot-streaked, unflinching grit of a frontline nurse and soldier, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. On the night of September first, Tadeusz gave the order to evacuate the burning Old Town through the manhole on Plac Krasińskich. There was no escaping the conclusion that by the third week of August the Germans were systematically pulverizing the medieval Old Town block by block with dive-bombers and Goliath mines, though every detail of the quiet garret on Ulica Freta where she and Phillip had broken the Christmas wafer eight months ago, now a heap of burning rafters remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, the Old Town was surrounded on all four sides, and the only road left to the city center lay fifteen feet underground through the main storm sewers, whatever price it demanded.

In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Across the hushed thoroughfares of Nowy Świat, five years of silent endurance had burst into open flame at the stroke of five. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Keeping her pulse steady, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. In the amber half-light, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, the truth of her allegiance remained locked deeper than any steel vault. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she committed every face and shadow in the chamber to memory.

Along the damp paving stones of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Letting the silence work for her, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. Within the mirror-lined rooms of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. Measuring the room one last time, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that a single cipher group copied at three in the morning could save a battalion. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Under the hooded blue lamps of Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. As the shadows lengthened, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

And on the eighth day of the battle, during a counter-attack near the ruins of the Saxon Garden colonnade, Phillip spotted a black-uniformed SD officer directing a Goliath tracked-mine crew from behind a burned half-track: Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler. Sighting down the barrel of his scoped Mauser at two hundred yards through the smoke, Phillip squeezed the trigger once—and ended the hangman of Aleja Szucha forever.

* * *

At midnight on the first of September—five years to the day since the war had begun—the iron manhole cover on Plac Krasińskich was pried open behind a screen of burning trucks, and one by one the survivors of Battalion *Gustaw* descended the iron rungs into the pitch-black abyss of the Warsaw sewers—the *kanały*.

Above the slated mansard roofs of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. When Phillip whispered her true Polish name against her skin, a single touch of his mouth undid the hardened spy and woke the woman beneath. Measuring the room one last time, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

She trusted gripping the back of Phillip's soaked belt with her left hand while her right guided the blinded boy behind her and the familiar armor of absolute, unbreakable courage in the belly of the earth to see her through, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. In that subterranean hell, the touch of Phillip's wet fingers against her face was the thread that led them back to the upper world. In the corner of the pitch-black, waist-deep concrete sewer tunnel beneath Krakowskie Przedmieście, she noticed the wet hemp guide-rope linking Krystyna, Phillip, Wanda, and the twelve blinded wounded, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Nothing about Phillip's steady hand reaching back every thirty paces to touch her cheek and confirm she was still there escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Her training insisted that twenty feet directly above this foul black tunnel lay the marble floors of the Brühl Palace and the Hotel Europejski where she had walked in satin and furs, while the contrast with the soundproofed map vault of the Brühl Palace above their heads where they had shared their first kiss in the dark made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the hollow, echoing boom of German concussion grenades dropped down the grates ahead and the slosh of black water at the same moment she breathed in sewer methane, burning gasoline on water, wet concrete, and cold sweat that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise.

Where five years of longing deepened into an all-consuming love, the contrast between the Colonel's storm and Phillip's reverence cut her to the bone. Within the lamplit precincts of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. As the shadows lengthened, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Down in the concrete tunnel, four feet high and three feet wide, the foul black water ran waist-deep and ice-cold. Above their heads, the Germans had learned the insurgents were using the sewers: at every third intersection along the route beneath Krakowskie Przedmieście, SS pioneers lifted the grates and dropped concussion grenades or poured burning gasoline onto the water.

Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. In the secret light that shone whenever their eyes met across the dark, their slow, luminous love refused to die beneath the boots of the Reich. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

For six hours Krystyna moved hunched double through the subterranean blackness, holding a guide-rope with one hand to lead twelve blinded, burned hospital patients while Wanda steadied the rear and Phillip walked directly ahead of Krystyna, testing the footing for carbide gas traps and unexploded grenades with his rifle butt.

The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beyond the guarded courtyard of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. Yet in the stolen, breathless stillness with Phillip, the boy from the Konstancin lindens had become the man she would die beside. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Drawing a slow breath, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

Wrapped in the single blanket beside the warm bricks of the cellar bakery, Phillip kissed her eyes closed and held her until she slept. Against the quiet of the room, the small white scar on Krystyna's wrist pressed against the red handcuff scar on Phillip's stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the patter of September rain through the broken ceiling and the quiet breathing of the saved wounded while the room held the aroma of carbolic soap, wet brick dust, and the miraculous sweetness of clean surface air that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. From where she stood in the bombed cellar at Ulica Warecka after emerging from the sewer manhole at dawn, two buckets of clean well-water brought by the Śródmieście girls to wash the sewer filth from their skin was just visible, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the choreography of the room, stripping off their filthy, soaked rags in the dark corner behind the boiler and pouring clean water over each other's shivering shoulders flowed seamlessly into weeping, laughing tenderness as they wrapped a single dry army blanket around their bare bodies, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. She forced herself to accept that they had walked five miles through the bowels of the earth beneath the enemy's boots and brought every single wounded fighter out alive, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of washing Wanda's blood from Phillip's hands in the Żoliborz convent four months ago.

Beneath the looming cornice of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Against the slow, luminous fire that burned between her and Phillip, his hands asked permission where the world took by force, and that undid her. As the minute hand crept on, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

Where the boy who had held her youth now stood beside her as a man, their love was the one truth in Warsaw that needed no cipher. Along the riverward slope of Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Steeling her resolve once more, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Across the curfew-bound sweep of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. In those clandestine meetings where no uniform stood between them, five winters of exile burned away in a passion more perilous than any bullet. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she let the calm of her voice govern the temperature of the room.

The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of Nowy Świat, five years of silent endurance had burst into open flame at the stroke of five. Within the sacred, unguarded sanctuary of Phillip's arms, a single touch of his mouth undid the hardened spy and woke the woman beneath. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. In that narrow pause between beats, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

From the sentry-guarded gates of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. Against the gentle, trembling honesty of Phillip's touch, the contrast between the Colonel's storm and Phillip's reverence cut her to the bone. With unhurried grace, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Held close against the steady beat of Phillip's heart, their slow, luminous love refused to die beneath the boots of the Reich. Past the iron railings of Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. At the center of her resolve stood the unshakeable fact that the men who ruled by terror were themselves haunted by unseen shadows. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

Halfway beneath the ruins of the Brühl Palace—the very building where they had stood under crystal chandeliers ten months earlier—a German demolition charge detonated in the upper shaft, collapsing a ton of brick into the tunnel behind them and extinguishing the single shaded flashlight. In the absolute, suffocating blackness, chest-deep in freezing filth while toxic gas swirled above the water, panic seized the wounded column. Phillip reached back through the dark, caught Krystyna’s freezing face in his hands, pressed his soaked forehead against hers, and spoke in calm, steady Polish until every shaking boy and woman along the rope quieted to the sound of his voice—and at dawn, when they finally climbed up the iron ladder at Ulica Warecka into the free air of Śródmieście, they collapsed into each other’s arms in a ruined cellar and held each other like the last two human beings on earth.

* * *

On the tenth of September, in the vaulted brick cellar beneath the bombed ruins of Saint Alexander’s Church on Plac Trzech Krzyży—while German Nebelwerfer rockets howled like iron organs across the rooftops above—Father Antoni, the seventy-year-old chaplain of Battalion *Gustaw*, stood before an altar made of two stacked ammunition crates covered with a clean parachute silk.

The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Near the sandbagged corners of Nowy Świat, five years of silent endurance had burst into open flame at the stroke of five. Within the sacred, unguarded sanctuary of Phillip's arms, they held each other as though each stolen hour had been won from death itself. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. With unhurried grace, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Against the muted backdrop of the vaulted brick cellar beneath the ruins of Saint Alexander's Church, two plain wedding bands hammered from a brass 9mm cartridge casing drew her eye, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. A subtle trace of parachute silk, tallow candle smoke, pulverized brick dust, and gunpowder hung in the draft alongside the howl of Nebelwerfer rockets overhead and the soft Latin blessing of Father Antoni pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. That night behind the stacked sandbags of the cellar, Phillip held his wife against his heart while the city burned above them. She observed Wanda wiping her eyes with the corner of her Red Cross apron beside Tadeusz with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond sliding the warm brass band onto Phillip's fourth finger below the red scar of the German handcuffs and luminous, unshakeable devotion shining through the soot on her face, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. Not a single second could be wasted when when the capitulation came in October, they would walk out of the ruins as man and wife under the protection of the Geneva accords.

Outside the tall casements of Ulica Świętojańska, the Gothic brick walls stood glowing like embers between the barricades. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. With a veteran operative's detachment, she reckoned that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. Against the gentle, trembling honesty of Phillip's touch, every heartbeat shared in the dark bound their fates closer to the same precipice. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Before him stood Krystyna and Phillip in their soot-stained insurgent smocks, with Tadeusz Kowalski and Wanda Zieleńska standing beside them as witnesses. There were no gold rings left in the cellar; Tadeusz had hammered two plain bands out of a brass 9mm cartridge casing on a cobbler’s anvil that morning.

Held close against the steady beat of Phillip's heart, she knew what it meant to be cherished as sacred rather than claimed as spoils. Under the starless canopy above Ulica Długa, the field hospitals worked beneath the vaults while the rubble shook overhead. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Without haste, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

"*Ja, Filip, biorę ciebie, Krystyno, za żonę...*" Phillip repeated in clear, steady Polish as the dust sifted down from the vaulted brick ceiling with each shell burst overhead, sliding the warm brass ring onto her finger.

Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of the Warsaw sewers, columns of the wounded moved by touch beneath the very boots of the German garrison. Whenever Phillip's hand found hers in the shadows, tenderness proved a fiercer fire than brutality could ever ignite. With practiced serenity, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of Powiśle, every paving stone pried from the street had become a rampart of free earth. In the reverent, aching tenderness of Phillip's embrace, every unspoken year between thirty-eight and forty-three spoke in a single kiss. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

In the long shadow cast by Ulica Warecka, survivors emerging from the manholes breathed the smoke of a city that refused to kneel. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. Beneath the quiet worship of Phillip's mouth and dark eyes, every wall she had built to survive the war dissolved into pure truth. While the night deepened outside, she moved seamlessly into the next beat of the evening's choreography.

When Phillip whispered her true Polish name against her skin, there was no mask left between them—only a devotion that had outlived the ashes. Beyond the shuttered windows of the Old Town, sixteen-year-old couriers and veteran officers held every doorway like a fortress. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. With her eyes clear and level, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Across the darkened perspective of Plac Krasińskich, the roar of dive-bombers could not drown the singing from the cellar chapels. Where five years of longing deepened into an all-consuming love, they held each other as though each stolen hour had been won from death itself. Anchored by her silent oath, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of Plac Napoleona, captured German radios turned the enemy's own orders against his columns. In the secret light that shone whenever their eyes met across the dark, every heartbeat shared in the dark bound their fates closer to the same precipice. As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Behind the double-bolted doors of Śródmieście, the white-and-red flags of the Republic snapped undefeated in the August smoke. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. She never allowed herself to forget for a single breath that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Yet in the stolen, breathless stillness with Phillip, she knew what it meant to be cherished as sacred rather than claimed as spoils. Without a backward glance, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

"*I ślubuję ci miłość, wierność i uczciwość małżeńską, aż do śmierci,*" Krystyna answered—*And I vow to you love, fidelity, and marital honesty until death*—lifting her mouth to her husband’s in the candlelight of the cellar while the fighters along the walls beat their rifle butts softly against the bricks in salute.

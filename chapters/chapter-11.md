# Chapter 11 — The Cipher in the Crypt

*Warsaw — Early December 1943*

---

On the sixth of December, a Berlin string quartet played Schubert’s *Death and the Maiden* in the second-floor Pompeian Salon of the Brühl Palace. Outside on Plac Piłsudskiego, the thermometer stood at twelve degrees below zero; inside, a hundred senior officers of the Wehrmacht and the civil administration sat in rows of gilt chairs while the cello wept beneath the swastika standards.

Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Above the slated mansard roofs of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead sharpened her senses until even the shadows seemed legible. Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

Because Viktor was wedged three seats in from the aisle beside General Stahel, polite protocol trapped him in his chair. She knew beyond doubt that the second and third movements of the Schubert quartet would last exactly twenty-one minutes before the applause, while the contrast with listening to the same Schubert quartet on her father's gramophone while rain beat on the Foksal windows made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the mournful,minor-key variations of Schubert's Death and the Maiden at the same moment she breathed in overheated radiators, beeswax, wool uniforms, and French perfume filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Through the lens of her cover identity, the row of generals blocking Viktor's seat from the center aisle was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with pressing her lace handkerchief lightly to her temple as she whispered in Viktor's ear, letting the delicate, overheated languor of a lady needing fresh air serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where she had twenty-one minutes to descend to the ground-floor west wing, open Viktor's wall safe, photograph the W-Netz schedule, and return before the final chord.

Down the wind-scoured length of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next reminded her that every minute bought in secret had a price. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Drawing a slow breath, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

Marion sat in the third row beside Viktor, wearing midnight-blue velvet with long tight sleeves. Inside the wrist of her left sleeve rested the three-inch nickel-brass key Tadeusz had cast from her beeswax mold on Saturday.

Halfway through the second movement—the slow, variation-threaded march toward the grave—she touched her lace handkerchief to her temple and whispered against Viktor’s ear: "The stoves in here are stifling, darling. I am going down to the cloakroom for a glass of water and my smelling salts. Do not get up and disturb the General."

The crisp rustle of paper turning in the adjoining room reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Through the narrow archways of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, she carried the weight of the network behind a smile that never wavered. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. In the amber half-light, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

She let her attention brush past the brass nameplate 'OBERST V. FRHR. VON HARENBERG — CHEF TRANSPORTWESEN' on the oak door, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. By the threshold of the shadowed ground-floor west corridor of the Brühl Palace, the nickel-brass duplicate key warming against the pulse of her left wrist stood out in sharp relief, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. As she completed carrying her midnight-blue velvet pumps by their heels in her left hand while her right drew the key, she drew the silent, cat-footed speed of an infiltrator inside enemy headquarters around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. The duplicate key slid into the outer door lock and turned as smoothly as oiled silk. She measured the risk by the certainty that in her stockinged feet she made not a single click against the terrazzo floor between the rotunda and Viktor's door, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of creeping down the dark gallery at Konstancin on Christmas Eve to see the tree before midnight Mass. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that if the duty clerk at the rotunda glanced down the west wing before she turned the lock, she would drop an earring and claim she had lost it on the stairs.

Experience inside the high command had proved to her that fear was a luxury she had burned out of her blood in thirty-nine. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Along the riverward slope of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. Within the double-locked rooms where the empire kept its darkest ledgers, she measured her breath by the tick of the watch and the turn of the key. With her eyes clear and level, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

Viktor pressed her hand once and kept his eyes on the quartet. Marion slipped silently from the end of the row, walked past the two SS orderlies at the salon doors with a faint, dizzy smile toward her temple, and descended the side marble stair toward the ground-floor administrative wing.

At nine in the evening during a command concert, the ground-floor west corridor—where Viktor’s transport directorate adjoined the vaults of Abteilung Ic—was deserted save for the night duty clerk at the far rotunda. Krystyna stepped out of her high-heeled velvet pumps, carried them in her left hand, and glided in her silk-stockinged feet down the shadowed runner to Viktor’s oak door.

* * *

Inside Viktor’s office, the tall bulletproof shutters were bolted against the December frost. By the blue beam of her miniature pencil torch, Krystyna crossed to the steel-plated wall safe behind the map curtain, inserted Tadeusz’s nickel-brass key into the primary keyhole, and found—just as Phillip had warned her—that the inner armored compartment containing the *W-Netz* repeater cipher required the secondary Abteilung Ic counter-key.

Combining total, breathless silence in the dark with the quiet motion of snatching the nickel-brass key out of the lock in a fraction of a second before the corridor boots reached the door, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She backed toward the connecting archive door with her hand on her cyanide clasp. In the hush that filled the pitch-black interior of Viktor's ground-floor office, the steel-plated wall safe hidden behind the heavy green baize map curtain appeared almost motionless, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Nothing about the beam of an SS flashlight slicing beneath the crack of the outer oak door escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She did not allow herself to forget that the Luftwaffe shelter drill had cut the building power and sent SS clearing patrols down every ground-floor corridor, though the thought of the real air-raid sirens of September 1939 when the ceiling plaster rained down on Ulica Foksal returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of cold steel, ozone, and the sudden icy spike of adrenaline in her veins hung in the draft alongside the deafening, wailing shriek of the rooftop air-raid Klaxon cutting through the palace that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise.

Near the sandbagged corners of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside kept her anchored to the cold arithmetic of her mission. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. Without a backward glance, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

Before she could reach for the inner handle, the air-raid Klaxon on the roof of the Brühl Palace suddenly erupted in a deafening, rising-and-falling shriek: *Fliegeralarm*—an unscheduled night blackout and shelter drill ordered by the Luftwaffe command.

The quiet scrape of a bough against the upper windowpane settled over the furniture with the weight of an unspoken warning. Beside the stone balustrades of Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. With practiced serenity, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

Instantly the electricity in the building cut off. Down at the rotunda, iron fire-doors clanged shut, and the heavy jackboots of the SS palace guard came pounding down the west corridor, checking office doors and shouting, *"Alles in den Luftschutzkeller! Sofort!"*

She held fast to the hard-won certainty that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. A far-off clang of a streetcar bell at the intersection left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Across the hushed thoroughfares of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, no margin existed for sentiment when the gestapo watched every threshold. Steeling her resolve once more, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

His left palm covered her mouth gently for three seconds until the SS boots faded down the cellar stairs. Against the quiet of the room, the faint luminous dial of Phillip's military wristwatch glowing in the dark stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the muffled thud of SS jackboots passing on the other side of six inches of steel and the faint drift of old map linen, India ink, cedar soap, and the sudden enveloping heat of Phillip's body that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Past the edge of the soundproofed steel map vault of Abteilung Ic, the heavy iron locking wheel of the inner vault door closing silently behind them waited in the gloom, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Without haste, her stockinged feet coming to rest between his boots as he pulled the vault door shut reinforced the quiet authority of the sudden, dizzying transition from mortal terror to the warmth of his chest, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. There was no escaping the conclusion that it was Phillip who had been waiting on the other side of the connecting vault door with the counter-key in his hand, though every detail of hiding with Phillip inside the hollow oak tree at Konstancin when they were fourteen remained as sharp as cut glass.

If the SS guards found Oberst von Harenberg’s mistress shoeless inside his locked office with a duplicate key in the safe during an air-raid drill, no lie on earth could save her.

She whipped the key from the safe, pulled the map curtain shut, and backed toward the connecting fire-door that led into the inner archive vault of Abteilung Ic. Before her fingers touched the handle, the heavy steel vault door swung open an inch from the inside, a hard hand closed around her wrist in the pitch dark, and drew her backward into the soundproofed map vault just as the SS patrol’s flashlights swept Viktor’s outer doorway.

* * *

In the absolute darkness of the Abteilung Ic map vault, six inches of armored steel shut out the wail of the air-raid siren and the boots of the SS guard. There was no light except the tiny green-phosphor hands of Phillip’s military watch, showing sixteen minutes past nine. The narrow space between the floor-to-ceiling steel map drawers was barely three feet wide; Krystyna stood pressed flat against Phillip’s chest, her velvet pumps still clutched in one hand, her heart hammering like a trapped bird against his ribs.

Set against the narrow, pitch-black aisle between the steel map drawers of the vault, the tiny brass Minox cassette and the duplicate key pressed together into her palm held a quiet, insistent weight, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Her senses caught the muffled, distant hum of the palace generators and the ragged catch of Phillip's breath at the same moment she breathed in old map linen, cedar soap, midnight-blue velvet, and the clean, electric heat of his mouth pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. He kissed her eyelids, her cheekbones, and her parted lips as though trying to drink five lost years in ten minutes. She observed the green phosphor glow of his watch dial catching a single tear on her cheekbone with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted dropping her shoes to the floor and twining her arms fiercely around his neck in the dark and the familiar armor of utter, weeping honesty with not a shred of Marion Herschlag left between them to see her through, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. She could not afford a single misstep when outside this six-inch steel door stood a hundred German officers and her possessive lover, yet inside the dark belonged only to them.

The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space made the contrast between outward calm and inward urgency all the starker. Across the cobble-paved expanse of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. In those clandestine meetings where no uniform stood between them, every heartbeat shared in the dark bound their fates closer to the same precipice. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. Collecting her thoughts into a blade, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

"Are you hurt?" Phillip whispered against her temple. In the pitch dark, his breath was warm against her skin; his right hand still rested at the small of her waist where he had pulled her through the door.

"No," she breathed, though her whole body was trembling now—not from the SS patrol outside, but from the overwhelming shock of being sealed in the dark against the length of his body. "You knew I would come down during the quartet."

"God help me, Krysiu," he breathed against her throat. "If I cannot have you in the light, I will die for you in the dark." Not three paces from the cold steel face of the map cabinets behind her back, her midnight-blue velvet bodice crushed against the field-gray wool of his tunic lay in the amber half-light, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She listened to the twin thunder of their heartbeats echoing in the soundproofed chamber while the room held the aroma of warm skin, salt tears, and the faint lavender of her hair an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. She forced herself to accept that this was why she had survived the cellars, the dead-drops, and the Rue de Rivoli bedroom—to feel her own heart beat alive again in his hands, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the summer orchard at Konstancin when the whole world had smelled of white linden blossoms and sun. In the choreography of the room, arching her throat into the warm cradle of his palms as his mouth moved down to the pulse at her collar flowed seamlessly into a low, trembling sigh of pure surrender that no force on earth could have wrung from her, where for once there was no war, no cover name, and no lie between their mouths. Before the night was over, when the all-clear siren sounded in four minutes, they would have to step out of this vault as strangers, and she intended to be ready.

Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. A low throb of generators working deep beneath the building defined the boundary between what was safe and what was lethal. Under the starless canopy above the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. When Phillip whispered her true Polish name against her skin, their slow, luminous love refused to die beneath the boots of the Reich. Keeping her pulse steady, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

"I saw you touch your temple in the third row," he whispered, his fingers rising in the dark to trace the line of her jaw, her cheek, the corner of her trembling mouth. "I came down two minutes ahead of you to unlock the inner vault from my side—and then Stahel’s idiocy set off the drill siren. We have ten minutes before the all-clear sounds, Krysiu."

"The safe—" she began.

A subtle trace of warm breath and the cold iron of the vault handle hung in the draft alongside the distant hum of the palace chandeliers coming back to life on the floor above as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. With one last fierce pressure of his fingers around her wrist, he let her slip back through Viktor's door. In the presence of the click of the connecting latch as Phillip checked the empty corridor for her, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond smoothing her gold chignon by touch in the dark while Phillip wiped a trace of her lipstick from his mouth and the heart-wrenching reconstruction of her icy mask in thirty seconds, breathing him in as though he were the only clean air left in a ruined country. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that the all-clear siren meant the concert upstairs would resume in three minutes and the SS guards would leave the corridor, though the thought of Cinderella's midnight bell turned into a Luftwaffe siren in occupied Warsaw returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when she now carried the complete W-Netz repeater cipher schedule inside her sleeve.

Within the sacred, unguarded sanctuary of Phillip's arms, she knew what it meant to be cherished as sacred rather than claimed as spoils. Beneath the looming cornice of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. The cold draft that crept persistently along the wainscoting gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that patience is deadlier than any pistol concealed in a velvet bag. While the night deepened outside, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

"I already photographed the inner W-Netz cipher schedule an hour ago," Phillip whispered, pressing a tiny brass Minox cassette into her palm alongside her duplicate key. "You didn’t need to risk your life tonight. I had it for you."

The realization that he had already taken the mortal risk upon himself—that he had stood watching her in the concert hall only to shield her when she came down—broke the last dam inside her. In the pitch dark of the steel vault, Krystyna dropped the velvet shoes to the floor, rose on her stockinged toes, and tangled both hands in the hair at the back of his neck.

When their mouths met in the dark, five years of silence, exile, and unbearable restraint went up like dry straw in a furnace. Phillip’s arms locked around her waist, lifting her against the steel map drawers as his mouth opened over hers—not with Viktor’s brutal, conquering force, but with a starving, reverent, all-consuming passion that tasted of salt tears and home. She whimpered into his mouth, clinging to his shoulders, feeling his hands cup her face and her throat as if he were worshipping a miracle raised from the dead.

* * *

When Marion glided back into the Pompeian Salon on the second floor two minutes after the lights returned, the Berlin quartet was just tuning its strings after the air-raid interruption. She slipped into her gilt chair beside Viktor, holding her small crystal vial of smelling salts in her gloved hand.

She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray accompanied the silent duel of wills taking place under the lamps. Within the lamplit precincts of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Without haste, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Everything she had witnessed confirmed that less than four minutes after kissing Phillip Brandt in the dark of the vault, she was sitting with Viktor's hand around her wrist, though every detail of the abyss between the two men in this room—one who owned her shadow, and one who owned her soul remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the draft stirred the third row of gilt chairs in the Pompeian Salon, her crystal vial of lavender smelling salts held openly in her right hand remained fixed in place, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Not once during the final movement did she allow her eyes to turn toward the back of the hall. To anyone else, Phillip's tall figure standing at attention by the rear doors across the sea of field-gray shoulders might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the resumption of Schubert's quartet rising beneath the painted ceiling and the faint drift of smelling salts, hot chandelier wax, and the secret trace of Phillip's mouth still burning on her lips sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. Whatever happened next, she knew that tomorrow morning Wanda would carry Phillip's Minox cassette to Tadeusz, completing the Nowogrodzka repeater file.

In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Along the darkened tramlines of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. The rhythmic sweep of a searchlight passing behind the clouds whispered that the clock of the occupation was running down. In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. As the minute hand crept on, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

"Where were you when the siren went off?" Viktor asked, his hand closing immediately over her wrist.

"Locked in the mezzanine ladies' room with General Stahel’s fat wife and three hysterical typists," Marion murmured with an exasperated roll of her eyes. "If the Russians ever actually bomb this building, Viktor, I prefer to take my chances with the bombs rather than suffocate in the cellar with those women."

Within the high-walled gardens of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. A sudden hush in the street below the balcony railings blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that the wall between her cover and her birth name had to remain solid iron. In the silent arithmetic of rail capacities, depot coordinates, and cipher keys, she turned the arrogance of the conquerors into the weapon of their undoing. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

Viktor chuckled under his breath as the quartet began the third movement. Thirty feet away, standing beside the rear doors of the salon where the junior staff officers had returned from the shelter corridors, Hauptmann Phillip Brandt stood with his hands clasped behind his back, his face calm and unreadable in the chandelier light.

Inside Krystyna’s left velvet cuff, the tiny brass Minox cassette Phillip had given her was still warm from his skin.

* * *

At eleven o'clock, after the Berlin string quartet had taken its final bow in the Pompeian Salon and the guests were filing down the grand marble staircase of the Brühl Palace toward their cars, Krystyna walked on Viktor’s arm with the tiny brass Minox cassette of the *W-Netz* cipher schedule tucked securely inside the velvet cuff of her left sleeve.

Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. Outside the tall casements of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct echoed the patient, relentless tempo of the underground war. In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. In the hush that followed, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

She answered the silence with pulling her fur collar up against her cheeks as Viktor handed her into the heated Mercedes, letting the sleepy, music-lulled contentment of a woman going home from a concert serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the stamping of jackboots in the portico snow and the roar of staff car engines at the same moment she breathed in freezing December air, wet wool, and the warm brass cassette hidden against her left pulse until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. As the car pulled out into Plac Piłsudskiego, she touched the velvet cuff at her wrist like a talisman. Between the doorway and the marble rotunda at the foot of the Brühl Palace staircase, the night duty logbook open beneath Phillip's fountain pen at the guard desk marked the half-lit interval, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She held fast to the recognition that with a single stroke of his pen Phillip had erased the vault log while she carried the complete W-Netz schedule out the front door on the Colonel's arm, while the contrast with the absolute darkness of the steel map vault forty minutes ago when his mouth had found hers made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where tomorrow the Home Army's engineers would have the exact blueprint to sever every German command cable in Warsaw when the hour came.

Beyond the guarded courtyard of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. A nearly inaudible sigh of the coal fire sinking in the grate held the room in a suspended, watchful equilibrium. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, every memorized five-letter group was a bullet placed in a partisan rifle. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

Down in the ground-floor rotunda, where the SS palace guard was signing out the evening visitor passes, Hauptmann Phillip Brandt stood beside the duty desk with the night logbook open under his arm. While the guard lieutenant was saluting Oberst von Harenberg at the front doors, Phillip’s fountain pen drew a neat black line through the 21:15 air-raid vault inspection entry—erasing the last record that the connecting door between Section Ic and Viktor’s corridor had ever been unlatched.

The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Past the shuttered shopfronts of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. Along the fragile chain of couriers who walked unarmed through the garrison, each night survived behind the lines was a quiet triumph of discipline. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that every rivalry between the Army and the SS was a blade she could turn. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

As Marion stepped out into the freezing December snow under the portico, she glanced once into the polished glass of the revolving door. Reflected in the pane behind her stood Phillip under the rotunda chandelier—watching her go into the night with the man who owned her cover, while her mouth still burned with the first kiss they had shared in five years.

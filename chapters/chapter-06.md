# Chapter 6 — The Ghost on Ulica Foksal

*Warsaw — October 1943*

---

Warsaw smelled of wet brick dust, lime, and old smoke. When Krystyna stepped down from the Mitropa car at the Dworzec Główny on the second of October, an icy drizzle was blowing off the Vistula across the bombed-out iron ribs of the station roof. Everywhere hung giant red-and-black placards in German and Polish, while from the street outside came the metallic, nasal bark of the street megaphones—the *szczekaczki*—reading out a list of thirty Polish hostages shot that morning by order of the SS and Police Leader.

Combining the haughty, indifferent stare of a Frenchwoman who found Warsaw ugly and provincial with the quiet motion of tucking her gloved hand into the crook of Viktor's greatcoat sleeve with a fastidious shiver, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of wet coal dust, carbolic disinfectant, soaked wool, and charred brick hung in the draft alongside the metallic bark of the street megaphone reading hostage names through the drizzle steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She looked straight through the boy's eyes without blinking and stepped into the waiting Mercedes. In the hush that filled the rain-swept platform of the Dworzec Główny in Warsaw, the wooden barrier stenciled 'NUR FÜR DEUTSCHE' in black paint appeared almost motionless, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Her training insisted that one syllable of Polish spoken from her mouth or one flicker of recognition on this platform would mean instant death, though the thought of returning from her Swiss boarding school at this very station in June 1937 while her father waited with roses returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she had to walk through her own ruined city as its conqueror's whore until she could reach Kruk on Ulica Chmielna.

"Filthy weather," Viktor muttered, turning up the scarlet-lined lapels of his Generalstab greatcoat as two field-gray adjutants hurried forward down the platform, heels snapping together in salute. "And a filthy town. Keep your furs closed, Marion. Hanke has the Mercedes waiting at the military exit."

Marion drew the silver-fox collar high against her cheekbones, letting her gray-green eyes sweep the platform with the cool, incurious disdain of a stranger. Ten yards away, behind a wooden barrier marked *NUR FÜR DEUTSCHE*, Polish railway porters in tattered peaked caps and soaked coats were hauling mail sacks under the rifle butts of the Bahnschutzpolizei. Any one of them might have carried her father’s trunks five years ago.

Past the edge of the rain-streaked window of the Mercedes staff car turning onto Nowy Świat, the gutted, windowless facade of the Zamoyski Palace sliding past in the gray light waited in the gloom, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Her fingernails dug into the palm of her kid glove hard enough to draw blood, while her face remained as smooth as ivory. Nothing about a fresh pink paper proclamation pasted to a kiosk listing twenty names executed at Pawiak escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She forced herself to accept that the entire northern quarter of her city had been turned into a moonscape of crushed bone and brick since she left in 1940, though every detail of the cafés along Nowy Świat where she and her friends had drunk hot chocolate after lectures at the university remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, turning her head lazily toward Viktor as if bored by the dreary streets outside reinforced the quiet authority of a slight, world-weary sigh against the silver-fox collar of her coat, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the clang of a divided red streetcar bell and the splash of military tires through potholes and the faint drift of wet lime, horse manure, and the persistent, sweetish ash drifting from the northern ruins of the Ghetto that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise.

Outside on the Aleje Jerozolimskie, the city hit her like a fist to the chest. Entire blocks stood gutted from September 1939, their fire-blackened window sockets staring blind into the rain; to the north, beyond the Saxon Garden, a flat, apocalyptic wasteland of pulverized brick marked where the Warsaw Ghetto had been dynamited and burned to the ground five months earlier. Yet down the center of the avenue rolled crimson streetcars with the front car marked for Germans only, while starving children in wooden clogs darted between coal carts selling single cigarettes.

"Do not look out at the beggars," Viktor said inside the warm rear cabin of the Mercedes as they turned onto Nowy Świat. "In a week you will not even see them."

"At least the façade is eighteenth-century," she said aloud in German, and walked through her father's front door. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that she was stepping out of a German staff car onto the very cobblestones where her father had been dragged into a Gestapo van in 1940, while the contrast with her mother waving from the second-floor Venetian window on the morning Krystyna rode away to Konstancin made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the crunch of the Mercedes tires on the familiar gravel of her childhood courtyard at the same moment she breathed in wet linden leaves, damp stucco, and the oil of the sentry's Mauser rifle that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. Against the quiet of the room, the chipped stone cherub by the carriage block whose wing she had broken with her hoop at age eight stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted pausing for a fraction of a second on the running board to shake the rain from her hem and the familiar armor of the critical, appraising glance of a mistress inspecting her new quarters to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when she had to walk up her own marble staircase without letting Viktor see that she knew where every step creaked.

Then the Mercedes swung right into the quiet, tree-lined cul-de-sac of Ulica Foksal—and pulled up before the wrought-iron gates of Number 14. Above the stone archway where the Stankiewicz coat of arms had been chiseled since 1790 hung a black-and-white wooden eagle sign: *WEHRMACHTKOMMANDANTUR WARSCHAU — OFFIZIERSQUARTIER*.

* * *

The grand marble staircase of Foksal 14 smelled no longer of her mother’s beeswax and dried rose petals; it smelled of German floor oil, boiled cabbage from the orderly kitchen in the basement, and wet jackboots. Yet as Krystyna climbed to the second-floor piano nobile beside Viktor, every Banister rail and plaster acanthus leaf screamed her name in silence.

She observed the pale rectangle on the damask wall where Count Ignacy's portrait had been ripped down with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Not three paces from the white-and-gold drawing room of the Stankiewicz palace on Ulica Foksal, her mother's black Bechstein grand piano standing beside the garden casements lay in the amber half-light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. In the choreography of the room, running her gloved fingertips lightly across the closed mahogany fallboard of the Bechstein flowed seamlessly into the cool, possessive appraisal of a woman taking inventory of her spoils, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She did not look twice at the empty rectangle on the wall; she looked out at the wet linden tree in the garden. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that to stand in her dead mother's drawing room pretending to be a stranger admiring the furniture required an iron clamp around her heart, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of Helena Stankiewiczowa's slender fingers playing the Chopin Nocturne in C-sharp minor on that very keyboard in August 1939. Before the night was over, behind the bookcase in the adjoining dressing room was the service shaft that led straight down to the garden coal door, and she intended to be ready.

A gaunt Polish housemaid in a black uniform and white apron stood by the double doors of the main salon, holding a brass tray of keys with her eyes fixed terrified on the parquet. It was a girl Krystyna did not know—thank God, not old Hanna—recruited by the German Quartieramt after the house was seized.

"This is the drawing room," Viktor said, pushing open the tall white-and-gold double doors. "And the master suite overlooks the back garden. Better than you expected, eh?"

Nothing moved in her expression beyond turning away from the bedroom doorway to unpin her hat before the Venetian pier glass and the unhurried vanity of a traveler smoothing her hair after thirty-six hours on a train, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Her fingers brushed the tiny scratch in the silvered glass as she laid her hat aside. Framed by the geometry of the threshold of her parents' former bedroom, the carved Empire bed newly draped in crimson German silk seemed almost innocuous, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. The sight of the small scratch on the bottom corner of the Venetian mirror that Phillip had made with his skate blade in 1936 brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She held fast to the recognition that tonight she would have to lie beneath Viktor von Harenberg in the very room where she had been born, though the thought of knocking on this bedroom door as a little girl when thunder frightened her in the night returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of camphor, damp wool, and the turpentine of newly varnished floorboards hung in the draft alongside the thud of Corporal Hanke setting her Parisian steamer trunks down in the dressing room an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place.

Krystyna stepped across the threshold of the white drawing room. Her mother’s Bechstein grand piano still stood in the curve of the tall garden windows; her father’s Canaletto etching of the Vistula still hung above the white marble fireplace, though a portrait of the Führer now occupied the wall where Count Ignacy’s oil portrait had hung. And through the open doorway of the master bedroom stood her parents’ carved Empire bed—re-covered in crimson German damask for Viktor von Harenberg and his mistress.

"It is quite passable, Viktor," Marion said, walking across the parquet to run one gloved finger along the polished lid of her mother’s Bechstein. "Though we shall have to do something about that hideous wallpaper in the hall. And you promised me a fire."

When Viktor hung up the receiver, he told her that his staff conference at the Brühl Palace began at nine tomorrow morning. In the presence of the black Bakelite Wehrmacht field telephone sitting on her mother's marquetry card table, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the ring of the field telephone installed on the brass side table and the faint drift of old leather-bound Polish encyclopedias and fresh black telephone cable as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. Where the draft stirred the oak-paneled library adjoining the salon, the heavy iron safe built into the wall beside her father's mahogany desk remained fixed in place, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. With polite disinterest as she warmed her hands before the drawing-room fire governing the motion of glancing casually into the library while Viktor answered the field telephone from the Brühl Palace to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. No illusion could soften the fact that the Quartieramt had left her father's built-in wall safe intact and fitted it with a new German combination dial for Viktor's papers, though every detail of watching her father spin the old brass dial to take out her grandmother's pearls before balls remained as sharp as cut glass.

Viktor came up behind her by the piano, unbuckling his wet greatcoat and letting it drop onto the gilded chair where her father had read the *Kurjer Warszawski* every evening. "You will be warm enough in five minutes," he murmured, his hands closing around her waist.

* * *

On the third morning after their arrival, while Viktor was locked in a four-hour transport conference with the Ostbahn directors at the Brühl Palace, Marion announced that she was going out to walk the shops along Nowy Świat and Krakowskie Przedmieście. Corporal Hanke offered to follow with the car; she waved him off with an imperious flick of her glove, declaring that she was not an invalid and wished to see whether Warsaw possessed a single decent furrier.

Between the doorway and the crowded pavement outside the Church of the Holy Cross on Krakowskie Przedmieście, the small silver-and-enamel Wehrmacht household pass pinned to her coat lapel marked the half-lit interval, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Her senses caught the shuffle of hundreds of wooden-soled shoes and the clang of a passing tram at the same moment she breathed in wet wool, horse dung, cheap Machorka tobacco, and autumn fog sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. To be hated by her own people on the street was the armor that would keep her alive. To anyone else, the chipped stone steps of the Holy Cross church where flower-sellers still huddled might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with walking past the old doctor without breaking her long, arrogant stride, letting the cold, unseeing stare of the Herrenvolk looking through a conquered crowd serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where that single encounter proved that her hair dye, her Paris makeup, and four years of war had made her invisible in plain sight.

Yet the walk down the steps of Foksal 14 into Nowy Świat was the longest half-mile of her life. Every face passing on the wet pavement was a minefield. Outside the Church of the Holy Cross—where Chopin’s heart was bricked into the pillar—she passed within four feet of Doctor Kamiński, the family physician who had treated her scarlet fever at age twelve. His hair was white now, his coat threadbare; he glanced up at the tall blonde woman in the Parisian silver-fox coat and German pass-pin on her lapel, and looked quickly down at the gutter as Poles had learned to do when the occupiers passed.

He did not recognize the twenty-five-year-old courtesan with the carmine mouth and hard Strasbourg poise as the nineteen-year-old girl who had left Warsaw four years ago. Her heart steadied.

Behind his steel-rimmed spectacles, Tadeusz's gray eyes flared once with fierce, incredulous recognition. Across the narrow, ticking interior of the clock shop at Ulica Chmielna 22, her gold Patek Philippe wristwatch lying on the green baize repair pad rested in the quiet shadow, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She listened to the asynchronous ticking of forty wall clocks and pocket watches along the shelves while the room held the aroma of watchmaker's whale oil, brass filings, damp sawdust, and nitric acid until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. She knew beyond doubt that Tadeusz 'Kruk' Kowalski had been her brother's chemistry tutor at the Warsaw Polytechnic before losing his finger at Modlin in 1939, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the last time she had seen Tadeusz in a smoke-filled cellar on Ulica Wilcza in January 1940. As she completed leaning over the glass counter as if pointing out a scratch on the bezel of her watch, she drew the impatient posture of a German customer instructing a Polish tradesman around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that with three words in Polish she had closed the circuit between the Rue deRivoli and the Home Army's Oddział II.

Wait until you cross Aleje Jerozolimskie, she told herself, checking the reflection of a pharmacy window before turning west into the crowded, soot-stained bustle of Ulica Chmielna. Halfway down the block, wedged between a German-requisitioned leather shop and a bakery with a bread queue fifty yards long, hung a rusted tin pocket watch above a narrow doorway: *A. KOWALSKI — ZEGARMISTRZ (UHRMACHER)*.

A brass bell jangled as she pushed open the door. The narrow shop smelled of watch oil, nitric acid, and damp sawdust; dozens of pendulums ticked out of sync along the walls. Behind the high wooden counter sat a man of forty with steel-rimmed spectacles, iron-gray hair cropped close to a square skull, and a jeweler’s loupe wedged into his right eye. The fourth finger of his left hand was missing at the second knuckle.

A subtle trace of solder flux, benzine, and the damp courtyard air behind the shop hung in the draft alongside the shop bell hanging silent above the latched street door filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. "Stefan died in my arms in Paris," she whispered in Polish. "Here is what he paid for." Through the lens of her cover identity, the concealed shortwave receiver built into the base of a grandfather clock in the corner was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the rapid, clipped efficiency of a trained field operative briefing a station chief with the quiet motion of slipping the three hollowed cakes of Roger & Gallet soap from her shopping parcel onto the workbench, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She measured the risk by the certainty that she had five minutes at most to pass the eighteen Minox cassettes and the Fall Weichsel summary before returning to Nowy Świat, though the thought of Stefan Wilk's dying whisper in the rain behind the Rue de la Huchette returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that the Fall Weichsel films would give the Home Army the complete German rail defense grid for the central Vistula.

He did not remove the loupe as she approached the counter. "Ich bedauere, gnädige Frau," he said in flat, accentless German. "We are three weeks behind on military watch repairs."

Krystyna laid her small gold Patek Philippe wristwatch on the green baize pad between them. "The mainspring is not broken, Herr Kowalski," she said softly in German, then switched for a single sentence into the pure, crystal Warsaw Polish of the Foksal: *"The swallow brings three straws from the western eaves, and Stefan Wilk sends his last watch from the Seine."*

The jeweler’s loupe dropped from Tadeusz Kowalski’s eye into his palm.

* * *

Behind the green baize curtain at the rear of the shop, Tadeusz Kowalski worked with the speed of a surgeon, splitting the three cakes of French soap along their invisible wax seams and lifting out the eighteen lead-foil-wrapped Minox cassettes. His scarred hands did not shake, though when he looked up at Krystyna, his weathered face was tight with emotion.

She did not allow herself to forget that Tadeusz had just spoken the name that had lived in the deepest, most sacred chamber of her heart for five years, though every detail of Phillip Brandt standing in the sunlit linden orchard at Konstancin in August 1938 with his hands in her hair remained as sharp as cut glass. Beside the narrow space behind the green baize curtain of the clock shop, the outline of the eighteen lead-foil-wrapped Minox cassettes resting in Tadeusz's scarred palm marked the stillness, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. For the space of three ticks of the grandfather clock, the floor of the shop seemed to drop away beneath her boots. She let her attention brush past the official German repair ticket stamped 'Abholung Dienstag' in Tadeusz's hand, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the heavy, measured tick of the grandfather clock concealing the radio set and the faint drift of French lavender soap shavings, watch oil, and damp plaster steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, Phillip Brandt—the boy who had held her heart, who had sworn under the lindens that no border would ever divide them—was wearing a Wehrmacht captain's uniform inside the Brühl Palace, whatever price it demanded.

"When London wired that 'Jaskółka' was coming in on Harenberg’s train, I did not know until last night that Jaskółka was Ignacy Stankiewicz’s daughter," Tadeusz murmured in Polish, his voice barely louder than the ticking of the grandfather clock beside them. "And they quartered him at Foksal 14. Good God, Krystyna."

"Save your pity for the couriers in Pawiak, Tadeusz," she said quietly, watching the street window through the slit in the baize curtain. "Foksal 14 has a built-in wall safe in my father’s library and a service shaft from my dressing room into the back garden. Have you put someone inside the Hotel Europejski for our weekly drops?"

She trusted taking the German repair ticket between two steady fingers and slipping it into her purse and the familiar armor of the calm, clipped nod of an agent concluding a briefing to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the distant rumble of a coal lorry grinding down toward Marszałkowska at the same moment she breathed in cold sweat beneath her silk blouse and the sharp metallic tang of shock that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. "I will watch him," she said in a voice that sounded as if it came from the bottom of a frozen well. In the corner of the slit in the green baize curtain overlooking the street window, she noticed the blurred shapes of German soldiers passing the bakery queue outside, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. There was no escaping the conclusion that she could not tell Tadeusz that Hauptmann Phillip Brandt knew her face, her voice, her childhood scars, and the name Krystyna Stankiewiczówna better than any man alive, while the contrast with Phillip's last letter from Berlin in August 1939, burned in the grate on Ulica Foksal the morning the Luftwaffe bombed Warsaw made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when if Tadeusz knew Phillip Brandt could identify her on sight, Oddział II would order her immediate extraction—or order Phillip's assassination before tomorrow night's reception.

"Better than someone," Tadeusz said, handing her back the repaired watch receipt written in German. "Wanda Zieleńska—your mother’s seamstress’s girl. We have her working as a second-floor linen maid at the Europejski under the name Sarna. When Harenberg dines there or sends your gowns to be pressed, Wanda handles the laundry basket."

A sudden warmth pierced the ice around Krystyna’s chest: little freckled Wanda, who used to hold the pins when her mother had gowns fitted at Konstancin. "And the Brühl Palace?" Krystyna asked. "Paris warned me there is a new Slavic desk chief in Abteilung Ic."

From where she stood in the pavement of Ulica Chmielna outside the clockmaker's shop, her shopping parcel tucked beneath her silver-fox sleeve was just visible, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Each step back toward Ulica Foksal felt like walking barefoot across broken glass. Against the quiet of the room, her own pale reflection passing across the dark glass of a haberdasher's window stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Her training insisted that tomorrow night at the Brühl Palace she would walk into a lighted ballroom on Viktor von Harenberg's arm and stand face to face with Phillip, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the way Phillip used to whisper 'Krysiu' against her palm when they swam in the Jeziorka at dusk. In the choreography of the room, walking steadily eastward toward Nowy Świat without once faltering in her stride flowed seamlessly into the lacquered, untouchable mask of Marion Herschlag, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the brass shop bell jangling once as the door closed behind her while the room held the aroma of autumn drizzle, wet coal dust, and the sudden, suffocating cold of the Warsaw wind that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike.

Tadeusz’s mouth hardened. "A Hauptmann transferred from Berlin three weeks ago. Speaks Polish like a native—which makes him twice as dangerous to our people. His name is Hauptmann Phillip Brandt. Watch yourself around him at tomorrow night’s reception, Krystyna. He is Harenberg’s right hand for counter-espionage."

* * *

That night in the master bedroom of Foksal 14, the wind off the Vistula rattled the tall garden casements—the same casements through which Krystyna had watched the linden leaves fall when she was a child. Inside, a coal fire burned high in her mother’s white porcelain stove, throwing flickering orange shadows across the crimson German damask of the bed.

She locked her hands behind Viktor's neck and used the physical pain of his grip to drown out the ghosts in the room. She forced herself to accept that to endure Viktor's body inside her parents' bedroom while knowing Phillip Brandt was six blocks away at the Brühl Palace was the cruelest test of her life, though the thought of the summer night in 1938 when Phillip had climbed the garden wall outside that very window to bring her white lilacs returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of anthracite coal, French cognac, sweat, and the new crimson damask hung in the draft alongside the wind rattling the garden casements and the heavy thud of the Empire bedstead pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. She observed the carved mahogany swan on the bedpost that her mother had polished every Easter with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond turning her face sideways into the pillow and biting the linen to keep her voice steady and the fierce, arching heat of Marion Herschlag giving the Colonel what he demanded, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. Not a single second could be wasted when if Viktor sensed even a shadow of distraction in her body tonight, his suspicion would be awake before tomorrow's reception.

Viktor returned from the Brühl Palace at eleven, smelling of rain, cigar smoke, and the exhilaration of absolute command. He stripped off his tunic, revealing the white linen shirt stretched across his broad chest and the Verdun scar curving beneath his ribs, and watched her where she stood by the porcelain stove in a sheer black negligee.

"Tomorrow night Governor Fischer and the entire Wehrmacht command will be at the Brühl Palace reception," Viktor said, pouring two glasses of French cognac from the traveling case. "Wear the white satin with the emeralds. Let these provincial staff wives see what Paris looks like."

The sight of the emerald necklace box waiting on her mother's dressing table for tomorrow night brought home the cold reality of the uniform and the machinery behind it, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Where the shadow fell across the Venetian pier glass reflecting the firelit bed, her sheer black silk negligee twisted around her waist remained half-hidden, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. With the breathless, conquering stillness of a woman who left her lover spent governing the motion of meeting Viktor's final, shuddering thrusts with a calculated clench of her thighs that wrung a hoarse shout from his throat to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, matching the relentless pressure of his hands with an unflinching heat of her own. When Viktor rolled heavy and sated beside her, she stared straight up at the plaster rosette on the ceiling. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that what had five years in Hitler's army done to the boy who used to read Rilke to her by the river?, though every detail of Phillip's dark eyes looking back at her from the train window in Berlin in 1938 remained as sharp as cut glass. Whatever happened next, she knew that in twenty hours she would know whether Phillip Brandt was her salvation or her executioner.

Krystyna took the glass from his hand and drained half of it in a single swallow, welcoming the burn in her throat. All evening a single name had echoed like a church bell inside her skull: *Hauptmann Phillip Brandt*. Tomorrow night she would walk into a room where Phillip stood in field-gray wool. And tonight she had to lie in her dead parents’ bed with the man who owned her cover.

When Viktor set his glass down and reached for her, dragging her against his chest with a rough, possessive groan, she closed her eyes tightly so she would not see her mother’s porcelain stove or the ceiling molding of her childhood. She let him throw her back across the crimson damask, her fingernails digging into his wrists as he parted her thighs and drove into her with his familiar, bruising weight. In the dark behind her tightly shut eyelids, while Viktor’s harsh breath rasped against her ear and the bedstead struck the wall of her father’s house, she fought with every ounce of her will to keep another man’s name from escaping her bitten lips.

She answered the silence with wrapping a cashmere shawl over her bare shoulders as she stood barefoot by the cold glass, letting the icy, resurrected composure of an operative facing a firing squad serve as her shield, turning his possessive hunger into the very instrument of his blindness. She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass looking toward the Saxon Garden and whispered Phillip's name once to the silent room. Under the dim lamp near the garden window of Foksal 14 at four in the morning, the bare branches of the old linden tree scraping against the second-floor balustrade cast a narrow shade, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. In the presence of the faint white scar on her left wrist gleaming in the dark windowpane, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that there was no retreat from tomorrow night; she had to walk down the Brühl Palace staircase in white satin with her head high, while the contrast with her father's last words to her: 'A Stankiewicz does not bow to uniform cloth' made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the distant, muffled pop of a rifle shot somewhere toward Powiśle at the same moment she breathed in cold night rain, wet earth, and the ash of the coal stove as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry.

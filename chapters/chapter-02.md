# Chapter 2 — Blackout on the Seine

*Paris — June 1943*

---

Three weeks later, the June sun poured through the tall open casements of the Rue de Rivoli dining room, gilding the silver coffee pot and the porcelain plate of yellow Normandy butter that no ordinary Parisian had tasted since 1940. Viktor sat at the head of the table in a crisp fresh tunic, his hair damp from his bath, slicing the top from a soft-boiled egg with surgical neatness while an Abwehr circular lay beside his elbow.

Inside her chest, a fierce, silent jubilation flared and was instantly smothered beneath ten inches of ice. She measured the risk by the certainty that the Siedlce derailment meant her Minox film from May had reached Warsaw and Tadeusz's demolition squads on time, while the contrast with the small brick station at Siedlce where her uncle's stud grooms used to board the Warsaw express made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the cheerful clatter of hooves from a fiacre passing the Tuileries railings below at the same moment she breathed in freshly ground Brazilian coffee, warm brioche, and Viktor's shaving soap as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. In the presence of the red 'GEHEIM' stamp visible through the back of the circular beside Viktor's butter knife, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She answered the silence with the steady, unhurried pouring of dark coffee into his Meissen cup, letting the vapid, self-absorbed serenity of a mistress who could not find Siedlce on a map serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Time was narrowing toward the point where an SD Sturmbannführer from Berlin meant the Gestapo suspected the leak was in Paris rather than Cracow.

"A munitions train was derailed four kilometers east of Siedlce on Tuesday night," Viktor said without looking up from his egg. "Eighty tons of artillery fuses burned on the embankment. The partisans hit the train during a forty-minute water-stop that was only added to the timetable seventy-two hours before departure."

Marion, seated across from him in a pale ivory silk peignoir that showed the faint fading shadow of his fingers on her collarbone, lifted the silver pot and filled his cup with steaming Brazilian coffee. Her hand did not waver by a hair's breadth. "Siedlce?" she asked, setting the pot down on its trivet and reaching for a brioche. "Is that near Russia, Viktor, or near the marshes you were complaining of last month?"

To anyone else, the faint yellow-green bruise fading beneath the lace of her collar might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Across the tall French balcony overlooking the green canopy of the Tuileries, her ivory silk peignoir stirred by the warm June breeze rested in the quiet shadow, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. As she completed lifting her wrist to dab a crumb from the corner of her lower lip with a linen napkin, she drew a look of mild annoyance that work should intrude upon a June morning around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She smiled across the rim of her teacup as Viktor folded the circular and slipped it into his breast pocket. She did not allow herself to forget that if Berlin had sent an SD auditor, the dead-drop at Saint-Germain-des-Prés would have to be shifted after today, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her handler Zygmunt's rule that a successful sabotage always brings the hounds back along the wire. Beneath the surface of the evening lay the inescapable fact that she had to warn Zygmunt at two o'clock before the SD began surveilling the Left Bank couriers.

He looked up then, his cold blue eyes narrowing slightly against the morning glare. "It is fifty miles east of Warsaw. And the timetable was drafted at the Hôtel Majestic before being wired to Cracow. Which means someone between the Avenue Kléber and the Vistula has long ears."

"Then you should shoot your cipher clerks," Marion said lightly, spreading apricot conserve across a corner of brioche and biting into it with small white teeth. "They are all pale little men with spectacles who spend their evenings in Montmartre cabarets. Comtesse Solange says half the girls at the Shéhérazade are paid by the British."

Combining a soft, purring sigh against his collar as his hand slipped inside the lapel of her peignoir with the quiet motion of tilting her head back over the chair rail so his mouth could find her throat, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. "Wear the black Balenciaga tonight," he murmured against her ear, his thumb grazing her nipple before he strode out. Within the sunlit parquet by the dining-room doors, her gaze returned briefly to Viktor's peaked cap with its silver bullion cord resting on the sideboard, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. It was in small things like the cold metal of his Iron Cross pressing through the thin silk against her shoulder blade that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. There was no escaping the conclusion that he never left the apartment without claiming a physical reminder of his ownership, though the thought of the morning her father had kissed her forehead in the hall on Ulica Foksal before walking out to the Gestapo car returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of warm wool, leather polish, and the sweet butter on her tongue hung in the draft alongside the click of his heels as he rose from the table and came around behind her chair until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing.

Viktor studied her across the white damask tablecloth for three long seconds—taking in the sunlit gold of her hair, the lazy curve of her throat, the utter absence of guilt in her gray-green eyes. Then the corner of his mouth twitched. "Stick to horses and dressmakers, Liebchen. Tonight we dine at Lapérouse. A Sturmbannführer from Berlin has arrived to audit the Paris liaison offices, and I want him to see that my house is in order."

* * *

At twenty minutes past two, after spending two hours at the salon of Cristobal Balenciaga on the Avenue George V and switching metro trains twice between Concorde and Mabillon, Marion stepped out of the bright June glare into the cool, incense-heavy gloom of the Church of Saint-Germain-des-Prés. Only three old women in black shawls knelt before the side altar of the Virgin, their rosaries clicking softly in the eleventh-century silence.

The sound of her own native language inside the dark wooden box felt like cold well-water on a parched tongue. Through the lens of her cover identity, the loose brass brad at the lower corner of the confessional grille was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the muffled click of rosary beads from the side chapel and the rumble of a bus on the boulevard and the faint drift of burned beeswax candles, old frankincense, and damp Romanesque stone filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. Beside the dark oak confessional in the north aisle of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the outline of the carved wooden lattice separating her face from Zygmunt's shadow marked the stillness, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Without haste, folding her white-gloved hands piously upon the narrow wooden kneeler reinforced the quiet authority of the bowed, penitent posture of a parishioner receiving absolution, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. Her training insisted that Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler had come directly from the Gestapo headquarters on Aleja Szucha in Warsaw, though every detail of the iron gates of the Ministry of Religious Affairs on Aleja Szucha, which every child in Warsaw now knew as the house of torture remained as sharp as cut glass.

She dipped her gloved fingers into the stone stoup, crossed herself with the practiced grace of a Polish Catholic raised by Ursuline nuns, and slipped into the third oak confessional along the north aisle. Through the carved wooden lattice came the dry, papery cough she knew by heart.

"Bless me, Father," she murmured in French, before dropping her voice to a barely audible Polish whisper that made her throat ache with homesickness. "The swallow brings three straws from the western eaves."

In the corner of the stone pillar outside the confessional door, she noticed a rack of flickering votive candles casting small pools of yellow light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. Her senses caught Zygmunt's dry, tubercular cough muffled into his handkerchief at the same moment she breathed in tallow smoke, damp wool, and the faint camphor of the church vestments steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. She did not wait for Zygmunt to leave his side of the box; their protocol forbade ever seeing each other under the open sky. She let her attention brush past the worn Latin inscription carved into the flagstone beneath her Ferragamo heel, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted bowing her head toward the altar as she stepped out of the booth and genuflected and the familiar armor of the tranquil, unhurried demeanor of a woman leaving afternoon prayers to see her through, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. She could not afford a single misstep when she had to switch to the secondary dead-drop at the Jardin du Luxembourg until Kessler left Paris.

Behind the lattice, Zygmunt—whose real name had once been Stefan Wilk, a major of the Polish General Staff before 1939 and now a stooped sixty-year-old bookseller on the Rue Saint-Séverin—drew a sharp breath. "Siedlce burned for two days, Jaskółka. London wired their commendation last night. The fuses for two entire artillery regiments went up in one flash."

"And the bill has arrived," Krystyna whispered, sliding the Guerlain lipstick tube through the loose panel at the bottom of the grille while her eyes remained fixed on the crucifix in front of her. "An SD Sturmbannführer from Berlin dines with Harenberg tonight at Lapérouse. Have they named him?"

She turned toward the Seine, letting the summer heat warm her gloves while her mind prepared for the evening's duel. From where she stood in the sunlit porch of Saint-Germain-des-Prés facing the Deux Magots, the green tin kiosk plastered with Vichy decrees and German theatre bills was just visible, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She listened to the hiss of a café espresso machine and the clatter of wooden-soled shoes on the pavement while the room held the aroma of roasted chicory, hot asphalt, and lime blossoms along the Boulevard Saint-Germain that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that Kessler was thirty-four, sober, and trained at Aleja Szucha—a hunter of a wholly different breed from Viktor's Wehrmacht staff, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of her father's voice telling her that the SS were not soldiers but executioners in tailored cloth. In the choreography of the room, pausing on the church steps to adjust the black net veil over her cheekbones flowed seamlessly into an air of leisurely Parisian elegance as she scanned the café terraces in the reflection of a shop window, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. Before the night was over, there was no tail behind her today, which meant Kessler had not yet narrowed his net to Viktor's household, and she intended to be ready.

There was a brief silence behind the oak screen, followed by the rustle of Zygmunt’s sleeve as he pocketed the tube. "Erich Kessler. Formerly of Department IV in Warsaw, Aleja Szucha. He is thirty-four, does not drink past his first glass, and broke the Poznań courier ring in forty-two. And Krystyna—there is word from Warsaw. Your family's house on Ulica Foksal has been refitted as a senior officers' billet for the Oberfeldkommandantur. If Harenberg is sent east, that is the street where they will quarter him."

* * *

The private cabinet particulier on the second floor of Lapérouse had been built in the reign of Louis XV for royal mistresses; its low ceiling, scratched mirrors—where courtesans had once tested the authenticity of their lovers' diamonds against the glass—and crimson velvet banquettes felt closer to a jewel box than a dining room. Yet when Sturmbannführer Erich Kessler rose from the table as Marion entered on Viktor’s arm, the temperature in the low room seemed to drop ten degrees.

A subtle trace of rich Burgundy wine, roasted duck skin, candle wax, and dried human blood hung in the draft alongside the soft hiss of the duck press in the corridor and the lap of the Seine outside the narrow casement that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike. She did not look at the dark brown crust along the notebook's spine; she looked straight at the penciled French script. Against the quiet of the room, the diamond-shaped SD patch stitched to the lower left sleeve of Kessler's black cuff stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond a fastidious wrinkle of her nose as she nudged the oilskin cover open with the tip of a silver butter knife rather than her fingers and the squeamish, aristocratic distaste of a woman who hated having ugly things brought to the dinner table, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. Everything she had witnessed confirmed that this was a deliberate trap: Kessler wanted to see whether the sight of a dead courier's blood made her pupils dilate, though the thought of the bloodstained handkerchief of her cousin Marek after the Gestapo had questioned him in 1940 returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when if the address in the notebook was a live safe house in Lyon, she had three seconds to alter it without alerting Kessler.

Kessler was slender, pale, and immaculate in his black SD dress uniform with the silver runic tabs and the diamond SD lozenge on his left sleeve. He had colorless blond hair combed flat across a high skull and eyes the shade of watered gin. When he bowed over Marion’s hand, his lips stopped a millimeter short of her skin.

"Gnädiges Fräulein," Kessler said in a soft, pleasant Saxony accent that carried not a trace of warmth. "Oberst von Harenberg warned me that Paris had its distractions, though he understated the case."

She held fast to the recognition that the penciled line read 'Chez la veuve Moreau, 14 Rue de la Charité'—a real courier relay she knew from Zygmunt's briefings, though every detail of the map of the Lyon-Geneva courier line she had studied before crossing from Switzerland remained as sharp as cut glass. Where the shadow fell across the diamond-scratched mirror behind the crimson velvet banquette, the reflection of Kessler's unblinking gin-pale eyes watching her eyelashes remained half-hidden, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. "Your clerks need better spectacles, Herr Sturmbannführer," she murmured, and read the altered street name aloud without a tremor. She observed the silver Totenkopf ring on Kessler's slender fourth finger with outward indifference, though her memory photographed every contour, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She was acutely aware of the clink of Viktor's fork against his porcelain plate and the faint drift of truffles, aged Chambertin, and the cold iron smell of the dried stain on the oilskin pressing against her awareness with quiet insistence. Whatever happened next, she knew that changing 'Charité' to 'Charrue'—a street three quarters of a mile away—could buy the Lyon cell twelve hours to scatter.

"And Viktor warned me that Berlin had sent a monk to count our champagne corks, Herr Sturmbannführer," Marion replied, sinking onto the crimson banquette in her black Balenciaga gown and letting the diamond bracelet Viktor had given her at Christmas catch the candlelight. "Do tell me you drink wine. Nothing ruins a pressed duck at Lapérouse like a man drinking mineral water."

Viktor barked a short laugh and signaled the sommelier to pour the Chambertin, his heavy hand settling proprietarily over Marion’s knee beneath the tablecloth. Kessler smiled thinly, took a single sip of the Burgundy, and set his glass down.

She answered the silence with lifting her Chambertin glass and taking a slow, appreciative sip while meeting Kessler's stare, letting the cool, unbothered poise of a woman who had just performed a parlor trick serve as her shield, so that anyone watching saw only the exact woman the papers declared her to be. Her senses caught the distant chime of Notre-Dame's clock drifting across the dark river at the same moment she breathed in burned sugar from the soufflé, Burgundy, and Kessler's starch-stiffened wool an unmistakable signature of the hour and the place. "Put your butcher's ledger away, Kessler," Viktor growled across the table. "Fräulein Herschlag is not on the payroll of the Prinz-Albrecht-Straße." Under the dim lamp near the white damask tablecloth between the candlesticks, Kessler's pale hand closing slowly over the oilskin notebook cast a narrow shade, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. No illusion could soften the fact that Kessler did not know Lyon well enough to spot the substitution of Rue de la Charrue for Rue de la Charité on the spot, while the contrast with her mother teaching her at age ten to play a wrong note in a Chopin étude as though it were written in the score made the present hour all the starker. Time was narrowing toward the point where Viktor was already bristling at an SD officer bringing Gestapo business to his private dinner table.

"In my line of work, Fräulein Herschlag, wine blurs the margins," Kessler said, reaching into his black tunic pocket and laying a small, blood-stiffened oilskin notebook beside the silver breadbasket. "We took this four hours ago from a French railway courier who jumped from a third-story window in Lyon. My Paris clerks are arguing over two lines of provincial slang on the third page. Since you were raised on both sides of the Rhine, perhaps your ear is finer than theirs."

* * *

On the drive back across the Pont Neuf, Viktor said nothing at all. He sat with his jaw set like granite, his gloved hands resting on his knees, the silence inside the Horch thick with the territorial rage that always seized him when the SS encroached upon his domain. Krystyna knew better than to speak first; when Viktor was in this mood, words were merely kindling.

By the threshold of the dim drawing room of the Rue de Rivoli apartment, Viktor's peaked cap lying overturned on the marble console stood out in sharp relief, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She let her gloved fingers rise slowly to cover the back of his wrists where he gripped her arms. In the presence of the open collar of his field-gray tunic showing the thick red scar at his throat, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. With cold clarity she reckoned that Viktor's hatred of the SS was her strongest shield, yet his pride was also a loaded pistol pointed at her heart, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the basement cells of Aleja Szucha that Zygmunt had described only two hours earlier in the confessional. As she completed standing motionless within the bruising vise of his hands without pulling back, she drew a steady, unflinching gaze that held neither guilt nor plea around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She listened to the harsh rasp of Viktor's breathing in the silent room while the room held the aroma of wet night fog, Burgundy wine, and the ozone of rising fury as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry.

Wait until the third deadbolt clicked shut in the Rue de Rivoli foyer, she told herself. Wait until the uniform comes off.

In the drawing room, Viktor did not even pour a brandy. He tore his collar hook loose with one thick finger, threw his peaked cap onto the console, and turned on her before she had finished unpinning her veil. "A bloodstained notebook at my dinner table," he snarled, his hands closing hard over her upper arms. "That creeping little hangman from Himmler’s sewer thinks he can sniff around my staff—and around my woman—because someone leaked a train schedule in Siedlce!"

His blue eyes darkened until the iris was almost swallowed by the pupil. She knew beyond doubt that she had to let him expend his paranoia on her body tonight so that by morning he would write an angry protest to General von Stülpnagel barring Kessler from his department, though the thought of the way a wild boar in the Masurian forests always charged straight at the spear returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. A subtle trace of crushed black silk, warm skin, and the iron tang of adrenaline hung in the draft alongside the distant wail of an ambulance siren across the river on the Left Bank sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. To anyone else, the pulse hammering visibly in the hollow of Viktor's temple might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining the dark, fearless intimacy of a woman who welcomed his storm with the quiet motion of taking a half-step closer until her breasts pressed flat against the wool of his chest, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that the Lyon cell had until dawn to evacuate Rue de la Charité before Kessler's clerks checked a street map of Lyon.

"He is a policeman, Viktor," Marion said calmly, looking straight up into his suffused face. "Policemen have dirty fingernails and see thieves under every bed. Did it displease you that I translated his little scribble?"

"It displeased me that he looked at you as if you were a file on his desk!" Viktor’s fingers dug into the bare flesh of her arms above her long black gloves. "Listen to me, Marion. Kessler was three years at the Aleja Szucha in Warsaw. I have seen what they leave of women in those cellars. If I ever thought—for a single second—that there was a shadow behind your eyes that did not belong to me, I would not hand you to Kessler."

It was in small things like the heavy silver buckle of his service belt pressing through her skirt that the true nature of their rule revealed itself, where every courtesy concealed a blade. Past the edge of the gilded pier glass between the drawing-room windows, her black net veil falling unpinned to the parquet floor waited in the gloom, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. Without haste, tilting her throat back to bare the pale skin from collarbone to ear reinforced the quiet authority of a look of fierce, intoxicating surrender, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. "I would break your neck with my own two hands," he breathed into her mouth, and crushed her back against the tall shutters. She measured the risk by the certainty that he needed to prove to himself that neither Kessler nor Berlin could touch what belonged to him, though every detail of her own cold oath that she would survive both the wolf and the hangman remained as sharp as cut glass. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, every bruise tonight would buy her another month of untouchable protection inside the Hôtel Majestic, whatever price it demanded.

She did not pull back from the bruising pressure of his thumbs. Instead she stepped half an inch closer, letting the black silk of her bodice brush the open wool of his tunic. "And what would you do, Viktor?" she whispered.

* * *

He did not carry her to the bed at first. Driven by the twin goads of Kessler’s intrusion and the dark wine in his veins, Viktor pinned her upright against the tall mahogany shutters of the drawing room, his large hands tearing the black Balenciaga silk from her waist while his mouth ground against her throat and breastbone. The carved molding bit into her bare shoulder blades; the brass latch of the shutter pressed cold as ice against her spine.

She trusted the fierce clench of her thighs around his waist as she drove him toward exhaustion and the familiar armor of an untamed, vocal abandon that echoed off the high plaster ceiling to see her through, turning his possessive hunger into the very instrument of his blindness. Power in this room was a seesaw in the dark, and the one on the bottom held the fulcrum. Set against the tall mahogany shutters of the drawing room, the torn shreds of black lace lying beside her high-heeled shoes held a quiet, insistent weight, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. Through the lens of her cover identity, the four parallel red scratches her nails had left across the nape of his neck was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She did not allow herself to forget that by giving him the illusion of absolute carnal mastery, she had ensured he would never allow Kessler to question her again in Paris, while the contrast with the cold discipline that allowed her to separate the nerves of her skin from the fortress of her mind made the present hour all the starker. Her senses caught the rhythmic shudder of the wooden panels under the force of his body at the same moment she breathed in salt, crushed silk, Courvoisier, and the musky heat of raw sex filtering through the heavy draperies without cease.

"Tell me whose you are," he rasped against her breast, his teeth grazing the pale curve of her flesh hard enough to make her gasp, his fingers tangling in the lace at her thighs and ripping it away in a single fistful.

"Yours," Marion breathed, her hands clamping into the silver-streaked hair at the back of his skull, pulling his mouth back up to hers. "Only yours, Viktor—damn you—"

Even with her chest pinned to the velvet, her mind was already three hundred miles south on the Rhône. She let her attention brush past the heavy weight of his palm between her shoulder blades, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the harsh, ragged rhythm of his breath against the nape of her neck while the room held the aroma of velvet dust, sweat, and the metallic taste of blood on her lower lip steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. Not three paces from the crimson velvet banquette beside the cold fireplace, her diamond bracelet tangled in the dark fringe of the upholstery lay in the amber half-light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. In the choreography of the room, pressing her cheek into the cool velvet cushion while her fingers gripped the carved armrest flowed seamlessly into the shuddering, gasping release that sealed his certainty of her devotion, knowing that the more fiercely he claimed her body, the less he guarded his keys. There was no escaping the conclusion that every bruise she wore tomorrow would be seen by her maid and reported to the house porter as proof of a lovers' quarrel, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the scarred oak trees of Konstancin that stood upright after every winter gale.

He lifted her by the thighs and drove into her with a savage, upward thrust that knocked the breath from her lungs in a hot rush. The shock of it—raw, unsparing, almost violent in its urgency—sent a dark jolt down her spine. She locked her ankles behind his lower back, her fingernails scoring deep red furrows down the muscles of his neck as he hammered her against the shuddering wooden shutters. Every thrust was an act of possession, a fifty-three-year-old soldier trying to outrun death, Berlin, and the SS by burying himself to the hilt inside a twenty-five-year-old woman’s body.

Krystyna met the onslaught without flinching, letting the sheer physical intensity of the clash burn away the terror of Kessler’s gin-pale eyes. She bit Viktor’s lower lip until she tasted salt, tightening her inner muscles around him until his control snapped completely; he wrenched her away from the wall, threw her face-down across the velvet banquette beside the hearth, and took her from behind with one hand splayed heavy and immovable between her shoulder blades, pinning her chest to the crushed crimson pile until they both shuddered over the edge in a tangle of torn black silk and ragged, hoarse cries.

Framed by the geometry of the dark bedroom doorway after the storm had passed, her black silk robe wrapped tight around her aching ribs seemed almost innocuous, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. A subtle trace of cold night air, cooling sweat, and lavender water from the washbasin hung in the draft alongside the slow, heavy snore of the sleeping Colonel in the next room that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She touched her swollen lower lip with one fingertip and watched the street until the rain washed the tire tracks away. Nothing about the blue-slitted tail-lamp of the SD car receding down the wet street escaped her scrutiny, for survival in this house depended on reading what others overlooked, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Nothing moved in her expression beyond standing motionless behind the edge of the blackout curtain so no silhouette showed against the glass and the stone-cold watchfulness of an operative checking the perimeter, using the raw, bruising friction between them to anchor her power over him. Not a single second could be wasted when Kessler had lingered outside to see when the lights went out in Viktor's apartment.

An hour later, when he lay sprawled asleep across the bedroom sheets with her red nail-marks scored across his shoulders, Krystyna stood by the darkened window with a silk robe pulled tight around her bruised ribs. Down on the Rue de Rivoli, a solitary black Mercedes with SD plates idled under the arcade for two minutes before pulling away into the fog. She watched its blue tail-lamp vanish toward the Place de la Concorde, her face as cold and still as marble.

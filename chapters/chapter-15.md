# Chapter 15 — The Stairwell Behind the Casino

*Warsaw — February 1944*

---

On the morning of February first, at nine minutes past nine, the Warsaw Underground struck the Reich a blow that echoed all the way to Berlin. Less than two hundred yards from the Gestapo headquarters on Aleja Szucha, along the snow-swept lindens of Aleje Ujazdowskie, twelve young combat operatives of the Home Army’s *Pegaz* unit blocked the black limousine of SS-Brigadeführer Franz Kutschera—the SS and Police Leader of Warsaw—and riddled him with submachine-gun fire in ninety seconds flat.

As the courier lines carried her microfilms across the borders toward London, every whisper overheard in the corridor became a warning flashed to the field. Through the narrow archways of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. The lingering resonance in the air after the latch clicked shut reinforced the discipline she had practiced for four long years. Her survival among the wolves of the Reich rested on the truth that the smallest domestic gesture could conceal a decisive intelligence strike. Without haste, she kept her hand steady on the helm of her dangerous masquerade.

She watched the coffin pass and felt a fierce, terrible pride braided with the horror of the reprisals. In the presence of the black-bordered swastika armband on Kessler's sleeve as he marched behind the caisson below, the contrast between conquered street and gilded drawing room grew sharper still, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the slow, hollow thud of forty muffled SS funeral drums echoing off the Saxon colonnade while the room held the aroma of smoking pitch torches, wet black crepe, snow, and the heavy wool of the assembled officers as though the street itself had leaked through the masonry. By the threshold of the tall second-floor window of the Brühl Palace overlooking Plac Piłsudskiego, the black-draped artillery caisson carrying Brigadeführer Kutschera's coffin through the snow stood out in sharp relief, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. As she completed standing motionless beside Viktor with her black-gloved hands folded over her astrakhan muff, she drew the solemn, respectful gravity required at a German state funeral around herself like silk, leaving no seam into which suspicion could wedge a blade. She knew beyond doubt that the execution of Kutschera on Aleje Ujazdowskie had proved that not even the highest hangman in Warsaw was safe from the Home Army, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the three hundred hostages shot in reprisal the day after the ambush, whose blood still stained the snow on Ujazdowskie.

Along the riverward slope of the Saxon Garden, white signs forbade Polish feet to tread the gravel paths of their own capital. The soft rustle of heavy drapery stirred by the draft followed her thoughts like a shadow along the wall. Through all the perils of the autumn and winter, she knew that nothing taken from her family was lost while she stood inside their walls. Between the whispered drop site and the locked Generalstab safe, each document lifted from the high command shifted the odds of the coming battle. As the minute hand crept on, she turned her attention back to the immediate task before her.

By noon, Warsaw was an armed camp under siege. Three hundred Polish civilians were dragged to the ruins of the Ujazdowskie walls and shot before sundown; the curfew was moved back to seven in the evening; and on the fourth of February, the Germans staged a massive, torchlit military funeral cortege down Krakowskie Przedmieście and through Plac Piłsudskiego, with muffled black drums beating a slow march and black swastika crepe draped from every balcony.

A subtle vibration of heavy wheels passing along the avenue reminded her that in occupied Europe no sanctuary was ever permanent. Along the damp paving stones of Ulica Chmielna, ordinary shopfronts concealed the tireless arithmetic of the underground army. Inside the nerve center of the command while the Resistance gathered below, every checkpoint passed in silence was another victory in the war of nerves. Months of walking the razor's edge had taught her that the hunter who believes he owns the quarry never looks at her hands. Smoothing the silk at her wrist, she kept her mask as smooth and cool as Meissen porcelain.

In the second-floor windows of the Brühl Palace overlooking the square, Viktor stood with the senior staff watching Kutschera’s black-draped gun carriage roll past between double ranks of steel-helmeted Waffen-SS.

In the cold calculus of deep-cover work, it remained true that the secrets of an empire always leaked fastest through its pride. The slow drip of moisture from the stone cornice overhead grounded her awareness in the unforgiving geometry of the hour. Down the long colonnades of Plac Trzech Krzyży, the ruined church dome watched the patrols march past in the snow. Where every stolen timetable and troop manifest tipped the unseen scales, she read the subtle shifts in the staff room before the generals themselves. Anchored by her silent oath, she remained fixed on the distant horizon where the storm was gathering.

In the hush that filled the overheated buffet hall behind the Brühl Palace viewing gallery, a silver urn of steaming bouillon and trays of funeral brandy appeared almost motionless, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. A subtle trace of hot beef bouillon, wet astrakhan fur, and stale cigar smoke hung in the draft alongside the low, angry murmur of SS and Wehrmacht colonels arguing over street security sharpening her focus as she prepared her next reply. She slipped through the heavy baize service door into the unlighted stone stairwell behind the officers' casino. To anyone else, the portrait of Heinrich Himmler hanging in the corridor outside the casino might have seemed unremarkable, but to an operative it was a coordinate on a map, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. Combining a pale, composed inclination of her head as she stepped out of the hot crowd with the quiet motion of setting her untouched cup of bouillon down on a side table and excusing herself toward the back powder room, she gave the room only what it expected to see, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. She steadied her pulse, knowing full well that she needed two minutes alone in the cold back stairwell before the tremor in her stomach betrayed her.

Where a two-second pause at a coatrack could mean life or the gallows, she harvested the secrets of the staff while pouring their vintage champagne. Along the quiet curbstones of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. A hushed stillness that settled between one spoken word and the next recalled every other night she had stood on guard behind enemy lines. Long nights of training in London and Paris had shown her that a spy's greatest armor is the vanity of the men who watch her. Resting her hand lightly on the table, she trusted the quiet strength that had carried her from Paris to the Vistula.

"Your friend Kessler looks as if he has swallowed vinegar," Viktor muttered to Marion, who stood beside him in black wool and astrakhan fur. "Kutschera was shot within pistol range of Kessler’s own window. Now Berlin wants heads on pikes."

Down on the square, the muffled funeral drums beat a slow, hollow rhythm—*boom, boom, boom*—that vibrated through the soles of Krystyna’s shoes. Every beat of the drum brought back the sight of eighteen-year-old Marek’s one swollen eye looking up at her beside the radiator at Aleja Szucha before he bit the glass cyanide bead between his teeth.

* * *

In the narrow stone service stairwell behind the second-floor casino, the air was freezing and dark, lit only by a slit of gray winter sky through a frosted ventilator high in the wall. Krystyna leaned her forehead against the cold limewashed stone, stripping off her black kid gloves and pressing her bare palms flat against the wall while a dry, silent shudder racked her shoulders.

Every fierce, muffled stroke in the dark drove the cold ghost of Aleja Szucha out of her bones. Past the edge of the dark linen-storage alcove beneath the Brühl Palace service stair, stacks of folded white damask tablecloths cushioning her shoulders against the wall waited in the gloom, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She was acutely aware of the distant, rhythmic boom of Kutschera's funeral drums vibrating through the stone floor and the faint drift of freshly laundered linen, salt tears, cedar soap, and the sudden scorching heat of their bodies until both became part of the rhythm of her breathing. She did not allow herself to forget that only Phillip understood the moral abyss of what they did every day, because his hands were stained with the same impossible choices, though every detail of the two of them comforting each other in the dark after her brother's funeral in 1936 remained as sharp as cut glass. Without haste, clinging to his neck with her legs locked around his waist in the narrow closet reinforced the quiet authority of the raw, life-affirming hunger of two people defying the house of death around them, letting her fingers tremble for the only man who knew her true name and held it sacred. In the cold arithmetic of the underground, twenty feet away on the other side of the baize door, fifty senior German officers were drinking brandy to a dead SS general, whatever price it demanded.

Across the hushed thoroughfares of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. The measured cadence of patrol boots on the stone outside left no doubt that the hunters were still circling in the dark. Even when her ribs ached with fatigue, she remembered that to smile in the candlelight was ten times more dangerous than to frown. In the reverent, aching tenderness of Phillip's embrace, there was no mask left between them—only a devotion that had outlived the ashes. In the hush that followed, she measured the distance to the door and found her balance.

*He was eighteen,* her mind repeated over the distant thud of the funeral drums outside. *Eighteen years old, and I put the poison in his hand.*

A muffled chime from the church tower three streets away marked another fraction of an hour won from the enemy. In the long shadow cast by Aleja Szucha, the police district stood ringed with machine-gun posts and the dread of the cellars. Where the boy who had held her youth now stood beside her as a man, a single touch of his mouth undid the hardened spy and woke the woman beneath. She governed every muscle of her face by the rule that each successful drop tightened the invisible net around the garrison. Betraying not a flicker of doubt, she let her gaze settle calmly upon the threshold ahead.

The baize service door at the foot of the landing clicked open and shut without a sound. Before she could turn and pull her mask back over her face, two warm, bare hands closed gently over her shoulders, and Phillip drew her back against his chest.

Her senses caught the ragged, synchronized gasps they breathed directly into each other's mouths so no sound escaped at the same moment she breathed in warm sweat, wool, and the clean starch of the palace linens filtering through the heavy draperies without cease. "I love you, Krysiu," he breathed into her mouth. "Never carry the dark alone while I am breathing." Through the lens of her cover identity, Phillip's warm forehead resting against hers for thirty seconds while their pulses slowed was merely part of the scenery, yet to Krystyna it spoke volumes, confirming what the underground had suspected for weeks. She trusted burying her teeth in the thick wool of his shoulder pad as the wave broke through her knees and the familiar armor of a silent, convulsive shudder that left them both trembling in each other's arms to see her through, until the mask itself began to dissolve beneath the quiet reverence of his gaze. There was no escaping the conclusion that this urgent, stolen passion in the belly of the Brühl Palace was the only sanity left in Warsaw, while the contrast with the quiet attic on Ulica Freta on Christmas Eve where they had had hours instead of minutes made the present hour all the starker. She could not afford a single misstep when if anyone tried the latch of the linen closet before she smoothed her skirt, they would die together on the stairs.

In the silent discipline of her double life, she knew that composure under scrutiny was the one fortress no key could unlock. The steady whisper of rain against the outer shutter underscored how thin the wall was between the drawing room and the street. Beneath the shadowed trees of the northern rubble wall, the snow could not cover the ash of the quarter that had been burned to the earth. Beneath the quiet worship of Phillip's mouth and dark eyes, they held each other as though each stolen hour had been won from death itself. Lifting her chin by a fraction, she carried her secret onward into the heart of the night.

"Don't hide from me, Krysiu," Phillip whispered in Polish against her hair, wrapping his arms around her shivering ribs and pulling her into the dark Linen-storage alcove beneath the upper stair flight, locking the wooden latch behind them. "I watched you at the window when the drums started. You have been carrying that boy from Aleja Szucha alone for fourteen days."

In those clandestine meetings where no uniform stood between them, the contrast between the Colonel's storm and Phillip's reverence cut her to the bone. Under the hooded blue lamps of the Belweder precinct, the winter fog muffled the sleigh bells and the jackboots alike. A dry whisper of wind moving through the branches outside seemed to belong solely to this dark season of the war. Behind the calm gray-green of her eyes lay the recognition that loyalty to the dead was best paid by keeping the living out of the cellars. Measuring the room one last time, she walked the tightrope without looking down into the dark.

"He looked at me as if I were an angel, Phillip," she whispered, turning in his arms in the narrow, pitch-dark closet and burying her face against the open collar of his throat while hot, silent tears finally broke against his skin. "I gave him a glass bead of acid and told him to be free—and then I drank Malaga wine upstairs while he died on the cellar tiles."

Phillip’s hands framed her wet cheeks in the dark, his mouth coming down over hers with a fierce, aching tenderness that swallowed her sob whole. "You saved him from six days on Kessler’s hooks, and you saved Wanda and forty others," he breathed against her lips, kissing her tear-salted eyelids, her cheeks, her trembling mouth. "Look at me. Feel me. You are alive, Krysiu. Hold onto me."

In the pitch-black linen alcove, with the muffled SS funeral drums still booming two hundred yards away across the square, Krystyna clung to him as if he were the only solid ground in a drowning world. Her fingers tore at the silver buttons of his tunic until her bare palms found the hot skin of his chest over his heart; Phillip lifted her against the stacked linen shelves, parting her knees through the black wool skirt as his mouth claimed hers in a desperate, breathless frenzy of life against death. There was no time even to undress—only the urgent, scorching slide of his hands beneath her garters, the rasp of uniform cloth against silk, and the sudden, blinding heat as he entered her standing in the dark, his left hand braced behind her head to cushion her skull from the wall while she bit deep into the wool of his shoulder to silence the cry that tore through her body.

* * *

Before she slipped out of the alcove, Phillip pressed a folded strip of cigarette paper into the palm of her black kid glove.

A faint ticking from the clock mechanism across the room gave the quiet chamber the tension of a drawn bowstring. Beneath the looming cornice of Ulica Foksal, requisitioned palaces burned high coal fires while the conquered city froze outside. Within the tightening noose of surveillance patrols and street cordons, one mistimed glance or trembling finger would bring the entire network down. In the private ledger of her conscience, she held it certain that every stone of Warsaw remained an ally that remembered her true name. Drawing a slow breath, she sealed the thought away inside the vault of her discipline.

Her training insisted that she had a four-month window before the Strasbourg and Lausanne archives exposed the death of the real Marion Herschlag, and she set that truth beside the indelible recollection of the genuine 1937 Lausanne hospital ledger where the real Marion Herschlag's death had been recorded. Not three paces from the cold stone landing outside the service alcove, the strip of cigarette paper tucked inside the palm of her black kid glove lay in the amber half-light, as the shadows lengthened across the polished floor. She walked back into the crowded buffet hall and let Viktor hand her a glass of cognac. She let her attention brush past the small brass mirror in her compact showing her face pale, immaculate, and calm, cataloging it alongside every other clue in the room, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She listened to the distant bark of SS commands as the funeral cortege cleared Plac Piłsudskiego while the room held the aroma of cold limewash, French powder, and the steady calm returning to her veins steadying her nerves when they threatened to fray. Before the night was over, between now and June, she and Phillip had to strip Viktor's safe of every defensive plan for Festung Warschau, and she intended to be ready.

In a house where every mirror had eyes, she understood that the quiet pauses over wine were deadlier than the cordons in the street. The intermittent hiss of steam in the iron radiator pipes remained her only companion in the silence behind her mask. Near the iron-grated cellars of the Old Town rooftops, a hundred hidden printing presses and cellar radios kept the republic alive in the dark. Along the razor-thin wire separating seduction from exposure, she turned the enemy's own headquarters into an arsenal for the underground. In the amber half-light, she prepared her next word with the care of a jeweler cutting diamond.

"Kessler has requested the Strasbourg municipal birth registry and the Lausanne police files through the Reichssicherheitshauptamt in Berlin," Phillip whispered, his fingers buttoning his tunic in the dark with rapid precision. "Because the French railway strikes have delayed mail from Alsace, he sent a written inquiry rather than a wire. It will take twelve weeks to come back through Berlin channels—which gives us until May or June before the Strasbourg archive answers."

Through the labyrinth of checkpoints, cipher tables, and midnight drops, the black-coated hunters were always only half a step behind the shadow. Across the darkened perspective of the Hotel Europejski, field-gray greatcoats brushed past linen baskets that carried the fate of regiments. A sharp crackle of resinous wood shifting on the hearth measured out the narrow interval before the next watch changed. Beneath the flawless poise of her assumed name, she remembered that every day endured in field-gray company brought the hour of rising nearer. With her eyes clear and level, she waited for the signal that would open the next act of the play.

Krystyna’s heart steadied back into its cold, operational cadence as she smoothed her black skirt and repinned a loose strand of gold hair by touch. "Until May or June," she repeated softly. "By June the Russians will be on the Bug, and the Home Army will be ready for *Burza*."

Past the iron railings of Ulica Świętokrzyska, hidden couriers slipped between the bookshops before the siren sounded. The regular rise and fall of breathing in the half-lit space brought the reality of the garrison straight into the room. Nothing in her voice or posture betrayed the knowledge that even in the lion's mouth, a clear head could find the seam in the armor. In the shadow war waged with wax impressions and subminiature lenses, every photograph developed in the dark cellar bought allied blood with German ink. With unhurried grace, she let the moment pass without leaving a trace for watchful eyes.

"And when the answer comes from Strasbourg," Phillip said, his dark eyes glinting in the thin crack of light by the door, "every teleprinter and courier pouch entering the Brühl Palace passes through my registry clerk before it reaches Kessler’s desk. I will intercept it myself."

She touched his cheek once with her gloved fingertips, slipped out the baize door into the upper gallery, and returned to Viktor’s side just as the funeral drums outside fell silent.

* * *

Three days after Kutschera’s funeral, during the noon church hour when German patrols rarely entered the nave, Krystyna descended into the whitewashed subterranean crypt of the Church of the Holy Cross on Krakowskie Przedmieście. Among the stone sarcophagi of Warsaw’s poets and bishops, Tadeusz Kowalski stood in a workman’s padded coat, holding a mason’s trowel beside the pillar upstairs that held Frédéric Chopin’s heart.

Looking out from behind her borrowed identity, she saw clearly that survival depended on storing maps behind her eyelids without looking twice. The distant whistle of a freight locomotive beyond the river blended with the familiar scent of wax, wool, and winter stone. Within the high-walled gardens of Krakowskie Przedmieście, every passerby walked with eyes lowered against the sudden whistle of a roundup. Across the chessboard of safe houses, dead-drops, and candlelit receptions, every trace of wax, film, and sympathetic ink had to vanish without a seam. With practiced serenity, she met the gaze of the room with tranquil, unreadable poise.

Nothing moved in her expression beyond kneeling motionless at the wooden rail with her gloved hands folded over her prayer book and devout, solitary mourning in a church basement, confirming that her nerve held firmer than the men who hunted her. A subtle trace of damp limestone, extinguished tallow candles, and winter wool hung in the draft alongside the muffled boom of the church organ playing above their heads in the main nave that no amount of French perfume or beeswax could disguise. She whispered the drop coordinates twice until they were locked in her memory and rose from the kneeler. Framed by the geometry of the vaulted subterranean crypt beneath the Church of the Holy Cross, the stone sarcophagi and Tadeusz's mason's trowel resting on a brick ledge seemed almost innocuous, beneath the heavy stillness that followed the last spoken word. She forced herself to accept that thirty-eight Allied air drops from Italy meant the Home Army would have British anti-tank weapons to fight German armor in the streets, though the thought of visiting the crypt with her school class on All Souls' Day when hundreds of red glass lamps burned on the tombs returned with a sudden, involuntary ache. Not a single second could be wasted when with Kessler's watcher posted in the Kopernika back alley, she had to seal the Foksal garden exit until the spring rains.

In the silent underground relay linking the operatives in the dark, not a single link in the chain could falter while the Resistance prepared to strike. Across the curfew-bound sweep of Aleje Jerozolimskie, divided red streetcars rattled through the slush between two worlds at war. A quiet creak of seasoned timber settling in the floor pressed against the tall windows with quiet insistence. Her father's memory and her own oath reminded her that her body and her nerve were instruments tuned to a single sovereign purpose. Steeling her resolve once more, she held her ground amid the shifting currents of the room.

"London has confirmed the spring drop schedules, Jaskółka," Tadeusz whispered in Polish as Krystyna knelt at the prayer rail three feet away. "Wait until you hear the numbers: thirty-eight Allied Liberator flights from Brindisi between March and May, bringing PIAT anti-tank launchers, plastic explosive, and gold dollars for the rising."

Within the mirror-lined rooms of Ulica Freta, behind the seventeenth-century gables the candles of Christmas Eve burned in secret. The muted murmur of voices filtering through the closed doors served as a quiet metronome for the calculation in her mind. Every instinct honed by the Underground insisted that memory had to be used as a whetstone rather than a weight. Beneath the crystal chandeliers where generals spoke too freely over brandy, she kept her eyes clear and her fingers steady on the lock until the bolt turned. Letting the silence work for her, she chose once more the cold courage that had brought her through the fire.

"And Kessler?" she asked softly, her eyes fixed on the stone tablet before her.

A faint chime of crystal vibrating on the sideboard tray confirmed how little margin remained for a careless word. Behind the double-bolted doors of Nowy Świat, the street megaphones hung like iron crows above the silent bread queues. Between the polite applause of the salon and the click of the safe dial, she moved between the salon and the cellar without dropping a single stitch. Experience inside the high command had proved to her that the darkest nights before dawn were always the ones that decided the war. Guarding the fire behind her ribs, she readied herself for the next turn of the glass.

"Kessler has put two full-time watchers on the Brühl Palace registry and one on the Foksal postern alley," Tadeusz warned. "Do not use the garden stair at Foksal 14 again until April. Pass everything through Sarna at the Europejski or through Phillip at the palace."

* * *

That evening at Foksal 14, Viktor spread a three-foot blueprint across the dining table after the maid had cleared the soup: the preliminary Pioneer Corps mining diagram for the Kierbedź and Poniatowski bridges across the Vistula.

Where microfilms hidden in tortoiseshell and soap crossed the checkpoints, she balanced deception against survival with the cold precision of a watchmaker. Past the shuttered shopfronts of the Vistula embankment, the wind from the eastern plains drove needles of frost against the ruins of thirty-nine. A low hum from the electrical wiring behind the plaster turned an ordinary pause into a test of nerve. Every lesson Stefan Wilk and Tadeusz had imparted confirmed that the war in the dark was won five seconds at a time by the steadier hand. Keeping her pulse steady, she continued the silent watch that would end only when the city was free.

Where the draft stirred the mahogany dining table of Foksal 14 spread with blue bridge blueprints, four silver salt cellars holding down the curled corners of the Poniatowski Bridge mining chart remained fixed in place, as if time itself had slowed inside the shuttered walls. She poured him a glass of port and went to the piano while her mind redrew the bridge pier by pier. Against the quiet of the room, the red lightning-bolt symbol marking the main Amatol chamber in Pier Three stood as a reminder of what waited beyond the guarded gates, where every courtesy concealed a blade. She reminded herself, calmly and without illusion, that knowing that the trunk firing cables ran beneath the pedestrian walkway at Piers Three and Five would allow Home Army sappers to cut the charges before the bridge blew, though every detail of walking across the Poniatowski Bridge in the spring sun with the Vistula sparkling wide and silver below remained as sharp as cut glass. With helpful, domestic disinterest as she smoothed the collar of his house jacket governing the motion of laying Viktor's spectacles beside his right hand while her gaze swept the red cable lines in three seconds to conceal the rapid calculation behind her eyes, keeping the invisible wall between Marion Herschlag and Krystyna Stankiewiczówna intact. She was acutely aware of the crackle of the porcelain stove and the dry rustle of stiff blueprint paper and the faint drift of blueprint ammonia, cigar smoke, and hot beef broth that clung to the wool, the stone, and the damp pavement alike.

Beyond the shuttered windows of the Konstancin road, the ancient linden trees stood sheathed in ice like sentries waiting for the spring. The hollow echo of an iron gate latching in the courtyard marked the slow passage of the rationed night. Against the paranoia of the men she served, she knew that a lie told with enough fire becomes indistinguishable from the truth. Where a single altered word or duplicate key decided forty lives, she played her role under the chandeliers knowing thousands rode on her lie. While the night deepened outside, she stepped forward to meet whatever the next hour would demand.

"If the Russians reach Praga in the summer," Viktor said, weighting the corners of the blueprint with silver salt cellars, "every pier of the four Warsaw bridges will be packed with two tons of Amatol wired to a master dynamo in the Citadel. Bring me my reading spectacles from the library, Marion."

A metallic clink of a sentry's buckle from the pavement below told her that the city outside was still holding its breath. From the sentry-guarded gates of the Kierbedź Bridge, eastern troop trains rumbled through the frost toward a front that never stopped bleeding. Across the secret map of Europe drawn in invisible ink and silk thread, the fate of an entire sector rested inside the lining of a handbag or a glove. She held fast to the hard-won certainty that an operative who controlled her own pulse controlled the room around her. In that narrow pause between beats, she kept faith with the living and the dead who depended on her nerve.

As she weighed each detail of the hour, she understood that no uniform on earth could command the mind that refused to yield. The distant rumble of lorries crossing the iron viaduct threaded through the silence like a wire stretched taut. Through the drifting night mist of Plac Piłsudskiego, the Brühl Palace blazed behind its curtains at the heart of the German quarter. Under the constant threat of the direction-finding vans prowling the streets, the slightest smudge on a brass latch would have signed her death warrant. As the shadows lengthened, she held the line between the woman they saw and the soldier she was.

As she brought him the tortoiseshell spectacles and leaned over his shoulder to lay them beside the blueprint, her eyes photographed the exact pier numbers—Pier Three and Pier Five on the Poniatowski viaduct—where the trunk firing cables ran inside lead conduits beneath the pedestrian walkway.
